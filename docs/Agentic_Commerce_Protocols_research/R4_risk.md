# R4 Risk brief: research, security, fraud, liability and regulation

Strand R4 of the agentic commerce protocols study. As of 2026-11-15. Follows R0_schema.md. Baseline rows from R0_R4_risk.md are reused by id (for example C296) and were rechecked only where this brief leans on them. Every page cited here was opened in this run unless the row says "baseline".

Method note. 200 web searches were allowed per turn across all agents and the cap was reached part way through this run. After that point only known URLs were opened directly. Areas not covered because of the cap are listed in section 8.

## 1. Summary

- A signed mandate is not proof of intent. A systematic AP2 v0.2 threat model found 48 threats, 8 of them high risk, and showed that valid mandate signatures do not ensure the transaction reflects what the user wanted when the context before signing is manipulated (R4-17). The AP2 spec itself offers mandates as dispute evidence but puts dispute resolution out of scope (R4-61, R4-62).
- x402 has design and code flaws, not only theory risk. Two 2026 papers show unpaid service, paid but denied outcomes and resource leakage up to 100 percent against official SDKs (R4-14, R4-15). A V12 review summarised by the Cloud Security Alliance found the default client had no spending cap before August 2026 fixes, and the fixes shipped with no security advisory (R4-31, R4-32).
- AI shoppers still choose badly and can be steered. Visa's own benchmark (2026-11-05) found models failed 42.8 percent of runs and 54 percent of failed runs still ended in a purchase (R4-27, R4-28). Lab studies show position bias, first proposal bias and a near tripling of sponsored picks under LLM persuasion (R4-01, R4-02, R4-04).
- Prompt injection reaches checkout. Red teaming of an AP2 shopping agent, a cross platform benchmark with 33 structural flaws and a vendor demo where an agentic browser paid a fake store all point the same way (R4-11, R4-16, R4-09).
- No confirmed case of a hijacked consumer shopping agent causing a real payment loss was found. The largest documented case is the reverse: attackers used open source agent frameworks to breach at least 27 retailers and take over 600,000 card records (R4-30).
- Agent traffic is growing fast but measured only by vendors. LexisNexis saw 450 percent growth in 2025, HUMAN 7,851 percent, DataDome 82.3 percent for all AI traffic. These figures do not agree and none is audited (R4-21 to R4-25).
- US law leaves the consumer exposed by default. Reg E excludes transfers by a person the consumer gave the access device to, and Reg Z turns on actual, implied or apparent authority. Neither has been applied to an AI agent. The CFPB has said nothing on agents as far as found (R4-34 to R4-41).
- Card networks let agents pay under existing rules with no liability shift and no agent reason code found. Amex is the only network found that covers agent errors by policy, and only for registered agents with authenticated intent (R4-64, R4-65, baseline C372).
- The UK is the most active regulator: HM Treasury consultation Question 15, an FCA plan for a trusted framework for agentic finance and CMA guidance that a business is responsible for its own agents (R4-53, R4-55, R4-56). The EU PSR is still not published in the Official Journal as far as found. MAS guidelines take effect 2027-10-07 with agentic guidance to be consulted on in 2027. India has a two factor rule and an unlaunched agent protocol (R4-47, R4-60, R4-57, R4-59).
- In the window 2026-10-22 to 2026-11-15 the main moves were industry, not law: the x402 SDK disclosure, the Visa benchmark and survey, and Cloudflare's Trusted Agent Delegation coalition. CFPB, FCA and EBA newsrooms show no agent item in the window (section 6).
