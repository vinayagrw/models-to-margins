# Cross-Border Shipping — Source Ledger

**Scope:** evidence base for two deep-dive pages on US/Mexico/Canada cross-border freight, structured around the five challenges named in the Redwood Logistics article *"Logistics Challenges in Cross-Border Shipping"* (https://www.redwoodlogistics.com/insights/logistics-challenges-cross-border-shipping).

**Compiled:** 21 August 2026. All "as retrieved" timestamps are that date unless stated.

---

## How to read this ledger

### A note on the source article

The Redwood article was fetched and read in full. **It contains no numbers at all** — no percentages, no dollar figures, no dates. It is a qualitative framing piece. Every quantified claim below was sourced independently. Do not cite Redwood for any figure; cite it only for the five-challenge taxonomy.

### Grades

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed** | A primary source states it, the page was actually loaded, and the number is exact as recorded. |
| **Vendor claim** | A company asserts it about its own product or market. Recorded, never Confirmed. The company is named in the claim itself. |
| **Proposed** | A reasonable inference or a figure I computed from primary data. Not directly stated by any source. The computation is described. |
| **Open** | Contested between sources, or could not be verified against a loaded page. |
| **Illustrative** | A plausible worked number for a figure, with no source at all. |

### Source preference, highest first

1. US government — CBP, BTS, USITC, GAO, Treasury OIG, Federal Register, Federal Reserve
2. International bodies — WCO, World Bank, OECD
3. Named industry research with a stated method and sample
4. Vendor claims

### Verification method

Every claim below was checked against a page or dataset that actually loaded. Several US government domains (`cbp.gov`, `bts.gov`, `supremecourt.gov`, `gao.gov` PDFs, `congress.gov`, `federalregister.gov` HTML) return HTTP 403 or bot-challenge pages to the standard fetch tool. Where that happened, the page was retrieved with `curl` using a browser user-agent, or via the site's JSON/XML API, and parsed locally. The "Verified how" column says which route was used. **Anything that could not be loaded is graded `Open`, not `Confirmed`.**

### Three standing caveats

1. **FY2026 CBP figures are a partial year.** CBP's fiscal year runs 1 Oct 2025 – 30 Sep 2026, and the trade statistics page states FY2026 and FY2025 data are "updated as of July 27, 2026". Any FY2026 figure covers roughly ten months, not twelve. Never compare a FY2026 number to a full FY2025 number without saying so.
2. **The US tariff regime changed four times in six months.** IEEPA tariffs struck down 20 Feb 2026 → Section 122 surcharge 24 Feb – 24 Jul 2026 → Section 301 action from 24 Jul 2026, with a Court of International Trade ruling against Section 122 in May 2026 layered on top. Any tariff *rate* is a rate as of a stated date. Rates without dates are useless here.
3. **De minimis is gone.** This is the single biggest structural change in the data and it distorts every year-on-year entry-count comparison. See CB-4-08.

## The strongest five claims

These would survive a hostile read — a specialist reader trying to break them.

1. **CB-5-02 — the two-business-day reporting lag in 19 CFR 18.1(h) and (j).** It is regulatory text, quoted verbatim, from the eCFR versioner API with a dated snapshot. It cannot be out of date, cannot be a vendor's framing, and it reframes the whole visibility argument: the gap is legally permitted, not a technology failure. There is nothing to attack.

2. **CB-4-03 — the Supreme Court holding.** Quoted verbatim from the slip opinion PDF, not from any secondary account, with the vote derived by reading the lineup paragraph. It is paired with CB-4-04, which pre-empts the most common error in coverage of this case by establishing that the opinion contains no refund figure at all. A hostile reader's likely attack — "you got the 6-3 from a news report" — is already answered.

3. **CB-1-01 — 19,949 ten-digit HTS statistical lines.** Counted directly out of USITC's own machine-readable export rather than repeated from anyone, and the count independently reproduces the figure USITC prints in its HTS preface. Two independent routes to the same number.

4. **CB-2-01 / CB-2-02 — 12,856,744 inbound truck crossings, 22.9% of them at Laredo.** Aggregated from BTS port-level primary data via the Socrata API, and computed twice by two researchers working separately with results identical to the unit. The honest caveat that it is my aggregation rather than a BTS headline is stated, which is itself what makes it defensible.

5. **CB-2-05 — GAO on CBP's undefined "high risk".** A named GAO report with a report number, quoting CBP's own inspection parameter back at it. It is the rare claim that explains *why* other data does not exist: CBP cannot publish an exam rate because the triggering category is undefined. That makes it both a claim and a shield for the gaps elsewhere in the ledger.

## The weakest five

These look quotable and would not survive scrutiny. Listed with what is actually wrong.

1. **CB-3-07 — the "44 percent reduction in truck processing wait times" from ACE.** Superficially perfect: a government source, a clean percentage, on exactly the right topic. In fact it is from a February 2018 CBP press release marketing CBP's own programme, with no baseline, no measurement window and no audit. Three current CBP ACE pages restate neither this nor the 269-forms/47-PGAs figures. Eight years stale and self-assessed. Graded Open for that reason. **The temptation to use it will be strong. Do not.**

2. **CB-2-06 — "$1.5 billion in annual border delay costs".** The number is real and the method is stated, but the qualifier does the damage: it is **passenger and commercial vehicles combined**, and TTI's own page says passenger vehicles are a large share by volume. Quoted as a freight cost it becomes a falsehood, and TTI publishes no passenger/commercial split to repair it with. It also runs on 2024 data.

3. **CB-4-05 — IEEPA collections and refunds.** Six different figures circulate for two quantities: $166bn / $175bn / $180bn / "over $200bn" collected, and $81bn / $86bn / ~$100bn refunded. Only the $166bn collections figure has two independent loaded sources. The refund total is still moving as CBP processes claims, so any single number is stale on publication. It reads authoritative and is not.

4. **CB-5-10 — Tive's "37% of companies can't track in-transit cargo".** Exactly the kind of statistic that gets repeated into apparent fact. It is a visibility vendor publishing a number that argues for buying visibility software, in a press release that states no sample size, no fielding period and no independent surveyor. The page was checked specifically for methodology and has none.

5. **CB-1-11 — "5 fraudulent documents a day".** Sits on a genuine CBP page next to solid figures, and reads as a customs-paperwork fraud statistic. But CBP never defines the term on that page, and in CBP's typical-day framing it almost always means traveller identity documents, not commercial invoices. Used in a documentation-errors section it would be actively misleading. Graded Open for that reason alone.

**Honourable mention:** CB-3-05, the World Bank LPI scores. The values loaded cleanly from the World Bank API, but the API dates them 2022 while the World Bank publishes them as the LPI 2023 edition, and a 2023-dated query returns null. Either way the data predates 2024. The scores are usable; the date is not defensible without the caveat.

## What I could not find

Real gaps, not padding. Each of these was actively searched for and is genuinely absent.

**No US, Mexican or Canadian WCO Time Release Study.** The WCO TRS Guide Version 4 (2025) exists, and 2025 TRS results were published by Sri Lanka Customs (average import release 76:43 hours, sample of 6,787 declarations) and Mumbai JNCH (72.86 hours). Neither CBP, nor Mexico's SAT/ANAM, nor CBSA publishes a TRS. There is therefore **no comparable clearance-time benchmark for the corridor this page is about** — the obvious international apples-to-apples number does not exist for North America.

**No CBP exam or hold rate, at all.** CBP publishes seizure counts, penalty counts and audit counts but no denominator: no percentage of entries examined, held or subjected to a document request. GAO-25-107379 explains why — CBP has not defined "high risk", the term its own 100%-inspection parameter turns on. The unpredictability described in the source article is therefore **unquantifiable from public data**, and any specific exam-rate figure encountered elsewhere should be treated as invented.

**No CBP data on USMCA preference-claim denials.** Nothing published on verification volumes, denial rates, or the share of certificates of origin found deficient. The Dallas Fed can tell us ~80% of imports now claim USMCA; nobody publishes how many of those claims fail on audit. This is the missing half of the certificate-of-origin story.

**No CBP historical border wait times.** The live feed at `bwt.cbp.gov` is excellent and genuinely primary, but CBP does not publish a historical archive. Any claim about average or peak-season commercial wait times over time cannot be sourced to CBP — which is why CB-2-03 had to be built from truck *volumes* rather than *waits*.

**No government in-bond data after FY2020.** Treasury OIG-22-033 (FY2019–FY2020 CBP data) is the most recent government figure that could be loaded. No successor audit exists, so the in-bond volume claims in Challenge 5 are six years old.

**No per-country OECD Trade Facilitation Indicator scores for the US, Mexico and Canada.** The March 2025 OECD report gives only regional aggregates. The underlying per-country scores on "information availability", "involvement of the trade community" and "internal/external border agency co-operation" — the four most relevant sub-indices for Challenge 3 — sit in the OECD TFI Simulator and compareyourcountry.org, both JavaScript applications with no inline data, and the OECD SDMX API carries no TFI dataflow at all. Extracting them would need a headless browser.

**No credible EDI rekeying or data-entry error rate for logistics.** Searched specifically. Everything returned was vendor marketing with no method. The "communication breakdown" challenge has **no quantified error-rate evidence** behind it — the case has to be made structurally (CB-3-01, CB-3-03) rather than statistically.

**No named industry survey with a stated sample size on border-specific visibility loss.** Descartes' 9th annual Transportation Management Benchmark loaded (over 600 global companies) but carries no border or visibility-gap figure. No project44, FourKites or Overhaul primary research with a number could be loaded. Tive is the only named survey found, and it discloses no method.

**No CBP-stated total number of licensed customs brokers.** The widely repeated "~11,000 licensed brokers" appears only in search snippets and vendor blogs. CBP's customs-brokers page loaded and contains no count — only the $185.38 FY2026 permit user fee. The two verified figures, 2,515 permitted firms and 4,830 triennial-report respondents, are **different measures and neither is the licensed-broker count.**

**The frequently cited "in-bond shipments are 30–60% of all US imports"** traces to GAO-07-561, whose PDF returned HTTP 403 and whose landing-page summary explicitly states the report does not give a specific percentage. **Not recorded, and should not be used.**

**No current count of CBP bonded warehouses, Centralized Examination Stations or FIRMS codes.** The only loadable figure is DHS OIG-12-25 (January 2012): 2,054 bonded facilities — 57 CES, 1,490 bonded warehouses, 507 FTZs. Fourteen years stale; excluded from the tables above. The FTZ count in CB-5-09 supersedes the FTZ part of it.
