# Why did US paid hours per worker stop falling after about 1980 while output per hour kept rising, which explanation is winning, and how sure can anyone be about hours in 2050 and 2076?

Researcher note for Q5 of the Time Spent vs Time Saved study. Written 2 November 2026. It adds to what the study already holds in research/02, 01, 13, 17 and 30 and does not repeat those numbers.

Tags. Verified means the primary page or the paper's own text was opened. Plausible means seen only in an abstract, a press summary or a search snippet. Projection means a scenario or forecast, never a fact. Not found means searched and not located, and must never be stated as a fact.

## What is known

Long run context the study did not hold

- Global annual hours per worker fell from about 3,200 in 1800 to about 2,100 in 2025, a 34 percent fall, across 57 territories, and most of the fall came between 1860 and 1980. Global, 1800 to 2025. Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- Global annual hours per working age person (15 to 64) fell from about 2,100 to about 1,300 over the same period, a 38 percent fall. Global, 1800 to 2025. Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- About 35 to 40 percent of two centuries of productivity gains went to fewer hours and 60 to 65 percent to more output. Global, 1800 to 2025. Verified (paper text). https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/wilwps/halshs-05500182.html
- Weekly hours per worker across OECD countries fell about 8 hours between 1970 and the 2010s, and the pace of decline slowed from 0.9 percent a year in the 1970s to 0.2 percent a year in the 2010s. OECD, 2021. Plausible (page refused connection, figures from the search summary). https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2021_5a700c4b-en/full-report/working-time-and-its-regulation-in-oecd-countries-how-much-do-we-work-and-how_bfff25bb
- Across countries hours per worker fall as income rises, but in the richest countries hours are flat or rise with the wage. Bick, Fuchs-Schuendeln and Lagakos, American Economic Review 2018, 80 plus countries. Plausible (abstract). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:108:y:2018:i:1:p:170-99
- The same team's 2022 model (with Tsujiyama) attributes more than half of the hours fall at low income to the shift from self employment to wage work, and says falling fixed costs of wage work imply higher hours in the future at high income. Journal of Monetary Economics 2022. Plausible (abstract). https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/29099.html
- A VoxEU column by the same authors puts the average cross country fall at about 0.45 percent a year. Date not confirmed. Plausible (page refused connection). https://voxeu.org/article/how-much-we-work-past-present-and-future

The US stall, mechanism evidence

- US weekly leisure rose 4.5 hours on average between 1965 and 2013, against market work of 33 to 37 hours a week, in a series that extends Aguiar and Hurst by ten years. Boppart and Ngai, CEPR 2017, published Journal of Economic Growth 2021. US. Verified (paper text). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- US adults with under 12 years of schooling gained slightly more than 8 hours a week of leisure 1965 to 2013, college graduates under 1 hour. US. Verified (paper text). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- Leisure and market hours did not diverge across US education groups until wages did, in the 1980s. After 1985 the more educated raised market hours and the less educated cut them, which held the average flat while leisure inequality grew. US, 1965 to 2013. Verified (paper text). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- Boppart and Ngai's mechanism is two forces: uneven productivity growth across home, market and leisure activities, plus a widening spread in pay. Their model fits the US path with no change in tastes. US. Verified (paper text). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- Long hours became 14.4 points more common among the fifth of US workers with the highest earnings and 6.7 points less common in the bottom fifth from 1979 to the mid 2000s. Kuhn and Lozano, NBER 2005. US. Plausible (figures from a secondary summary). https://www.nber.org/papers/w11895
- US average weekly hours fell 1950 to 1980, rose 1980 to 1990 and were constant 1990 to 2019 on one consistent method. Ruggeri, self published preprint, November 2022. US. Plausible (abstract, no numbers given). https://zenodo.org/records/7331283
- About half of the 1990s gap between US hours per person and other rich countries has since closed, and US hours per person fell after 2000 mainly because government health benefits for people not in work expanded. Birinci, Karabarbounis and See, Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis Working Paper 2026-002, posted 24 March 2026, revised 21 September 2026. US and comparison countries. Verified (abstract page). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlwp:102929
- In the comparison countries benefits for non workers changed little and public health cover does not depend on having a job, so their rising hours are explained by higher wages and a lower disutility of work. Same paper, 2026. Verified (abstract page). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlwp:102929
- Kaplan and five co authors list seven reasons the 15 hour week did not arrive: needs met by work, employer preference for full time staff, status and the ideal worker norm, US policy including employment tied health insurance, inertia and always on technology, class differences, and work no longer fitting an hours count. Industrial and Organizational Psychology vol 18 no 1, March 2025. US and rich countries. Verified. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/industrial-and-organizational-psychology/article/revisiting-keynes-predictions-about-work-and-leisure-a-discussion-of-fundamental-questions-about-the-nature-of-modern-work/311DE1B2CED69DC226AF5DD9AB04FD90
- Beretta and co authors tested Keynes against US data 1929 to 2019 and find productivity growth central to why hours stayed long. Journal of Risk and Financial Management, 2024. US. Plausible (abstract). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:gam:jjrfmx:v:17:y:2024:i:7:p:306-:d:1437283
- BLS American Time Use Survey for 2025, released 25 June 2026: men averaged 5.6 hours a day of leisure and sports, women 4.8, full time workers 8.1 hours on days worked, and all ATUS fieldwork stopped from 1 October to 12 November 2025 in the shutdown. US, 2025. Verified. https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm

Europe as the control group

- Euro area average hours per person employed have been falling, with a pandemic spike in the share working zero hours in the reference week that recovered only slowly, and a continued fall over the past decade in the share working very long hours. Berson and Weissler, ECB Economic Bulletin 3/2025. Euro area. Verified (abstract). https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecb/ecbbox/202500033.html
- A CEPR column argues the post pandemic fall in European hours is structural and extends a trend that predates 2020. Europe. Plausible (page refused connection, date not confirmed). https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/why-reduction-european-workers-hours-after-pandemic-here-stay
- The 2024 EU Ageing Report, read through the ECB, has euro area potential output growth falling to 0.8 percent in the early 2030s "as labour input growth turns negative" and 1.0 percent in 2070. It gives no hours per worker figure. Euro area, 2024. Verified. https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2024/html/ecb.ebbox202405_08~5f9531042f.en.html

Projections to 2035, 2050, 2076 and 2100 (every line is a scenario, not a fact)

- World Inequality Lab central scenario: annual hours per worker fall to about 1,000 by 2100 in all world regions, with 45 percent of productivity gains taken as time and 80 percent employment for both men and women. Global, 2100. Projection. Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- World Inequality Lab less ambitious scenario: about 1,260 hours a year by 2100, with 31 percent of gains taken as time. Global, 2100. Projection. Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- World Inequality Lab business as usual scenario extends 1990 to 2025 trends to 2100 and gives no separate hours number in the text read. North America hourly productivity would rise from 52.9 to 150.1 euros (PPP 2025) with no fall in hours implied. Projection. Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- The WIL authors say of their scenarios "Our objective here is not to predict the future". Verified (paper text). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf
- Metaculus Labor Automation Forecasting Hub, launched 21 April 2026 with a 2035 horizon: aggregated forecasts expect US average weekly hours to fall from 38 to 34, and forecasters flag "dark leisure", people at work who do less. US, 2035. Projection (vendor Metaculus, forecaster count not stated). https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/So7wrd6djpQcoP4rz/introducing-the-labor-automation-forecasting-hub-1
- Goldman Sachs analysts Briggs and Dong (vendor) say AI could automate about 25 percent of current work hours over the next decade. This is a share of tasks, not a forecast of hours worked. Global, 2025. Plausible (press summary). https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/goldman-sachs-ceo-says-ai-151700968.html
- Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan Chase (vendor opinion, restated 6 November 2025) expects the developed world to work three and a half days a week in 20 to 40 years. Projection (personal view). Plausible. https://fortune.com/article/jamie-dimon-jpmorgan-chase-ceo-ai-impact-working-week-3-day-100-years-future/
- OECD Employment Outlook 2025 (July 2025) projects the OECD working age population down 8 percent by 2060 and contains no hours per worker projection. OECD, 2025. Plausible (press release). https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/07/oecd-job-markets-remain-resilient-but-population-ageing-will-cause-significant-labour-shortages-and-fiscal-pressures.html

How the explanations now stack (my reading of the sources above, not a source claim)

- The taxes story (Prescott) has no new support in 2025 or 2026 work found. The newest US paper tests competing explanations and lands on benefits for non workers, not taxes, for the post 2000 period. Plausible (reading). https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlwp:102929
- The inequality story (Bell and Freeman, Skidelsky) now has a mechanism and a dated start. Boppart and Ngai show the split in hours and leisure across education groups starts in the 1980s exactly when pay spreads out, with high earners working more and low earners taking time. Plausible (reading). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- The institutions story (overtime threshold, holiday law, employment tied health cover) is the one both the 2025 psychology review and the 2026 St Louis Fed paper reach for on the US side. Plausible (reading). https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/industrial-and-organizational-psychology/article/revisiting-keynes-predictions-about-work-and-leisure-a-discussion-of-fundamental-questions-about-the-nature-of-modern-work/311DE1B2CED69DC226AF5DD9AB04FD90
- The measurement story (Aguiar and Hurst) still holds: US leisure per adult rose 4.5 hours a week to 2013, so the flat line is hours per worker, not free time. Verified (paper text). https://personal.lse.ac.uk/ngai/Boppart-Ngai.pdf
- The World Inequality Lab adds the longest view: the global fall was concentrated in 1860 to 1980, the era of labour movements and hours laws, so the stall after 1980 is the norm once that political push ends, not an American oddity. Plausible (reading). https://rloubes.github.io/Website/files/WP_LabourHours.pdf

## Who is working on it

- Global Labour Hours in Paid and Unpaid Work 1800 to 2100. Andreescu, Loubes, Piketty and Robilliard, World Inequality Lab Working Paper 2025/08, 14 May 2025, feeding the Global Justice Report due June 2026. Measures hours by gender, employment status and sector for 57 territories 1800 to 2025, with two scenarios to 2100. Latest finding: 34 percent fall in hours per worker, 35 to 40 percent of gains taken as time, most of the fall 1860 to 1980, and 1,000 or 1,260 hours a year by 2100 by scenario. https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/wilwps/halshs-05500182.html
- Why Do Americans No Longer Work So Much More Than Non-Americans? Birinci, Karabarbounis and See, Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis Working Paper 2026-002, 127 pages, posted March 2026 and revised September 2026. Measures US hours per person against other rich countries with a household labour supply model and the full tax and benefit system for non workers. Latest finding: half the 1990s gap closed, health benefits for the non employed the main US driver since 2000. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlwp:102929
- Labor Automation Forecasting Hub. Metaculus, launched 21 April 2026, updated continuously to 2035. Measures crowd forecasts of US employment, occupation exposure, wages and hours, young workers and wider indicators. Latest finding: US weekly hours 38 to 34, with "dark leisure" flagged as a reason reported hours may understate change. https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/So7wrd6djpQcoP4rz/introducing-the-labor-automation-forecasting-hub-1
- Rising inequality and trends in leisure. Boppart (IIES Stockholm) and Ngai (LSE), CEPR Discussion Paper 12325 (2017), Journal of Economic Growth 2021. Measures US time use 1965 to 2013 by education group in the Aguiar and Hurst method. Latest finding: leisure inequality tracks wage inequality from the 1980s. No 2025 or 2026 follow up found. https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/88064
- Revisiting Keynes' predictions about work and leisure. Kaplan, Aitken, Allan, Alliger, Ballard and Zacher, Industrial and Organizational Psychology 18(1), March 2025, a focal article with commentaries. Measures nothing new, sets a research agenda. Latest finding: seven explanation families, and open questions such as whether preferred hours predict wellbeing better than actual hours. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/industrial-and-organizational-psychology/article/revisiting-keynes-predictions-about-work-and-leisure-a-discussion-of-fundamental-questions-about-the-nature-of-modern-work/311DE1B2CED69DC226AF5DD9AB04FD90
- Who wants to work more? Berson and Weissler, ECB Economic Bulletin 3/2025. Measures euro area hours per person employed and the shares at zero and at very long hours. Latest finding: the fall is partly a slow recovery of zero hours workers and partly fewer very long weeks. https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecb/ecbbox/202500033.html
- American Time Use Survey. US Bureau of Labor Statistics, annual, 2025 data released 25 June 2026. Measures daily minutes by activity for the US population. Latest finding: leisure 5.6 hours a day for men and 4.8 for women, with a six week gap in fieldwork in late 2025. https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm
- Bick, Fuchs-Schuendeln and Lagakos. Cross country hours and income, AER 2018 and JME 2022. Their March 2026 NBER paper w34995 (with Blandin, Deming and Jessen) is about AI adoption gaps between Europe and the US, not hours. No 2025 or 2026 hours paper by them was found. https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/34995.html
- OECD annual hours database and Employment Outlook. The 2025 and 2026 editions focus on ageing and employment. No hours trend chapter was found in either. The database continues to give the OECD average (1,736 hours in 2025, already in the study). https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2025_194a947b-en/full-report

## One line for a manager

US hours per worker have been flat since 1980 because Americans took productivity as pay rather than time, the split began in the 1980s exactly when pay spread out, and since 2000 the fall in hours per person is mostly people leaving work on health benefits, not shorter weeks. For 2050 and 2076 there is no official US hours figure anywhere, only scenarios that run from about 1,000 to 2,070 hours a year, so plan for a band and watch the OECD annual series, not a point.
