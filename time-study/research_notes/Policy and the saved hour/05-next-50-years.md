# Policy and the saved hour: the next 50 years (2026 to 2076)

Research notes compiled 1 November 2026. Scope: how AI-driven productivity gains could be turned into shorter hours or shared income, the proposals and backers, pilot evidence, economists' projections by horizon, and the strongest challenges to the idea that policy can do this. Countries: US, UK, EU, Japan, Korea, China, plus international bodies.

How to read these notes. Every item carries a type label (Proposal, Pilot, Projection or Law) and a verification mark. Verified means I opened the source and saw the figure. Plausible means the figure comes only from a secondary source or a search snippet and the primary page could not be opened. Not found means I looked and could not confirm it. Year and country are given for every figure. Nothing in these notes is a fact about the future. Every Projection is a model output or a scenario, and the later writer should present it as such.

Research limits. The web search budget for this session ran out before the last round of checks, and several primary pages (DIW press page, Finnish ministry, OECD press release, Eurofound, UK Parliament answer, IIASA archive) refused automated access. Where that happened I used the nearest alternative primary source or marked the item Plausible. Gaps are listed under each question.

## Key Question 1: Shorter-week proposals and government positions, 2024 to 2026

### Takeaway
As of late 2026, no G7 or EU country has legislated a shorter statutory week in response to AI. Korea is the only country in scope with a government roadmap that puts a date (2030) on a lower hours target, and its enabling law was still stalled in mid 2026. The US bill is a repeated proposal with no committee progress found, the UK government has moved against four-day weeks in the public sector, Spain's bill failed in 2025, and the EU's 2025 to 2026 agenda is about disconnection and AI at work, not hours.

### Cited Findings

United States
- Proposal. Verified. 2026, US. Rep. Mark Takano and Sen. Bernie Sanders reintroduced the Thirty-Two Hour Workweek Act in September 2026 (119th Congress). It would amend the Fair Labor Standards Act so overtime starts after 32 hours, with no loss of pay or benefits. Takano first filed it in 2021. Endorsed by the AFL-CIO, National Nurses United and the United Auto Workers. Sanders: "A 32-hour workweek is not a radical idea." Takano: "It's time that labor standards caught up." No bill number, cosponsor count or committee action was reported. [WCAX, Gray News, 14 Sept 2026](https://www.wcax.com/2026/09/14/bill-introduced-reduce-workweek-32-hours/)
- Proposal. Not found. 2024, US. The 118th Congress version (Sanders, March 2024) could not be opened on Congress.gov (HTTP 403), so its phase-in schedule and cosponsor count are unconfirmed. [Congress.gov S.3947 page, blocked](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/3947)

United Kingdom
- Law. Verified. 2024, UK. Section 1 of the Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023 came into force on 6 April 2024, amending sections 80F and 80G of the Employment Rights Act 1996 (the right to request a contract variation). [SI 2024/438](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2024/438/made)
- Law. Plausible. 2024, UK. The 26-week service requirement was dropped by regulations so the request right applies from day one of employment. Employers can still refuse. [Conventus Law summary](https://conventuslaw.com/report/uk-flexible-working-right-to-make-a-flexible-working-request-becomes-a-day-one-right-on-6-april-2024/)
- Law (guidance). Verified. 2023, UK. The UK government told councils that "Councils which are undertaking four-day working week activities should cease immediately" and that "removing 20% of a local authority's potential capacity does not offer value for money for residents." Minister Lee Rowley wrote to South Cambridgeshire District Council asking it to end its trial. [gov.uk, 26 Oct 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-calls-time-on-councils-running-four-day-weeks)
- Proposal. Verified. 2026, UK. The Conservatives pledged on 8 April 2026 to ban councils from offering four-day weeks on full pay, led by Shadow Communities Secretary James Cleverly. South Cambridgeshire's 2023 trial reportedly saved nearly £400,000 in agency staffing costs, and 21 of 24 monitored services improved or held standards. Council leader Bridget Smith called the ban a call to end "a proven success." [LocalGov, 8 Apr 2026](https://www.localgov.co.uk/Conservatives-pledge-ban-on-council-four-day-work-weeks/64172)
- Government position. Plausible. 2026, UK. A written parliamentary answer dated 16 April 2026 said a four-day week on full pay is not civil service policy and is not being considered. The page returned HTTP 403 so the wording is from a search snippet. [Parallel Parliament question 124819](https://parallelparliament.co.uk/question/124819/cabinet-office-flexible-working)
- Proposal (campaign). Plausible. 2025, UK. An open letter with more than 100 signatories in October 2025 pushed the government to lead a transition to a shorter working week. Page returned HTTP 403. [HR Grapevine, 30 Oct 2025](https://www.hrgrapevine.com/content/article/2025-10-30-government-pushed-to-rethink-crackdown-on-four-day-work-weeks)
- Pilot (private). Plausible. 2025, UK. Around 200 UK companies had adopted a four-day week with no pay cut by January 2025, per the 4 Day Week Foundation as reported second hand. [Mezha, 28 Jan 2025](https://mezha.ua/en/2025/01/28/without-reducing-wages-200-uk-companies-have-already-switched-to-a-four-day-work-week/amp/)

Korea
- Proposal (pledge). Verified. 2025, Korea. On 30 April 2025 candidate Lee Jae-myung pledged "clear support measures for companies that adopt a 4.5-day workweek", a 2030 goal of cutting average hours below the OECD average, a legal cap on daily hours, a mandatory minimum rest period, mandatory recording of actual hours and a review of the "comprehensive wage system". Quote: "In the long term, we must move toward a four-day workweek." [Korea JoongAng Daily, 30 Apr 2025](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/dp-candidate-lee-jae-myung-announces-policy-aiming-for-a-45-day-workweek/12287421)
- Proposal (government plan). Verified. 2025, Korea. The government plan would cut the statutory week from 40 to 36 hours without a pay cut, with a long-term aim of a 4-day, 32-hour week. A "Reduced Working Hours Support Act" offering subsidies and tax breaks was due to go to the National Assembly by end 2025. Target: below the OECD average of 1,742 hours a year by 2030, from 1,874 hours in 2023. The Korea Enterprises Federation said "simply reducing statutory working hours will undermine corporate competitiveness." The KCCI's Sustainable Growth Initiative cited 2023 output of $65,000 per worker, ranked 22nd of 36 OECD countries. [Korea Herald, 29 Sept 2025](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10585955)
- Proposal (roadmap). Verified. 2025, Korea. Three-stage roadmap from the Ministry of Employment and Labor: 2025 pass the support law and start pilots, 2026 prohibit widespread use of the comprehensive wage system, 2027 begin wider debate on extending the 4.5-day week nationwide. Korea Employers Federation figures: hourly productivity $54.60 in 2023, 77.4% of the OECD average, G7 average $80.60. Korean employees logged 1,874 hours in 2023, 132 above the OECD average. [Korea Times, 19 Sept 2025](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/politics/20250919/govt-seeks-passage-of-45-day-workweek-bill-this-year-despite-industry-pushback)
- Proposal (timeline). Verified. 2025, Korea. Tripartite task force launched 23 September 2025. Timeline: final roadmap by end 2025, 4.5-day pilots in selected workplaces in 2026, expansion "in earnest" after 2027, annual hours down to 1,742 by 2030. Korea's 2024 figure given as 1,872 hours, 130 above the OECD average. [Asia Economy, 25 Sept 2025](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2025092510242785780)
- Pilot (regional) and Proposal (national budget). Verified. 2025 to 2026, Korea. Gyeonggi Province model (from June 2025): firms with 5 to under 300 workers can cut the 40-hour week to 35 hours by labour-management agreement. Wage-preservation incentive per worker per month: 260,000 won for a 5-hour cut, 210,000 for 4 hours, 160,000 for 3 hours, 110,000 for 2 hours. 107 companies had joined by October 2025. Runs through 2027. National "Work-Life Balance +4.5" programme in the 2026 draft budget: 27.6 billion won, adoption incentive 200,000 to 600,000 won per worker per month, hiring incentive 600,000 to 800,000 won per month per replacement hire. [JILPT Korea topics, Nov 2025](https://www.jil.go.jp/foreign/jihou/2025/11/korea_01.html)
- Status. Verified. 2026, Korea. Korea's average annual hours fell to 1,833 in 2025 (from 1,865 in 2024), 97 hours above the OECD average of 1,736, sixth longest of 36 OECD members. MOEL is "accelerating" its roadmap centred on the 4.5-day week, issued April 2026 guidelines against misuse of comprehensive wage systems, and wants Labor Standards Act amendments that are "stalled in the National Assembly" passed. Target: actual hours in the 1,700-hour range by 2030. [Financial News, 5 July 2026](https://en.fnnews.com/news/202607051045555290)
- Pilot (anecdotal). Plausible. 2025, Korea. A Severance Hospital nurse told a National Assembly briefing in August 2025 that four-day-week nurses reported higher satisfaction, lower stress and lower turnover. No official MOEL evaluation found. [Seoulz aggregator](https://www.seoulz.com/korea-4-5-day-workweek-2026/)

### Inferences
- Of the six jurisdictions, only Korea has a dated, quantified national hours target tied to a programme (1,700-hour range by 2030). Even there the mechanism is subsidy and voluntary adoption, not a statutory cut yet, and the support law was unpassed in mid 2026.
- Spain's 2025 defeat is the clearest recent test of a statutory hours cut in a large EU economy. It failed on a parliamentary majority, not on economics, which supports the political-economy challenge in Question 5.
- The UK is moving in the opposite direction from the campaign: the right-to-request route (Law 2024) plus active discouragement of public-sector four-day weeks (2023 guidance, 2026 opposition pledge).
- No bill in any of the six jurisdictions ties statutory hours to measured productivity growth. The closest is Korea's roadmap language that firms should get productivity support as they cut hours.

### Gaps
- Congress.gov blocked, so the 2024 bill's phase-in schedule and cosponsors are unconfirmed.
- No European Parliament plenary resolution on a four-day week in 2025 to 2026 was found. Absence in search is not proof of absence.
- UK Employment Rights Act 2025 flexible working provisions were not checked (search budget exhausted).
- Korea: no official pilot evaluation found, and passage status of the support act is unknown.
- Japan: no national-level 2025 to 2026 document linking AI or Society 5.0 to hours. The national "selective three-day weekend" promotion page (2024) returned HTTP 410.
- China: no hours cap or hours target found in the 15th plan materials opened.

## Key Question 2: Income-sharing proposals and pilot evidence

### Takeaway
Across seven cash pilots in five countries the measured labour supply effect ranges from a small positive (Finland, Stockton first year) to a small negative (US OpenResearch, 1.3 hours a week). None shows a large withdrawal from work. The AI-specific sharing proposals (Altman's equity fund, Sanders' AI sovereign fund, OpenAI's public wealth fund, the Windfall Clause) are all unlegislated, and the only enacted "robot tax" anywhere was a 2017 Korean cut to a deduction, not a levy.

### Cited Findings

Cash pilots
- Pilot. Verified. 2020 to 2023, US (Illinois and Texas). OpenResearch Unconditional Cash Study: 1,000 recipients got $1,000 a month and 2,000 controls got $50 a month for three years, across 19 counties, ages 21 to 40, household income at or below 300% of the federal poverty line. [OpenResearch study design](https://openresearchlab.org/studies/unconditional-cash-study)
- Pilot. Verified. 2024 results, US. Recipients worked 1.3 fewer hours a week on average across three years and were about 2 percentage points less likely to be employed in years two and three. Partners cut hours by a "comparable" amount. Under-30s cut 1.8 hours and single parents 2.8 hours a week. Household income excluding transfers fell $2,500 to $4,100 (about 5% of the control mean). Employment rose from 58% to 72% among recipients and from 59% to 74% among controls. Recipients were 6 percentage points more likely to be actively searching for a job. [OpenResearch employment findings](https://openresearchlab.org/findings/key-findings-employment-and-income)
- Pilot. Verified. 2017 to 2018, Finland. 2,000 unemployed people received 560 euros a month unconditionally. Over November 2017 to October 2018 the basic income raised employment by 6 days, to an average of 78 days. The 2018 activation model complicated interpretation. Recipients reported higher life satisfaction and less mental strain. [Finland Toolbox summary of Kela results](https://toolbox.finland.fi/?p=3883)
- Pilot. Verified. 2019 to 2021, US (Stockton). SEED gave 125 residents $500 a month for 24 months. In the first year (February 2019 to February 2020) recipients obtained full-time employment "at more than twice the rate of nonrecipients" and were less anxious and depressed. Evaluators Stacia West and Amy Castro Baker. [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/guaranteed-income-increases-employment-improves-financial-and-physical-health)
- Pilot. Plausible. 2019 to 2020, US (Stockton). Full-time employment rose from 28% to 40% among recipients versus 32% to 37% among controls. [UT Knoxville news](https://csw.utk.edu/news/results-from-guaranteed-income-study-released/)
- Pilot. Verified. 2021 to 2024, Germany. Pilotprojekt Grundeinkommen (Mein Grundeinkommen, evaluated by DIW Berlin and WU Vienna): 107 recipients got 1,200 euros a month for three years from June 2021, against 1,580 comparison persons. Participants neither withdrew from the labour market nor significantly reduced hours. Mental health improved significantly. About one third of payments was saved, more than double the comparison group, and nearly 8% went to charity, family or friends. Published April 2025. [DIW Wochenbericht 15/2025, Bohmann, Fiedler, Kasy, Schupp, Schwerter](https://ideas.repec.org/a/diw/diwwob/92-15-1.html)
- Pilot. Verified. 2022 to 2025, UK (Wales). 644 care leavers (97% of eligible) received £1,600 gross, £1,280 net, a month for two years. Sign-up ran 1 July 2022 to 30 June 2023. Qualitative findings on work were "varied": some used security to pursue education, "others reported reduced motivation to engage in work". One head of service said some who were working "then stopped working, because they had income that they didn't have to work for." Quantitative outcome data is "forthcoming in 2027". Report dated 26 February 2026. [Welsh Government third annual report summary](https://www.gov.wales/sites/default/files/statistics-and-research/2026-02/basic-income-care-leavers-wales-pilot-evaluation-annual-report-2025-2026-summary-380041.pdf)
- Pilot. Verified. 2019 onward, Korea (Gyeonggi). Youth Basic Income pays 24-year-old residents 250,000 won a quarter for four quarters in local currency. 2019 before-and-after surveys found improvements in life satisfaction and work motivation. Small business sales reportedly rose 45% (no baseline given). No employment effect estimate was found. [BIEN, Malcolm Torry, 12 Mar 2021](https://basicincome.org/?p=27293)

AI dividend and wealth fund proposals
- Proposal. Verified. 2021, US. Sam Altman's "Moore's Law for Everything" (16 March 2021) proposes an American Equity Fund: companies above a valuation threshold pay 2.5% of market value a year "payable in shares transferred to the fund", plus a 2.5% tax on privately held land "payable in dollars". "All citizens over 18 would get an annual distribution, in dollars and company shares." He estimates "each of the 250 million adults in America would get about $13,500 every year" "a decade from now", assuming company and land values roughly double and the tax lowers values about 15%. [moores.samaltman.com](https://moores.samaltman.com/)
- Proposal. Verified. 2026, US. Sen. Sanders proposed an American AI sovereign wealth fund in June 2026: major AI firms would transfer a significant portion of their stock into a publicly owned fund, with annual dividends to Americans and funding for health, education and housing. No percentages or fund size given. [Baton Rouge Business Report citing the Washington Post, 18 June 2026](https://www.businessreport.com/article/bernie-sanders-proposes-national-ai-wealth-fund)
- Proposal. Plausible. 2026, US. OpenAI's April 2026 paper "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age" proposes a Public Wealth Fund giving every citizen a stake, "targeted taxes on sustained AI-driven returns", exploring taxes on automated labour, automatic-trigger wage insurance, and employer "efficiency dividends" that could include four-day-week pilots. No rates, sizes or timelines. Summarised second hand. [Progress.org wiki summary](https://www.progress.org/wiki/openai-industrial-policy-intelligence-age/)
- Proposal. Verified. 2019 to 2020, UK and US. The Windfall Clause (O'Keefe, Cihon, Garfinkel, Flynn, Leung, Dafoe) is "an ex ante commitment by AI firms to donate a significant amount of any eventual extremely large profits" from transformative AI. [arXiv 1912.11595](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.11595)
- Proposal. Plausible. 2020. The report defines windfall as profits above 1% of gross world product. No company has adopted it as far as the sources show. [Future of Life Institute podcast with Cullen O'Keefe, 28 Feb 2020](https://futureoflife.org/2020/02/28/distributing-the-benefits-of-ai-via-the-windfall-clause-with-cullen-okeefe/)
- Proposal. Verified. 2017, US. Korinek and Stiglitz (NBER 24174, December 2017): "Under plausible conditions, non-distortionary taxation can be levied to compensate those who otherwise might lose." [NBER w24174](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24174)

Robot tax debate
- Proposal. Verified. 2017, US. Bill Gates told Quartz in February 2017 that robots doing human work should be taxed, with revenue funding retraining for roles such as child care and elder care, and that a tax could slow automation to a manageable pace. [Engadget, 19 Feb 2017](https://www.engadget.com/2017/02/19/bill-gates-calls-for-robot-tax)
- Proposal (rejected). Verified. 2017, EU. The European Parliament adopted the Delvaux robotics report by 396 votes to 123 in February 2017, but the robot tax and universal basic income passages were "narrowly defeated" and not adopted. [AK Europa, 23 Feb 2017](https://www.akeuropa.eu/en/first-proposal-rules-robots-still-robot-tax-rejected-parliament)
- Law. Verified. 2017, Korea. Korea's 2017 tax revision cut deductions for automation investment that had been "between 3 and 7 percent" by 2 points. MIT Technology Review: "certainly not a full-on 'robot tax'". [MIT Technology Review, 9 Aug 2017](https://www.technologyreview.com/2017/08/09/242207/south-korea-is-decreasing-tax-breaks-on-automation-and-thats-probably-a-bad-idea)
- Projection (model). Verified. 2020, US. Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo (Brookings, 18 March 2020) find US effective tax rates on labour "stand above 28.5 percent" versus "about 5 percent" on equipment and software capital. Their optimum is 9 percent on labour and 22 percent on capital. Eliminating the capital bias would raise employment 6.5 percent and the labour share 1.1 percentage points. The page gives no separate robot tax rate. [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/does-the-u-s-tax-code-favor-automation/)
- Projection (model). Verified. 2022, US. The "1 to 3.7 percent" optimal robot tax is from Costinot and Werning (Review of Economic Studies), not Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo. Their optimal robot tax is 1 to 3.7 percent of value and optimal trade tax 0.03 to 0.11 percent, with the robot tax falling as robots saturate the economy. Inputs from Acemoglu and Restrepo: each robot per 1,000 workers cut the employment-to-population ratio about 0.2 percent, each manufacturing robot replaced about 3.3 workers. [MIT News, 21 Dec 2022](https://economics.mit.edu/news/should-we-tax-robots-0)

### Inferences
- The pilot literature gives a defensible range for the hours effect of a roughly $1,000 to 1,200 a month unconditional payment in a rich country: about zero (Germany) to minus 1.3 hours a week (US), which is 3 to 5 percent of a 30 to 40 hour week. This is useful as a lower bound on how much freed time a cash dividend alone might produce, and it is small.
- The assignment's attribution of "optimal robot tax of 1 to 4 percent" to Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo 2020 should be corrected: the range is 1 to 3.7 percent and belongs to Costinot and Werning (2022). Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo argue for levelling capital and labour tax rates, not a robot-specific levy.
- The only AI-sharing proposal with explicit numbers is Altman's (2.5% plus 2.5%, about $13,500 per adult). Every other 2024 to 2026 proposal (Sanders, OpenAI, IMF notes) is a mechanism without a rate.
- Every rich-country pilot was time-limited (two to three years). The Welsh professionals' and recipients' accounts of the "cliff" at the end suggest pilot results understate behavioural change under a permanent scheme, in either direction.

### Gaps
- Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend: not researched before the search budget ran out. The classic employment study (Jones and Marinescu 2018) was not opened, so no figures are recorded.
- OpenResearch publication date (July 2024) and authors were not confirmed on the pages opened.
- Finnish final report release date (6 May 2020) comes only from search snippets. The ministry and VATT pages returned HTTP 403.
- Gyeonggi: no employment effect estimate found. The Gyeonggi Research Institute's December 2020 difference-in-differences study mentions "economic activity" but the measure is unspecified.
- Korea's reported 2025 idea of a national dividend funded by AI and semiconductor tax revenue (attributed to presidential secretary Kim Yong-beom in a secondary summary) could not be sourced. Not found.
- Windfall Clause's 1% of gross world product threshold is from a podcast summary only.

## Key Question 3: Working-time policy instruments under discussion

### Takeaway
The live instruments in 2024 to 2026 are incremental: a right to request shorter hours (UK), a right to disconnect (EU), overtime and wage-bundling rules (Japan, Korea), wage subsidies for voluntary hours cuts (Korea), and life-course time accounts (Belgium, Germany). No jurisdiction is using a productivity-indexed hours rule, and the public sector is being used as a lever in both directions (Tokyo for, UK against).

### Cited Findings
- Law. Verified. 2024, UK. Right to request flexible working from 6 April 2024 under s.1 of the 2023 Act (see Question 1). [SI 2024/438](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2024/438/made)
- Law. Verified. 2012 reform, Belgium. Time credit lets a worker suspend the contract fully or partly. Three types: with motive (child-raising, caring for a sick parent), without motive, and end of career. The reform cut no-motive time credit from up to 5 years to "the equivalent of one year throughout the career": 12 months full time, 24 months half time, or 60 months at a one-fifth reduction. Eligibility: 5 years' experience and 24 months in the company. [Mind, 10 July 2012](https://www.mind.eu.com/rh/en/article/belgium-social-partners-agree-to-reform-time-credit/)
- Law (collective agreement). Plausible. 1998 and 2000, Germany. A 1998 WSI study found agreed working time account limits of 40 to 600 hours of credit and 20 to 170 hours of debit. In October 2000 IG Metall and steel employers signed the first sectoral agreement on "working-life time accounts" allowing overtime pay to be banked and exchanged for free time. Eurofound later reported low uptake. Pages returned HTTP 429. [Eurofound, provisions in collective agreements](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/provisions-working-time-accounts-collective-agreements) and [Eurofound, low uptake](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/low-uptake-working-time-accounts)

### Inferences
- Time accounts (Belgium, Germany) are the only instruments that let a worker bank productivity-related time and spend it over a lifetime, and both show restriction or low uptake rather than expansion in the sources found.
- Korea's approach (subsidy per worker per month for a voluntary hours cut) is the one instrument in scope that explicitly compensates firms for the cost of shorter hours. Its 2026 to 2027 results would be the first test of a paid-for hours cut at national scale.
- The right-to-request model (UK) shifts the hours decision to the individual bargain with the employer, which the Stansbury and Summers evidence in Question 5 suggests favours the employer.

### Gaps
- Belgium's post-2012 time credit rules (later tightening of end-of-career credit) were not checked.
- Germany's current share of workers with time accounts was not confirmed (Eurofound blocked).
- No source found for a "lifetime hours" or sabbatical account proposed as an AI-era policy in 2024 to 2026.

## Key Question 4: Economists' projections and official futures at 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 25 and 50 year horizons

### Takeaway
The quantified projections split into two camps. Task-based macro models (Acemoglu) give AI a total factor productivity gain of no more than 0.53 to 0.66 percent over ten years, too small to fund a meaningful hours cut on its own. Scenario work (Korinek, UK GO-Science, BCG) spans everything from business as usual to full automation with wage collapse, and only one source (BCG, April 2026) attaches a number to hours per worker in 2050. No official body in scope publishes a 2075 hours figure.

## Key Question 5: Five challenges to policy-led hours reduction, each from a named researcher

### Takeaway
Each challenge has a named source with numbers. Together they say: freed time refills with consumption (Sorrell), care work cannot shrink hours where productivity does not rise (Baumol), gains flow to capital when worker power is weak (Stansbury and Summers, Acemoglu), leisure already rose and rose most for the less educated (Aguiar and Hurst), and ageing plus slow growth make shorter hours unaffordable (Gordon, OECD, Korea Employers Federation).

### Cited Findings

Challenge 1: rebound and Jevons, freed time refills
- Verified. 2020, UK. Sorrell, Gatersleben and Druckman (Energy Research and Social Science 64): "rebound effects can erode a significant proportion of the anticipated energy and emission savings from sufficiency actions", and downshifting (reduced working time and income) cuts energy use "but by proportionately less" than hours and income fall. The page gives no numeric erosion share. [CREDS](https://www.creds.ac.uk/publications/the-limits-of-energy-sufficiency-a-review-of-the-evidence-for-rebound-effects-and-negative-spillovers-from-behavioural-change/)
- Verified. 2008, UK. Sorrell and Dimitropoulos (Ecological Economics 65): the opportunity cost of time implies "a parallel rebound effect with respect to time". [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ecolec:v:65:y:2008:i:3:p:636-649)
- Verified (bibliographic only). 2005. Alcott, "Jevons' paradox", Ecological Economics 54(1), 9 to 21. Content on efficiency backfire and the case for caps is Plausible from a bibliographic record. [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolec/v54y2005i1p9-21.html)
- Plausible. 2016, Germany. Buhl and Acosta (Sustainability Science): "time savings due to a reduction in working time trigger relevant rebound effects in terms of resource use", though the authors put the size "into perspective". [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11625-015-0322-8)
- The argument for the writer: if saved hours are spent on travel, consumption and paid side work, the environmental and wellbeing case for shorter hours weakens, and the labour supply effect may be smaller than the statutory cut.

Challenge 2: Baumol and the care sector
- Verified. 2012 to 2013, US. Baumol, "The Cost Disease": rising costs in health and education come from "the nature of providing labor-intensive services". [Yale University Press](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300198157/the-cost-disease/)
- Plausible. 2023, UK. Analysis of a 32-hour week for the NHS: "NHS workers are unlikely to be able to reduce their working hours by increasing labour productivity." Page returned HTTP 403. [inkl, The Conversation republication](https://www.inkl.com/news/nhs-what-impact-will-a-32-hour-working-week-have-on-it)
- Plausible. 2020, Poland. Russo models a no-growth economy with reduced hours and finds health care's relative cost still rises because the sector needs more workers when productivity grows slowly. [Economic and Environmental Studies](https://czasopisma.uni.opole.pl/index.php/ees/article/view/3069)
- Verified. 2026, global. BCG's AI Abundance scenario itself notes that remaining work "mostly focus[es] on the caring professions, AI oversight and judgment roles, and manual trades". [BCG PDF](https://web-assets.bcg.com/20/b1/521bbd2d431fba3b32dac5af5d64/bcg-scenarios-2050-apr-2026-web.pdf)
- The argument for the writer: a statutory hours cut applied economy-wide forces care, education and hospitality to hire more people at the same output, so the cost of the cut lands where AI productivity gains are smallest. Dietz could not be identified as a source on this point (see Gaps).

Challenge 3: political economy and employer capture
- Verified. 2020, US. Stansbury and Summers, "The Declining Worker Power Hypothesis" (NBER 27193): reduced worker power "are associated with lower wage levels, higher profit shares, and reductions in measures of the NAIRU", and this "is more compelling as an explanation for observed changes than increases in firms' market power". [NBER w27193](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27193)
- Plausible. 2020, US. Private-sector union membership fell from one third of the workforce at peak to 6 percent. [Marginal Revolution summary](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2020/05/stansbury-and-summers-on-the-declining-bargaining-power-of-labor.html)
- Verified. 2024, US. Acemoglu: "AI is predicted to widen the gap between capital and labor income." [NBER w32487](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487)
- Verified. 2026, UK. GO-Science Take-Off scenario: "capital income consumes a growing proportion of national income". [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-scenarios-2030-helping-policymakers-plan-for-the-future-of-ai/ai-scenarios-2030-helping-policymakers-plan-for-the-future-of-ai)
- Verified. 2012, UK. Skidelsky: hours stopped falling in the 1980s "at the same time as inequality started rising". [Carnegie Council](https://www.carnegiecouncil.org/media/series/39/20120612-how-much-is-enough-money-and-the-good-life)
- Plausible. 2013 and 2019. Piketty's argument that without strong redistribution inequality rises across generations, extended by Moll, Rachel and Restrepo (2019) to automation raising returns to wealth. Secondary only. [Trammell essay](https://philiptrammell.substack.com/cp/182887993)
- Verified. 2025, Spain. The 37.5-hour bill failed 178 to 170 with employer federations "firmly against". [The Local](https://www.thelocal.es/20250911/spains-congress-rejects-law-to-shorten-work-week)

Challenge 4: measurement, leisure already grew and is unequal
- Verified. 2006 to 2007, US. Aguiar and Hurst, "Measuring Trends in Leisure": leisure rose 6 to 8 hours a week for men and 4 to 8 for women between 1965 and 2003, equal to roughly 5 to 10 extra weeks of vacation a year, with less-educated adults gaining most and "growing leisure inequality" mirroring wage inequality. - Verified. 2024, EU. EU average hours fell from 37.0 (2014) to 36.0 (2024) with no AI policy. [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20250514-1)
- The argument for the writer: a uniform statutory cut gives the most new leisure to those who already gained it (lower-wage, less-educated, often involuntarily part-time) and the least to the long-hours professionals who want it, so measured average hours are a poor target.

Challenge 5: global competition and ageing make shorter hours unaffordable

- Plausible. 2025, OECD. Working-age population down 8% by 2060, pension and health spending up 3% of GDP. [OECD press release](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/07/oecd-job-markets-remain-resilient-but-population-ageing-will-cause-significant-labour-shortages-and-fiscal-pressures.html)
- Plausible. 2023, Japan. Recruit Works: shortfall of more than 11 million workers by 2040. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-03-29/japan-to-face-11-million-worker-shortfall-by-2040-study-finds)
- Verified. 2023, UK. Government view that cutting 20% of council capacity "does not offer value for money". [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-calls-time-on-councils-running-four-day-weeks)
- The argument for the writer: with fewer workers per retiree and tax bases shrinking, governments in Japan, Korea and the EU are more likely to ask for more hours per worker (later retirement, higher participation) than fewer, unless AI gains are both large and captured by the state.

### Inferences
- Challenges 3 and 5 reinforce each other: the ageing countries most in need of productivity (Japan, Korea) are also the ones whose employer federations have the strongest fiscal argument against hours cuts.
- Challenge 4 cuts against the simplest policy design (a uniform statutory week) more than against hours reduction in general. It supports targeted instruments (time accounts, right to request, care-sector staffing) over blanket cuts.
- Challenge 1 is the weakest on numbers: the sources agree rebound exists but none gives a share of saved time that refills.

## Key Question 6: Quantified scenarios for hours per worker in 2050 or 2075

### Takeaway
One source in scope gives a scenario-based hours-per-worker figure for 2050 (BCG, April 2026: 1,600 to 2,070 hours a year by scenario, from 2,100 in 2025). One older extrapolation (IIASA 1996: 27-hour week by 2050) is Plausible only. No official body (OECD, IMF, EU, UK, Japan, Korea, China) publishes a 2050 or 2075 hours-per-worker scenario that could be opened.

### Cited Findings
- Projection. Verified. 2026, global. BCG Henderson Institute scenarios for 2050: annual hours per worker 2,100 in 2025, then AI Abundance 1,600, Battling Blocs 2,070, Climate Coalition 2,070, Digital Darwinism 1,900. Regional 2025 baselines: India and China about 2,350, US 1,800, Europe 1,500. [BCG PDF](https://web-assets.bcg.com/20/b1/521bbd2d431fba3b32dac5af5d64/bcg-scenarios-2050-apr-2026-web.pdf)
- Projection. Plausible. 1996, global. Ausubel and Grübler: a 27-hour average work week by 2050 if long-term trends continue. [IIASA](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/4461/1/RR-96-04.pdf)
- Projection. Plausible. 2024, EU. Ageing Report 2024 total hours worked down 9% from 2022 to 2070. [Pension Policy International](https://www.pensionpolicyinternational.com/?p=71282)
- Baseline. Verified. 2025, OECD. OECD average 1,736 hours a year in 2025, Korea 1,833. [Financial News](https://en.fnnews.com/news/202607051045555290)
- Baseline. Verified. 2024, EU. 36.0 hours a week. [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20250514-1)

### Inferences
- A defensible scenario band for the writer, labelled as projection: for an OECD economy at about 1,736 hours in 2025, a BCG-style 25% automation-led fall gives about 1,300 hours in 2050, while the no-AI-dividend scenarios give 1,700 to 1,720. Applying BCG's AI Abundance ratio to Europe's 1,500 gives about 1,125 hours, roughly a 25-hour week over 45 working weeks. These are my arithmetic on BCG's ratios, not BCG figures.
- No source supports a 15-hour week by 2076. The most aggressive published scenario (BCG AI Abundance) leaves 2050 hours above 1,600 a year.
