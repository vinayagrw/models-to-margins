# Post-war hours policy and the US to Europe divergence, 1945 to 2022

Scope: US, UK, France, Germany, Nordics (Sweden, Denmark, Norway), Japan, Korea, China. Every number carries a status tag. Verified means I opened the source and saw the figure. Plausible means the figure comes from a secondary source or a search snippet I could not open in full. Not found means I looked and could not confirm it. Year and country are given for every figure. Figures from one OECD vintage should not be mixed with another vintage without a note, because the OECD revises the whole series.

A note on the OECD hours series. The JILPT Databook reproduces the OECD "Average annual hours actually worked per worker" table and repeats the OECD warning that the data are built for within-country time comparison and are not suited to comparing levels across countries in a given year because sources and methods differ. All cross-country level comparisons below should be read with that caveat.

## Key question 0. Annual hours per worker by country, 1950 to 2023

### Takeaway
Every country in scope cut annual hours per worker sharply between 1950 and 1980. After 1980 the US series went flat (about 1,816 in 1980, 1,832 to 1,880 in 2000 depending on vintage, 1,804 in 2022), while France, Germany, Denmark, Norway and Japan kept falling by 300 to 500 hours. Sweden is the odd Nordic case, its hours rose from 1980 to 2000. Korea fell from about 2,860 in 1980 to 1,901 in 2022, and China's PWT series rose from 2,243 in 1980 to about 2,357 in 2019.

### Inferences
- The US plateau is visible in every series. In the PWT series the US figure in 2000 (1,888) is above its 1980 figure (1,840). In the OECD 2024 vintage the US figure is 1,880 in 2000 and 1,804 in 2022, a fall of only 4 percent over 22 years, against 8 percent for Germany, 4 percent for France and 12 percent for Japan over the same window.
- Sweden's rise from 1,370 (1980) to 1,489 (2000) in PWT, and 1,382 to 1,486 in the OECD 2017 vintage, is driven by composition. Sweden had very high part-time participation among women in 1980 and shifted to longer part-time and full-time hours later. This is my reading of the series, not a cited finding.
- Korea's series, not Japan's, is the extreme case. Korea in 1980 was about 1,000 hours above the US and still 100 above it in 2022.

## Key question 1. France: the 1982 39-hour week and the 1998 to 2000 Aubry laws

### Takeaway
France cut the legal week from 40 to 39 hours and added a fifth week of paid leave in 1982, then cut it to 35 hours in 2000 (firms over 20 employees) and 2002 (smaller firms). The best micro evidence says firms that moved to 35 hours cut weekly hours about 10 percent, lost about 3.7 percent of total factor productivity, and still grew employment 9.9 percent faster than firms that stayed at 39 hours, mainly because of payroll tax cuts and wage moderation rather than work sharing. The official DARES estimate is about 350,000 jobs created or saved over 1996 to 2002. The Alsace-Moselle natural experiment finds no employment effect at all.

### Cited Findings
- Ordinance 82-41 of 16 January 1982 cut the legal weekly duration from 40 to 39 hours and generalised the fifth week of paid leave. Plausible, search snippets from Larousse and vie-publique, I could not open the full pages. - Larousse adds that the provisions took effect on 1 February 1982 with a daily maximum of 10 hours. Plausible. [Larousse archive 1982](https://www.larousse.fr/archives/journaux_annee/1982/44/relations_sociales)
- The 1936 laws (20 and 21 June 1936) had set the 40-hour week and the first two weeks of paid leave. Plausible. [CNAM, Les congés payés ont 90 ans](https://www.cnam.fr/actualites/les-conges-payes-ont-90-ans)
- Aubry I, law 98-461 of 13 June 1998, set the legal duration at 35 hours from 1 January 2000 for firms with more than 20 employees and from 1 January 2002 for the rest, with incentive aid for firms that moved early. Plausible, search snippet from an Assemblée nationale written answer. [Assemblée nationale written question](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/13/questions/QANR5L13QE111830.pdf)
- Aubry II, law 2000-37 of 19 January 2000, set the operating rules. Eurofound dates the fall from 39 to 35 hours for firms over 20 employees to 1 February 2000. Plausible, snippet only, the Eurofound page returned 429. [Eurofound, law on 35-hour week in force](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/resources/article/2000/law-35-hour-week-force)
- Aubry II rules as summarised by IRES: overtime quota 130 hours a year, a payroll relief replacing the general relief, majority agreement required, monthly minimum wage maintained for workers moving to 35 hours. Verified, read from the IRES presentation PDF. [Lerais, IRES, 2 October 2014](https://ires.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Effets_des_35_heures_octobre_2014_final.pdf)
- The overtime ceiling was raised from 180 to 220 hours a year on 22 December 2004. Plausible, Wikipedia. [Wikipedia, 35-hour workweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/35-hour_workweek)
- Crépon, Leclair and Roux (2004), firm data, Aubry I firms versus firms still at 39 hours at end 2000: the move to 35 hours should have cut the working week by four hours or 10.2 percent. Total factor productivity fell 3.7 percent relative to 39-hour firms. Employment rose 9.9 percent relative to 39-hour firms. The authors write that productivity losses were smaller than the effects of wage moderation and reductions in charges, that Aubry I firms used the shorter week to cut unit production costs, and that work sharing mechanisms do not appear to play a prominent role. Verified, read from the IDEAS abstract page. [Crépon, Leclair, Roux, Économie et Statistique 376-377](https://ideas.repec.org/a/prs/ecstat/estat_0336-1454_2004_num_376_1_7234.html)

- Estevão and Sá (IZA DP 2459, 2006, published in Economic Policy 2008): using the timing difference by firm size and the French labour force survey, the law "constrained the choice of a significant number of individuals". Employment of persons directly affected declined, but the net effect on aggregate employment was not significant. - Abowd's comment on Chemin and Wasmer: the hours cut was 0.38 (plus or minus 0.17) hour per week less effective in Alsace-Moselle, close to the 0.4 hour predicted (10 percent of 4 hours). - OECD series for France: 1990 1,645, 2000 1,558, 2005 1,532, 2010 1,540. Verified, JILPT. The fall from 1995 (1,601) to 2005 (1,532) is 69 hours, about 4.3 percent, which brackets the 35-hour implementation.

### Inferences
- What happened to the saved time. The micro evidence splits it three ways. Part became jobs in the firms that cut hours (about 7 to 10 percent extra employment growth relative to controls), part became higher hourly productivity (the 10.2 percent hours cut produced only a 3.7 percent TFP loss, so output per hour rose), and part leaked out through second jobs and moves to small firms. Pay per hour rose, monthly pay was held flat by the wage guarantee and wage moderation.
- Did productivity per hour rise enough to keep output flat? Not quite at firm level. A 10.2 percent hours cut with a 3.7 percent TFP loss implies output per hour rose roughly 6 to 7 percent, so about two thirds of the hours cut was offset by productivity, and the rest by extra hiring. At the national level the aggregate employment effect is disputed, from zero (Chemin and Wasmer) to 350,000 (DARES).
- The 350,000 figure is a short-run estimate for 1996 to 2002 and includes the Robien scheme. It is not an estimate for the 35-hour law alone.

### Gaps
- I could not open the DARES 2004 study or the Assemblée nationale 2014 inquiry report directly, so the 350,000 attribution to DARES rests on the IRES presentation (which cites Gubian et al 2004) and on secondary pages.
- No INSEE series on hourly productivity growth around 2000 was opened. The Prescott table gives French GDP per hour at 110 percent of the US level in 1993 to 96, before the 35-hour law.
- The legal entry date is given as 1 January 2000 (Aubry I) and 1 February 2000 (Eurofound, Aubry II). Both appear in sources. The writer should say "January to February 2000".

## Key question 2. Germany: IG Metall 35-hour week 1984 to 1995, works councils, Kurzarbeit 2009 and 2020

### Takeaway
West German metalworkers won a phased cut from 40 to 38.5 hours after a seven-week strike in 1984, to 37 hours in 1987 and to 35 hours from 1 October 1995. Hunt's evaluation finds each one-hour cut in standard hours cut total hours by 2 to 3 percent but raised employment only 0.3 to 0.7 percent, with the wage bill rising. Kurzarbeit peaked at 1.516 million workers in May 2009 (jobs saved estimates range from about 0.7 to 0.8 percent of employment to 400,000) and at 6.83 million in April 2020 (one estimate of 2.2 million jobs saved).

### Cited Findings
- 1984 strike: 57,500 workers from 23 companies struck in North Württemberg, North Baden and Hesse from mid-May 1984, employers answered with lockouts and the slogan "not a minute less than 40 hours". The settlement set a staged plan toward 35 hours without loss of pay. Plausible, IG Metall history page via search snippet. [IG Metall, Der Kampf um die 35-Stunden-Woche](https://www.igmetall.de/ueber-uns/geschichte/der-kampf-um-die-35-stunden-woche)
- The 1984 settlement gave a 38.5-hour week plus a 5.3 percent wage increase. Plausible, OCR of a contemporary October 1984 report. [FOSATU Worker News, October 1984](https://sahistory.org.za/sites/default/files/archive-files/FwOct84.1562.7624.000.033.Oct1984.42.pdf)
- 37-hour week introduced in 1987 after warning strikes. 35-hour week in force in the West German metal industry since 1 October 1995. In the east the 35-hour week has not been implemented across the board. Plausible, IG Metall pages via snippet. [IG Metall milestones](https://www.igmetall.de/ueber-uns/ig-metall--a-strong-community/what-we-have-already-achieved-our-milestones)
- Hunt (1999), "Has work-sharing worked in Germany?": starting in 1985 West German unions cut standard hours industry by industry. For 1984 to 1989 a one-hour fall in standard hours reduced total hours worked by 2 to 3 percent and raised employment by 0.3 to 0.7 percent. The mean standard hours decline implied at most 1.1 percent employment growth, "not enough" to bring German employment growth near the US rate. Workers as a group were better off because the wage bill rose. Results for 1990 to 94 were more pessimistic. Verified abstract. [Hunt, NBER w5724](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5724)
- Estevão and Sá cite Hunt as finding that the hours reductions decreased employment over 1984 to 94. Verified in the IZA PDF. [Estevão and Sá](https://docs.iza.org/dp2459.pdf)
- Kurzarbeit 2009: cyclical short-time workers peaked at 1.516 million in May 2009, averaged 1.089 million over 2009 across about 50,000 firms, and the average hours loss per short-time worker was 31 percent of normal hours. The IAB writes that the contribution to employment stabilisation in 2009 cannot be exactly quantified because it is unknown how many short-time workers would otherwise have been dismissed. Verified, downloaded IAB statement. [IAB Stellungnahme 2/2010](https://doku.iab.de/stellungnahme/2010/sn0210.pdf)
- Other reports give 1.44 million for May 2009 as the pre-pandemic record. Plausible, snippets. [Börsen-Zeitung](https://www.boersen-zeitung.de/konjunktur-politik/kurzarbeiterzahl-steigt-auf-rekordhoch)
- Jobs saved 2008 to 2009, as summarised by Cahuc in IZA World of Labor: Boeri and Brücker (2011) about 400,000 jobs, roughly 85 percent of the full-time equivalent of short-time work, 95 percent confidence interval 34,000 to 770,000, with a warning about selection bias. Hijzen and Venn (2011) found the largest effects in Germany and Japan where 0.7 to 0.8 percent of jobs were saved. More than 4 percent of the labour force in Germany, Japan and Italy was on short-time work in 2009. Hijzen and Martin (2013) estimate the net employment effect was negligible or possibly negative by end 2010. Benefits fall mainly on permanent workers. Verified. [Cahuc, IZA World of Labor](https://wol.iza.org/articles/short-time-work-compensations-and-employment/long)
- Kurzarbeit 2020: preliminary Federal Employment Agency data show 6.83 million employees received cyclical short-time benefit in April 2020. Notifications covered 10.66 million employees in March and April combined, 1.14 million in May, and 342,000 from 1 to 25 June. Unemployment in June 2020 was 2,853,000, a rate of 6.2 percent, up 637,000 on the year. Verified. [Bundesregierung, Arbeitsmarkt Juni 2020](https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/suche/arbeitsmarkt-juni-2020-1765548)
- ifo put April 2020 short-time work at just under 6 million people. Plausible, snippet. [Personalwirtschaft](https://www.personalwirtschaft.de/?p=98106)
- IMK with the universities of Kiel and Münster estimated that Kurzarbeit secured 2.2 million jobs at the height of the 2020 crisis, extrapolated from hours data of registered employees. Kurzarbeit supported six times as many jobs in 2020 as in 2008 to 2009. Unemployment rose from 2.3 to 2.7 million in 2020. In April 2020 the average single person on the scheme lost about a fifth of income. Verified (news report of the study). [IamExpat, 13 May 2021](https://www.iamexpat.de/career/employment-news/study-germanys-kurzarbeit-scheme-saved-22-million-jobs)
- Germany's OECD annual hours: 1985 1,666. 1990 1,573. 1995 1,531. 2000 1,466. 2008 to 2010 (the Kurzarbeit window): 2010 1,426. 2020 1,316. 2022 1,347. Verified, JILPT. The 2020 dip of 57 hours against 2019 (1,373) is the Kurzarbeit year.

### Inferences
- The German bargaining route cut standard hours by 5 hours over 11 years in metals. Hunt's numbers imply most of the saved time became leisure and higher hourly pay rather than jobs, because actual hours fell 2 to 3 percent per standard hour cut (close to full pass-through on a 40-hour base) while employment rose under 1 percent in total.
- Kurzarbeit is the clearest case in the whole study of saved hours being used to protect jobs. In 2009 the average participant lost 31 percent of normal hours. Even the sceptical estimate (0.7 to 0.8 percent of employment, roughly 280,000 to 320,000 jobs on a base of about 40 million) is large relative to the 2009 GDP fall of about 5 percent, and the 2020 scheme ran at four and a half times the 2009 headcount.
- The 2020 estimate of 2.2 million is an extrapolation from hours, not a counterfactual. The OECD-style counterfactual estimates for 2009 are far lower in proportion, so the writer should present 2.2 million as an upper bound.

## Key question 3. Nordics: Swedish and Danish bargaining, Sweden's six-hour day trial, Denmark's 37-hour norm

### Takeaway
Denmark cut the agreed week from 40 to 37 hours through collective bargaining in the late 1980s, phased in to 1990 or 1991 as part of a wage-restraint compromise, and 37 hours is still the norm with averaging periods now up to 12 months. Sweden's hours per worker rose from 1980 to 2000, the opposite of the rest of Europe. Gothenburg's six-hour day trial at the Svartedalen care home (2015 to 2017) needed 17 extra staff at about 12 million kronor, cut sick leave among trial staff while the control home's sick leave rose, and was not continued because it was judged too expensive to scale.

### Cited Findings
- Denmark: "In the late 1980s, working hours were reduced from 40 hours per week to 37 hours" through a compromise among social partners aimed at curbing wage growth. Working time is mainly set through collective agreements and bargaining decentralised to workplaces after 1990. Verified abstract. [Social Policy and Labor Studies, J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/spls/10/1/10_62/_article/-char/en)
- Eurofound's 1997 review puts the general 37-hour week's introduction in 1991. A Danish national analysis places it in 1990. The 1995 Industry Agreement extended the averaging period from six weeks to six months. Plausible, snippets, the Eurofound page returned 429 three times. [Eurofound 1997 annual review Denmark](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/1997-annual-review-denmark)
- Denmark OECD hours: 1985 1,527. 1990 1,441. 1995 1,419. 2000 1,466. 2010 1,422. 2022 1,394. Verified, JILPT. The 86-hour fall from 1985 to 1990 lines up with the phased move to 37 hours.
- Sweden OECD hours: 1985 1,401. 1990 1,422. 1995 1,481. 2000 1,485. 2010 1,483. 2022 1,440. Verified, JILPT. PWT: 1970 1,545, 1980 1,370, 2000 1,489. Verified, OWID.
- Norway OECD hours: 1985 1,532. 2000 1,448. 2022 1,425. Verified, JILPT.
- Svartedalen trial, Gothenburg: nurses returned to eight-hour shifts in February 2017 after almost two years on six-hour days with no pay cut. 17 extra staff were needed, at a cost of about 12 million kronor (about 1.25 million euro). Trial nurses took 4.7 percent fewer sick days over the experiment while the control group took 62.5 percent more. Lead researcher Bengt Lorentzon said staff were "less tired, less sick, had more energy coming home and more time to do activities." The project ended because funding ran out and it would be too expensive to implement across the whole municipality. Verified (Bloomberg piece reprinted). [Irish Times, April 2017](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/economy/how-the-six-hour-working-day-can-actually-save-money-1.3052533)
- Earlier interim results: about 10 percent lower sick leave, 77 percent of assistant nurses reporting good health versus 49 percent at a comparison home, 4.7 million kronor in reduced unemployment costs over the first 18 months, and a city estimate of a 22 percent cost increase of which about 10 percentage points were offset. Plausible, snippets from several outlets with differing numbers. [Positive News](https://positive.news/?p=25017) and [Macau Daily Times, January 2017](https://macaudailytimes.com.mo/sweden-six-hour-workday-runs-trouble-costly.html)
- Gothenburg had no plan to make the trial permanent or extend it to other facilities. Plausible, snippet. [Jakarta Post, 5 January 2017](https://www.thejakartapost.com/life/2017/01/05/swedish-six-hour-workday-runs-into-trouble-its-too-costly)
- Bick, Brüggemann and Fuchs-Schündeln: lower hours per person in Scandinavia relative to the US are driven by fewer weekly hours per employed, not by employment rates, and employment rates in Scandinavia predict higher hours there than in the US. Weeks worked (vacation) account for one third to one half of the Europe to US gap across regions. Verified, downloaded IZA PDF. [Bick et al, IZA DP 10179](https://docs.iza.org/dp10179.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Nordic route is bargained, not legislated, and the Danish hours series shows the cut arriving quickly (1985 to 1990) with no later rebound. The saved time in Denmark went to leisure and to wage restraint, which was the explicit trade.
- Sweden's upward series after 1980 says that Nordic "short hours" are mostly about long holidays and high part-time shares, not about a falling trend. Bick et al confirm that weeks worked, not weekly hours, carry much of the gap.
- The Svartedalen trial is a cost study, not an hours policy. A 25 percent cut in hours (8 to 6) required roughly a 25 percent rise in headcount, so productivity per hour did not rise enough to offset the cut, and the city would not fund it.

### Gaps
- No Swedish statute history (40-hour week in the early 1970s, five weeks of leave in 1978) could be verified because the search budget ran out. Not found.
- The final Svartedalen report by Bengt Lorentzon was not opened. Numbers come from Bloomberg's report of it. The "sick leave minus 10 percent" and "minus 4.7 percent" figures come from different stages of the trial and should not be presented as the same number.
- Statistics Sweden and Statistics Denmark pages were not opened.

## Key question 5. US: no federal hours change since 1938, overtime threshold history, and why hours flattened after 1980

### Takeaway
The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set a 44-hour week stepping down to 40 hours by 24 October 1940, with time and a half beyond that, and the 40-hour standard has not been changed since. The white-collar salary threshold that decides who gets overtime was raised in 1940, 1949, 1958, 1963, 1970 and 1975 ($155 a week), then not until 2004 ($455), then 2019 ($684). A 2016 rule ($913) and a 2024 rule ($844 then $1,128) were both struck down in court. The leading explanations for the post-1980 flattening are taxes (Prescott), unions and regulation plus a social multiplier (Alesina, Glaeser and Sacerdote), preferences for leisure financed by productivity (Blanchard), inequality and promotion incentives (Bell and Freeman), and the finding that US leisure did rise even as market hours per adult stayed flat (Aguiar and Hurst). New survey-based data (Bick et al) put the Europe to US hours gap at 19 percent and attribute a third to a half of it to vacation weeks.

### Cited Findings
Law and thresholds.
- FLSA signed 25 June 1938, in force 24 October 1938, set "the maximum workweek at 44 hours" and the minimum wage at 25 cents, and covered industries employing only about one fifth of the labour force. Verified. [US DOL, FLSA 1938 history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)
- The standard workweek was to phase down to 40 hours on 24 October 1940. Verified. [CRS RL32088](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32088.html)
- The intermediate 42-hour step in 1939 is widely stated but was not in any source I opened. Plausible.
- Salary thresholds for the executive exemption (per week): 1938 $30. 1949 $55. 1958 (effective 2 February 1959) $80. 1963 $100. 1970 $125. 1975 $155. The higher "upset" test was $100 (1949), $125 (1958), $150 (1963), $200 (1970), $250 (1975). The 1975 rates were called interim and stayed in place. Verified. [CRS RL32088](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32088.html)
- 2004: DOL set the threshold at $455 a week ($23,660 a year). Plausible, snippets from EPI and legal summaries. [EPI 2014](https://files.epi.org/2014/Overtime-Rules-03-13-2014.pdf)
- 2016 rule set $913 a week from 1 December 2016, enjoined 23 November 2016 and later held invalid. 2019 rule (27 September 2019) set $684 a week, about $35,568 a year, highly compensated threshold $107,432. Plausible, Wikipedia. [Wikipedia, FLSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Labor_Standards_Act_of_1938)
- 2024 rule raised the threshold to $844 on 1 July 2024 with $1,128 due 1 January 2025. The Eastern District of Texas vacated it nationwide on 15 November 2024, returning the threshold to $684 and $107,432. Plausible, several law firm summaries. [DWT, November 2024](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/employment-labor-and-benefits/2024/11/texas-court-overturns-2024-dol-overtime-rule) Verified for the court, the $684 reversion and the $107,432 figure. The $844 and $1,128 figures are Plausible.
- US OECD hours: 1985 1,882. 1990 1,878. 1995 1,886. 2000 1,880. 2005 1,842. 2010 1,810. 2019 1,817. 2020 1,789. 2022 1,804. Verified, JILPT. PWT: 1950 2,022. 1980 1,840. 2000 1,888. 2019 1,797. Verified, OWID.

Explanations.
- Prescott (2004). "Americans now work 50 percent more than do the Germans, French, and Italians. This was not the case in the early 1970s when the Western Europeans worked more than Americans." Effective marginal tax rate on labour income and actual weekly hours per person aged 15 to 64, 1993 to 96: Germany 0.59 and 19.3. France 0.59 and 17.5. Italy 0.64 and 16.5. Canada 0.52 and 22.9. UK 0.44 and 22.8. Japan 0.37 and 27.0. US 0.40 and 25.9. Predicted hours from the tax model: Germany 19.5, France 19.5, Italy 18.8, Canada 21.3, UK 22.8, Japan 29.0, US 24.6, average error 1.14 hours a week. 1970 to 74: Germany 0.52 and 24.6. France 0.49 and 24.4. Italy 0.41 and 19.2. Canada 0.44 and 22.2. UK 0.45 and 25.9. Japan 0.25 and 29.8. US 0.40 and 23.5. Prescott: "when European and U.S. tax rates were comparable, European and U.S. labor supplies were comparable." The preference parameter chosen was 1.54. Verified, downloaded NBER PDF. [Prescott, NBER w10316](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10316/w10316.pdf)
- Alesina, Glaeser and Sacerdote (2005). Americans average 25.1 working hours per person of working age per week, Germans 18.6. Americans work 46.2 weeks a year, the French 40. Labour market regulations pushed by unions in declining industries under the slogan "work less, work all" explain "the bulk of the difference between the U.S. and Europe." Taxes "can explain only a small amount of the differences in hours." Culture is rejected because Europeans "worked more than Americans as late as the 1960s." They propose "a social multiplier where the returns to leisure increase as more people are taking longer vacations." Verified abstract. [AGS, NBER w11278](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11278)
- Blanchard (2004). "Over the last thirty years, productivity growth has been much higher in Europe than in the United States." "Productivity levels are roughly similar in the European Union and in the United States today." "The main difference is that Europe has used some of the increase in productivity to increase leisure rather than income." Verified abstract. [Blanchard, NBER w10310](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10310)
- Bell and Freeman (2000, Labour Economics 2001). Workers "choose current hours of work to gain promotions and advance in the distribution of earnings." "Since US earnings are more unequally distributed than German earnings, the same extra work pays off more in the US." Hours are positively related to earnings inequality across occupations in both countries, and hours raise future wages and promotion prospects in longitudinal data. Verified abstract. [Bell and Freeman, NBER w8051](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8051)
- Aguiar and Hurst (2006, QJE 2007). Using five decades of time-use surveys, "a dramatic increase in leisure time lies behind the relatively stable number of market hours worked (per working-age adult) between 1965 and 2003." Leisure for men rose 6 to 8 hours a week (less market work), for women 4 to 8 hours (less home production). The rise equals roughly 5 to 10 extra weeks of vacation a year on a 40-hour week and is valued at 8 to 9 percent of 2003 US consumption. Less-educated adults gained the most leisure, a growing inequality in leisure that mirrors wage inequality. Verified abstract. [Aguiar and Hurst, NBER w12082](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12082)
- Bick, Brüggemann and Fuchs-Schündeln (2016 IZA DP, published 2019). Using national labour force surveys 1983 to 2011 for 18 European countries and the US: "Europeans work 19% fewer hours than US citizens." Differences in weeks worked and in educational composition "each account for one third to one half of this gap." Lower hours in Scandinavia and Western Europe come from lower weekly hours per employed, in Eastern and Southern Europe from lower employment rates. The four European regions have hours 16 to 19 percent below the US. Measured with LFS data, hours account for 52 percent of the Europe to US output per person gap, versus more than 100 percent when measured with national accounts data. Verified, downloaded PDF. [Bick et al, IZA DP 10179](https://docs.iza.org/dp10179.pdf)
- Hunt (1999) on Germany, for comparison: standard hours cuts raised employment only 0.3 to 0.7 percent per hour and at most 1.1 percent in total, "not enough" to bring German employment growth near the US rate. Verified abstract. [Hunt, NBER w5724](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5724)

### Inferences
- The US did not legislate shorter hours after 1940 and let the overtime salary threshold erode. The 1975 threshold of $155 a week was left for 29 years. The one effective US lever on hours, the overtime premium, therefore covered a shrinking share of salaried workers through the whole period when US hours stopped falling.
- The explanations are not exclusive and the numbers can be stacked. Prescott's tax model fits the 1993 to 96 cross-section within about one hour a week, but it needs the US tax rate to stay at 0.40 while European rates rose by 7 to 23 points, which is a fact about policy, not preferences. AGS's weeks-worked gap (46.2 versus 40) and Bick et al's finding that vacation weeks explain a third to a half of the hours gap point the same way: much of the divergence is statutory and bargained holiday, not weekly hours. Bell and Freeman add why Americans did not bargain for it, the payoff to long hours is higher where pay is more unequal.
- Aguiar and Hurst show that US market hours per working-age adult were flat 1965 to 2003 while leisure rose, so the US "flat hours" story is about hours per worker and rising female employment, not about Americans having no more free time.

### Gaps
- No BLS or FRED series on average weekly hours or the share of salaried workers under the threshold was opened. Not found.
- The 2004 $455 and 2024 $844 and $1,128 figures were seen only in secondary summaries, not in the Federal Register.
- I did not open the published Economic Policy or AER versions of the papers, only the NBER and IZA working paper pages.

## Key question 6. Japan: 1987 Labour Standards Act revision, 2018 Work Style Reform, karoshi line

### Takeaway
Japan's 1947 Act set a 48-hour week. The 1987 revision cut the standard to 40 hours, implemented from 1988 with exemptions, fully effective by 1994 for most and 1997 for all. The 2018 Work Style Reform Law (passed 29 June 2018, in force April 2019 for large firms and April 2020 for small ones) put the first legal cap on overtime, 45 hours a month and 360 a year, with a special ceiling of 720 hours a year, under 100 hours in any month and an average under 80 hours over two to six months. The 80-hour figure is the health ministry's karoshi risk line. Annual hours fell from 2,121 (1980) to 1,821 (2000) and 1,607 (2022).

## Key question 7. Korea: 2004 five-day week, 2018 52-hour cap, 2023 69-hour proposal and reversal

### Takeaway
Korea cut the statutory week from 44 to 40 hours from July 2004, phased by firm size through 2011. ## Key question 9. Where laws cut hours, did productivity per hour rise enough to keep output flat?

### Takeaway
No, not fully, in any case with firm-level numbers. In France the 10.2 percent hours cut cost 3.7 percent of TFP, so output per hour rose but firms also hired, and the state paid for it through payroll relief. In Germany each one-hour cut in standard hours cut total hours 2 to 3 percent and raised employment under 1 percent, with the wage bill rising. In Sweden's six-hour trial a 25 percent hours cut needed roughly 25 percent more staff. At the macro level, Europe's output per hour caught up with and in France's case passed the US level by the mid-1990s (France 110 percent of US GDP per hour, Germany 99 percent), but GDP per person stayed near 70 to 75 percent of the US because hours per person were 25 to 32 percent lower.

### Cited Findings
- France, Crépon, Leclair and Roux (2004): hours down 10.2 percent, TFP down 3.7 percent, employment up 9.9 percent relative to 39-hour firms, productivity losses "smaller than the effects of wage moderation and reductions in charges." Verified. [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/prs/ecstat/estat_0336-1454_2004_num_376_1_7234.html)
- France, DARES via IRES: 350,000 jobs over 1996 to 2002, "a roughly neutral cost for firms" through wage moderation, productivity and aid. Verified. [Lerais, IRES 2014](https://ires.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Effets_des_35_heures_octobre_2014_final.pdf)
- France, Abowd's comment: national productivity per hour statistics confirm the increase in productivity and the non-increase in employment, and firms that went to 35 hours, in particular low-productivity firms, benefited from payroll subsidies so employment was redirected toward low-productivity firms in the short run. Verified. [Abowd, NBER c8248](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8248/c8248.pdf)
- Germany, Hunt (1999): one-hour standard hours cut, total hours down 2 to 3 percent, employment up 0.3 to 0.7 percent, wage bill up. Verified. [Hunt, NBER w5724](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5724)
- Sweden, Svartedalen: 17 extra staff for about 12 million kronor to cover the 8 to 6 hour cut. Verified. [Irish Times, April 2017](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/economy/how-the-six-hour-working-day-can-actually-save-money-1.3052533)
- Macro, Prescott Table 1 (1993 to 96, US = 100): GDP per person 15 to 64, Germany 74, France 74, UK 67, Japan 78. Hours per person, Germany 75, France 68, UK 88, Japan 104. GDP per hour, Germany 99, France 110, UK 76, Japan 74. "U.S. per capita output is about 40 percent higher than in the European countries with most of the differences in output accounted for by differences in hours worked per person and not by differences in productivity." In 1970 to 74 the picture was reversed, GDP per hour was 72 (Germany), 74 (France), 62 (UK), 49 (Japan) while hours were 105, 105, 110 and 127. Verified. [Prescott, NBER w10316](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10316/w10316.pdf)
- Macro, Blanchard (2004): European productivity growth "much higher" than the US over thirty years, levels "roughly similar" by 2004, Europe used part of the gain to buy leisure. Verified abstract. [Blanchard, NBER w10310](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10310)
- Macro, Bick et al: with LFS data, hours differences account for 52 percent of the Europe to US GDP per person gap, versus over 100 percent with national accounts hours. Verified. [Bick et al](https://docs.iza.org/dp10179.pdf)
- Korea, Carcillo et al: the 2018 cap reduced over-52-hour work by 1.3 percentage points and moved 4.7 points of workers into 41 to 52 hours, no output or productivity estimate. Verified. [Carcillo et al](https://docs.iza.org/dp16023.pdf)

### Inferences
- A rough rule from the French and German evidence: a 10 percent cut in standard hours produces roughly a 6 to 7 percent rise in output per hour at the firms that cut, a 1 to 3 percent rise in jobs, and the remainder is lost output or is paid for by the state and by wage restraint. Care work (the Swedish trial) shows no productivity offset at all because output is hours of attendance.
- At the national level the US to Europe GDP per person gap did not close between 1970 and 1996 even as Europe's productivity per hour caught up, which is exactly the pattern expected if Europe converted productivity gains into hours cuts rather than output.
