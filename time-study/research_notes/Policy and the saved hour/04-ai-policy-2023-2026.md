Scope: US (federal and states), UK, EU and member states (France, Germany, Spain, Portugal, Belgium, Nordics), Japan, Korea, China, plus OECD, ILO, IMF and WEF. Research run 1 and 2 November 2026.

Verified means I opened the cited source and saw the figure there. Plausible means the figure comes only from a secondary report or a search summary and I could not open the original. Not found means I looked and could not find it. Where an opened source is itself secondary (a law firm note, a newspaper), I say so in the entry so the writer can judge weight. ## Key question 1. Government-run or government-backed shorter-week trials and their measured results

### Takeaway
Every government-backed shorter-week trial that reported results between 2021 and 2026 found output held or improved and sickness or stress fell, yet no national government has legislated a shorter standard week on the back of them. Scotland ruled out a 32-hour week in August 2025 after a positive pilot, Spain's Congress rejected a 37.5-hour week in September 2025, and Belgium's 2022 right to a compressed four-day week is used by 0.8 percent of full-time workers. The clearest "made permanent" cases are a UK district council (July 2025) and seasonal Dubai government hours (2024 to 2026). Korea's Gyeonggi province pilot (March 2026 results) is the newest measured case and the only one that reports a productivity percentage.

### Cited Findings

#### Iceland, 2015 to 2019 trials, 2021 report, 2024 update. Status: In force through collective agreements (2019 to 2021)
- Two large public-sector trials ran from 2015 to 2019 covering 2,500 workers, over 1 percent of Iceland's working population. Workers moved to 35 to 36 hours a week with pay unchanged. Productivity and service provision "remained the same or improved" in most trial workplaces. Wellbeing rose on perceived stress, burnout, health and work-life balance. The trials were revenue neutral for Reykjavik City Council and the national government. Report published 4 July 2021 by Alda and Autonomy. (Iceland, 2015 to 2021, Verified, primary.) [Alda](https://en.alda.is/2021/07/04/going-public-icelands-journey-to-a-shorter-working-week/)
- The 86 percent figure: "since completion, 86% of the country's workforce are now working shorter hours or gaining the right to shorten their hours." This is a share of the whole workforce covered by contracts negotiated 2019 to 2021, not a share that actually cut hours. (Iceland, 2021, Verified.) [Alda](https://en.alda.is/2021/07/04/going-public-icelands-journey-to-a-shorter-working-week/)
- Baseline of 40 hours before the cut is reported in news coverage but is not stated on the Alda page I opened. (Iceland, Plausible.) [Scripps via KSHB](https://www.kshb.com/news/national/icelandic-trials-studying-shorter-workweeks-hailed-as-overwhelming-successes)
- October 2024 update by Autonomy and Alda: between 2020 and 2022, 51 percent of Icelandic workers accepted shorter hours, including a four-day week, and researchers say the share is "likely higher" now. Iceland's economy grew 5 percent in 2023 (IMF), second only to Malta among rich European economies, with unemployment 3.4 percent in 2023 and 3.8 percent forecast for 2024 and 2025. No numeric productivity figure is given. (Iceland, 2024, Verified in a CNN report syndicated by ABC7, secondary.) [ABC7 / CNN, 26 Oct 2024](https://abc7news.com/post/iceland-embraced-shorter-work-week-heres-how-turned/15470873/)
- Caveat: both reports are co-authored by shorter-week advocates (Alda, Autonomy).

#### United Kingdom, 2022 national pilot and 2024 follow-up. Status: Trial (private sector, completed Dec 2022), continuation measured Feb 2024
- Pilot ran June to December 2022 with 61 organisations and around 2,900 employees, "a 20% reduction in working hours for all staff for six months" with "no fall in wages." Results published February 2023: 92 percent of companies (56 of 61) intended to continue, 18 made it permanent at that point, revenue rose 1.4 percent on average across the 23 organisations that supplied data, sick days fell 65 percent and staff leaving fell 57 percent versus the same period a year earlier, 71 percent of employees self-reported lower burnout and 39 percent felt less stressed. (UK, 2022 to 2023, Verified, University of Cambridge page.) [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/fourdayweek)
- One year on (Autonomy report "Making It Stick", February 2024): 89 percent (54 of 61) still had the four-day week in place and 31 had made it permanent (about 51 percent, my calculation). (UK, 2024, Verified in HRD Connect, secondary.) [HRD Connect, 26 Feb 2024](https://www.hrdconnect.com/2024/02/26/one-year-on-uks-4-day-work-week-trial-has-been-a-success/)
- This pilot was run by 4 Day Week Global, Autonomy and the 4 Day Week Campaign with university researchers. It was not government run or government funded.

#### United Kingdom, South Cambridgeshire District Council. Status: In force, made permanent July 2025 (first UK council)
- A three-month trial began at the start of 2023 and was extended by one year. Staff do 100 percent of their work in "around 80 per cent of their contracted hours" with no pay reduction. Independent analysis by the Universities of Salford, Bradford and Cambridge (published 4 July 2025, covering 27 months): 21 of 24 monitored services improved or stayed the same, the number of workers leaving fell by more than 40 percent, job applications rose by more than 120 percent, and the council reports an ongoing yearly saving of £399,263, mainly from filling vacancies permanently instead of using agency staff. Sick-day figures were not reported. (UK, 2023 to 2025, Verified, Salford news and Local Government Lawyer.) [University of Salford, 4 Jul 2025](https://www.salford.ac.uk/news/reports-showcase-four-day-week-performance), [Local Government Lawyer, 21 Jul 2025](https://localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/employment/395-employment-news/61647-council-becomes-first-to-permanently-adopt-four-day-work-week)
- The Conservative government issued Best Value Notices in November 2023 and May 2024 pressing the council to stop. In November 2024 the Labour government said it would not reissue one, and MHCLG stated that "local authorities are independent employers." (UK, 2023 to 2024, Verified.) [Local Government Lawyer](https://localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/employment/395-employment-news/61647-council-becomes-first-to-permanently-adopt-four-day-work-week), [Left Foot Forward, Nov 2024](https://leftfootforward.org/2024/11/angela-rayner-opens-up-possibility-of-four-day-working-week-in-local-councils)
- The exact date of the council vote (reported as 17 July 2025 in search summaries) was not confirmed in the pages I opened. (Plausible.)

#### Scotland, public sector pilot. Status: Trial completed (Oct 2023 to 2025), wider rollout Dropped on 28 August 2025
- Two bodies, Accountant in Bankruptcy (AiB) and South of Scotland Enterprise (SOSE), trialled a "32-hour working week with no loss of pay." The pilot began on 2 October 2023 and the government page says it runs until 3 November 2025. Autonomy was the independent evaluator. Government page: "service delivery was not affected" and "both organisations maintained their key performance targets." Any changes must work within the existing 35-hour framework. (Scotland, 2023 to 2025, Verified, primary.) [Scottish Government](https://www.gov.scot/policies/public-sector-pay/four-day-working-week/)
- Autonomy report of 27 August 2025: combined workforce 259 staff, AiB moved from 37 hours and SOSE from 35 hours. AiB's three key processes stayed stable against the prior year and target times. SOSE's client-response KPIs stayed stable. SOSE sick days for psychological reasons fell about 25.7 percent. AiB self-assessed mental health improved 18.4 percent, work stress fell 18.4 percent, satisfaction with time rose 46.8 percent. SOSE staff "very satisfied" with work-life balance rose from 4 percent to 84 percent nine months in, 83 percent reported a positive impact on stress and 98 percent on motivation and morale. SOSE got a six-month extension and AiB two months. Turnover was not reported. (Scotland, 2025, Verified.) [Autonomy, 27 Aug 2025](https://autonomy.work/2025/08/27/introducing-a-shorter-working-week-in-scotlands-public-sector/)
- Civil Service World, 28 August 2025: public finance minister Ivan McKee said the Scottish Government and its agencies "will not be moving to a 32-hour week." SOSE sick days for psychological reasons fell 26 percent, from 357 to 265 days, and total sickness instances fell from 122 to 119. The evaluation found "a significant increase in productivity, with work performance remaining stable." (Scotland, 2025, Verified, secondary.) [Civil Service World, 28 Aug 2025](https://www.civilserviceworld.com/news/article/scottish-government-four-day-week-pilot-assessment-productivity-wellbeing-benefits)

#### Spain, 37.5-hour statutory week bill. Status: Dropped on 10 September 2025 (government says it will return)
- Congress rejected the government's bill to cut the legal working week from 40 to 37.5 hours by 178 votes to 170 on 10 September 2025, after PP, Vox and Junts backed amendments to the whole text. The government said more than 12 million people would have benefited, mainly private-sector workers in agriculture, retail and hospitality, since public servants and most large-company staff already work 37.5 hours. Labour minister Yolanda Díaz vowed to push for a shorter week "as soon as possible." (Spain, 2025, Verified, The Local and Catalan News.) [The Local, 11 Sep 2025](https://www.thelocal.es/20250911/spains-congress-rejects-law-to-shorten-work-week), [Catalan News, 10 Sep 2025](https://www.catalannews.com/politics/item/congress-vote-working-week-375-hours-10-september-2025)
- A 2026 update says there is no parliamentary timetable for reviving the bill and the statutory maximum remains 40 hours. (Spain, 2026, Plausible.) [Remote Work Europe](https://remoteworkeurope.eu/news/2026/spain-working-hours-bill-stalled)

#### Portugal, government-backed pilot. Status: Trial completed (June to December 2023), final report July 2024, no legislation
- Government statement, 12 December 2023: 41 companies and more than 1,000 workers took part. Weekly hours fell from 39.3 to 34, a 13.7 percent cut. 95 percent of companies rated the experiment positively. Anxiety fell 21 percent, fatigue 23 percent, insomnia or sleep disturbance 19 percent, and work-related exhaustion 19 percent. The pilot was "voluntary and reversible." Pay and productivity were not stated on this page. (Portugal, 2023, Verified, primary.) [Portuguese Government, 12 Dec 2023](https://portugal.gov.pt/en/gc23/communication/news/95-of-companies-assess-positively-the-four-day-week-experiment)
- Final report, 18 July 2024 (Henley Business School, Birkbeck and 4 Day Week Global, coordinated by Rita Fontinha and Pedro Gomes): 21 companies coordinated a six-month trial from June 2023. Employee hours including overtime fell from 41.6 to 36.5 a week (12 percent). 80 percent of managers rated the test financially neutral. Workers valued the benefit at 28 percent of salary. Workers struggling with work-life balance fell from 50 percent to 16 percent. Only four of the 41 companies returned to five days and 93 percent of workers wanted to continue. Mental and physical health improved against a control group. (Portugal, 2024, Verified.) [Henley, 18 Jul 2024](https://www.henley.ac.uk/news/2024/results-from-portugals-government-backed-4-day-week-trial-released)
- Caveat from a Portuguese business outlet: only 21 of about 100 initially interested companies saw the six-month test through, so headline results are from completers. Gomes also said financial data was not collected. (Portugal, 2024, Plausible.) [Essential Business](https://www.essential-business.pt/2024/06/24/four-day-working-week-pilot-has-mixed-results/)

#### Belgium, Labour Deal right to a compressed four-day week. Status: In force since 20 November 2022
- Employees may work full-time hours over four days with no pay reduction. The worker makes a written request, the employer may accept or refuse but must give reasons if refusing, arrangements run for a maximum of six months and can be renewed, and the employee is protected against dismissal and cannot do voluntary overtime on the day off. Entry into force 20 November 2022. (Belgium, 2022, Verified, CMS law firm note.) [CMS](https://cms.law/en/bel/legal-updates/Belgium-implements-important-changes-to-employment-law-in-2022)
- Uptake (Acerta Consult survey of 224,000 full-time workers at 20,000 private employers, reported 22 May 2025): "just 0.8% of Belgian employees use this regime," 2.85 percent of companies have at least one such worker, rising to 39.2 percent of companies with more than 500 staff and 19.3 percent of those with 200 to 500. The number is rising but "their share remains low." (Belgium, 2025, Verified, Brussels Times.) [Brussels Times, 22 May 2025](http://www.brusselstimes.com/belgium/1591779/hardly-any-enthusiasm-for-four-day-working-week-in-belgian-companies)
- Earlier readings: about 0.5 percent (5 in 1,000) three months after the law, and 0.73 percent in a Securex poll. (Belgium, 2023 to 2024, Plausible.) [Brussels Times](https://www.brusselstimes.com/belgium/1275369/the-work-life-balance-belgium-wants-four-day-work-week-not-catching-on)
- Hours were not cut. This is compression of a 38 to 40 hour week into four days.

#### Germany, 2024 pilot (not government run). Status: Trial completed (Feb to Jul 2024), two-year follow-up Feb 2026
- University of Münster press release, 18 October 2024: official start 1 February 2024 for six months, 45 organisations, two dropped out (large companies citing economic challenges or lack of internal support), 41 had completed by October. 85 percent granted one fixed or flexible day off a week and 60 percent applied the model to the whole workforce. Pay unchanged. Employees slept 38 minutes longer a week than a control group, measured daily stress was significantly lower, turnover and profit "rose slightly, but not significantly" versus the prior year, and more than 70 percent of organisations wanted to continue. Lead: Prof Julia Backmann. (Germany, 2024, Verified, primary.) [idw, 18 Oct 2024](https://idw-online.de/en/news841503)
- Two-year follow-up (February 2026): 70 percent of participating organisations continue with some form of reduced working time and a further 22 percent adapted their model. (Germany, 2026, Verified, 4 Day Week Global page.) [4 Day Week Global](https://4dayweek.com/germany-2024-pilot-results)
- A search summary reports 20 percent of remaining companies returned to five days. That conflicts with the 70 percent continuation figure and may refer to a different cut of the sample. (Germany, 2024, Plausible.) [People Matters](https://www.peoplematters.in/article/employee-relations/germanys-4-day-week-trial-offers-interesting-results-43132)
- The German federal government's own direction is the opposite: a move from daily to weekly maximum hours (see key question 4).

#### South Korea, Gyeonggi Province 4.5-day week pilot. Status: Trial (June 2025 to 2027), first results March 2026

### Inferences
- Across Iceland, the UK, Portugal, Scotland, South Cambridgeshire, Germany and Gyeonggi, the measured pattern is the same: output stable or slightly up, sickness and turnover down, wellbeing up. The only percentage productivity gain reported by a government-linked evaluator is Gyeonggi's 2.1 percent (2026), and the only hard money figure is South Cambridgeshire's £399,263 a year saved on agency staff.
- Governments treat these results as grounds for flexibility rather than for cutting hours. Scotland, France, Tokyo, Dubai and Belgium all chose compression or optional schemes that leave contracted hours unchanged. Where hours were actually cut (Iceland, Scotland pilot, South Cambridgeshire, Gyeonggi, Portugal), the cut was between 8 and 20 percent.
- Voluntary rights without pay or hours change get low uptake (Belgium 0.8 percent). Pilots that pay a subsidy (Gyeonggi) or keep pay while cutting hours (Portugal, Scotland) get high continuation rates.
- None of these trials involved AI or measured whether automation created the slack. The shorter-week evidence base and the AI productivity evidence base do not yet overlap in any government document found.

## Key question 2. Right to disconnect laws

### Takeaway
France (2017), Belgium (2022 to 2023), Portugal (2022) and Australia (2024 to 2025) have rights to disconnect in force, and all put the duty on process (negotiate, adopt a policy, refrain from contact) rather than on measured hours. The UK dropped its promised "right to switch off" in March 2025 and the Employment Rights Act 2025 contains none. California's AB 2751 died in committee in May 2024. The EU is in a second-stage social partner consultation (July to October 2025) with no proposal yet. Korea plans after-hours contact rules in 2026. No jurisdiction has published measured effects on working hours.

### Cited Findings

#### France. Status: In force since 1 January 2017
- The El Khomri law of 8 August 2016, effective 1 January 2017, created the right to disconnect in Article L.2242-8 of the Labour Code. It "generally applies to companies with at least 50 employees," which must negotiate with employee representatives. If no agreement is reached, the employer "must draw up a code of conduct or policies setting forth the terms and conditions." "There is no penalty for non-compliance," though breaches can feed burn-out or harassment claims. Article L.3121-64 also requires employers of any size to address disconnection for staff on annualised days. (France, 2017, Verified, Littler, secondary.) [Littler](https://www.littler.com/publication-press/publication/sorting-out-truth-about-right-disconnect-france)
- No empirical evaluation of effects on hours or contact was found (Not found).

#### Belgium. Status: In force, public sector 1 February 2022, private sector policy deadline 1 January 2023
- The 2022 Labour Deal (in force 20 November 2022) requires private-sector companies with at least 20 employees to adopt a company policy on the right to disconnect "by 1 January 2023 at the latest," through a collective agreement or work rules. (Belgium, 2022 to 2023, Verified, CMS.) [CMS](https://cms.law/en/bel/legal-updates/Belgium-implements-important-changes-to-employment-law-in-2022)
- Public-sector workers had the right from 1 February 2022. No specific penalty is set for non-compliance. (Belgium, 2022, Plausible, DLA Piper and Ius Laboris summaries.) [DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/belgium-new-labour-deal-overview-of-the-key-measures), [JD Supra / Ius Laboris](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/the-right-to-disconnect-which-countries-8901604)

#### Portugal. Status: In force since 1 January 2022
- Under Law 83/2021 (approved November 2021), effective 1 January 2022, employers "must refrain from contacting employees during their rest periods," defined as any time that is not working time, except in force majeure, and must not treat workers less favourably for exercising the right. Breaches carry administrative fines. Reported cases have been folded into broader moral harassment claims rather than treated as standalone breaches. Mayer Brown says the right applies to all employees and employers (public servants fall outside the telework regime but keep the general right to rest). (Portugal, 2022 to 2025, Verified, Mayer Brown March 2025, secondary.) [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/portugal-the-right-to-disconnect-qa)
- Secondary reports put the maximum fine at about €9,690 per infraction and say companies under ten employees are excluded. Mayer Brown's "all employers" statement conflicts with the exclusion claim. (Portugal, 2021 to 2022, Plausible, conflicting.) [Taurillon](https://www.taurillon.org/no-more-boss-after-working-hours-portugal-s-new-right-to-disconnect-law?lang=fr), [GPA](https://gpa.net/blogs/emea-1/portugal-big step-remote-work-law-approved)

#### Australia. Status: In force, 26 August 2024 for employers with 15 or more staff, 26 August 2025 for small business
- The right sits in the Fair Work Act 2009 as amended by the Closing Loopholes No. 2 Act 2024. "Small business employers will follow suit on 26 August 2025." Disputes go first to the workplace, then to the Fair Work Commission, which as of August 2025 had not issued formal guidelines or jurisprudence. First Federal Court case: Martin v Cairns Rudolf Steiner School Ltd (QUD148/2025), a teacher alleging dismissal partly for refusing to respond during school holidays, seeking about A$800,000, listed for mediation 11 August 2025. AHRI Quarterly Australian Work Outlook (June 2025): 29 percent of employers changed policy or practice, 27 percent saw a positive impact, 58 percent noticed increased productivity and engagement, 37 percent saw reduced stress, 39 percent saw improved work-life balance. (Australia, 2024 to 2025, Verified, HWL Ebsworth 25 Aug 2025, secondary.) [HWL Ebsworth](https://hwlebsworth.com.au/the-right-to-disconnect-12-months-on)
- First substantive rulings (September 2026 commentary) were in the South Australian Employment Tribunal (Kalleske v Mcguire [2026] SAET 106, Elliot v Mcguire [2026] SAET 107), finding no breach by a hairdressing salon. (Australia, 2026, Plausible.) [Cooper Grace Ward](https://cgw.com.au/?p=43844)
- No data on measured changes in after-hours contact volumes or hours was found (Not found).

#### United Kingdom. Status: Dropped in March 2025. Employment Rights Act 2025 contains no right to switch off
- Reports in March 2025, citing The Times, said ministers had discarded the "right to switch off" from the Employment Rights Bill to lessen the burden on business after the October 2024 Budget. ### Inferences
- Every enacted right to disconnect is a process right. None sets a cap on contact or measures hours. Enforcement evidence is thin everywhere: France has no penalty, Belgium has none specified, Portugal's cases are absorbed into harassment claims, and Australia had one Federal Court claim and two tribunal rulings in two years.
- The two large Anglophone economies that considered the right (UK, California) both dropped it under business pressure in 2024 to 2025, while Australia kept it and extended it to small business on schedule.
- The only quantified effect found is an employer survey (AHRI, Australia, June 2025), which is perception data. No government has measured whether these laws changed working time.

## Key question 3. AI and labour rules

### Takeaway
By November 2026 the binding AI-at-work rules are: NYC Local Law 144 (in force July 2023, judged "ineffective" by the New York State Comptroller in December 2025), Illinois HB 3773 (in force 1 January 2026), Korea's AI Basic Act (in force 22 January 2026 with fines deferred at least a year), China's algorithm and generative AI measures (2022, 2023) and Japan's penalty-free AI Promotion Act (June 2025). The EU AI Act's employment high-risk duties were pushed from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus (in force 27 July 2026). Colorado repealed its AI Act before it took effect and replaced it with a disclosure law effective 1 January 2027. California's CPPA automated decision rules bite for employment from 1 January 2027. The UK Employment Rights Act 2025 has no AI provisions, but a workplace monitoring consultation closed 30 September 2026. None of these rules addresses working hours.

## Key question 4. Official positions on AI and hours

### Takeaway
Only two political actors explicitly tie AI productivity to shorter hours: the Sanders and Takano "Thirty-Two Hour Workweek Act" (reintroduced 8 September 2026, cites AI) and South Korea's government, which is funding voluntary 4.5-day pilots and targeting annual hours in the 1,700 range by 2030. Germany's government is moving the other way, towards weekly rather than daily maximums. The UK says a four-day week is "not government policy" but will not stop councils. The international bodies (IMF, ILO, OECD, WEF) quantify exposure (40 percent of global jobs, one in four workers, 170 million created versus 92 million displaced) and in June 2026 the OECD found "no signs of widespread labour displacement" from AI, but none of them recommends shorter hours as a response.

### Inferences
- The international institutions converge on "transformation not replacement" (ILO 2025), "no widespread displacement yet" (OECD June 2026) and "40 percent exposed" (IMF 2024, 2026). None translates productivity gains into a hours dividend. The hours question is being asked by legislators (Sanders, Takano), one national government (Korea) and unions (Sweden's LO), not by the IMF, ILO, OECD or WEF.
- Governments facing labour shortages (Japan, Germany, Korea) split on direction: Japan and Germany want more labour supply or longer permissible days, while Korea wants fewer hours with subsidies. Korea's own employers cite 77 percent of OECD productivity as the reason not to cut.
- Where a left-of-centre government tried to legislate hours (Spain 2025), it lost in parliament. Where it ran the shorter week as an experiment (Scotland, Portugal, Gyeonggi), results were positive but did not lead to national law. This suggests the saved hour is politically easier to grant locally and voluntarily than to legislate.

## Key question 5. Governments measuring how AI time savings were used in their own workforces

### Takeaway
Five governments have measured AI time savings in their own staff. Self-reported savings range from 13 minutes a week (Australian Treasury break-even estimate, 2024) to 26 minutes a day (UK GDS, 20,000 staff, 2024) to 81 minutes per task (Singapore Pair dashboard, Q2 2026). Controlled or compared designs give smaller numbers: 19 minutes a day (UK DWP, 2026) and no evidence of productivity gain (UK DBT, 2025, Australian Treasury, 2025). Only one evaluation asked what happened to the saved time, and the UK GDS answered that "it was not possible to identify how time saved was spent." None reports hours worked falling.

### Inferences
- The only government that explicitly tried to find out where the saved hour went (UK GDS) could not, and the only controlled designs (DBT, DWP, Treasury) found either no productivity effect or a smaller saving than the self-reported headline. The honest reading is that governments can measure minutes saved per task but not whether those minutes became output, slack or shorter days.
- Where redirection is described at all, it goes to "other tasks," quality and planning (DWP) or "higher-value work" (DTA, Plausible), never to fewer hours. No government has linked its own AI savings to any change in staff hours, overtime or leave.
- Break-even thinking dominates: Australian Treasury frames value as 13 minutes a week redirected to justify a licence. That framing treats the saved hour as employer property by default.
- Adoption, not capability, is the binding constraint in every trial: a third daily use (Australia), 65 percent never used (GSA), 1.14 actions a day (DBT).

### Gaps
- No government measured changes in hours worked, overtime or leave taken after AI deployment (Not found anywhere).
- UK GDS: how saved time was used (explicitly Not found by the report itself).
- Australia DTA: the full evaluation PDF (timed out), so the 40 percent redirection figure stays Plausible.
- GSA: method behind the 400,000 and 500,000 hour claims (Not found).
- Singapore: method behind the 81 minutes per task estimate and the earlier 49 percent figure (Not found).
- Japan, Korea, China, EU institutions: any workforce AI time-savings evaluation (Not found).
