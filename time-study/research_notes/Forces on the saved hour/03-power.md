# Power force on time use: who owns the gains from AI, algorithmic management, platform work, offshoring, and care migration

Research date: 1 November 2026. Scope: US, UK, EU (France, Germany, Nordics), Japan, Korea, China. Horizon: today to 2050 and beyond.

How to read the labels. Verified means I opened the source and read the number there. Plausible means the number comes from a secondary report or a search summary of the source and I could not open the primary page (several OECD, WTO news and Goldman Sachs pages returned 403 or 429 errors). Not found means I searched and found no usable figure. Every future number is marked Projection (a model's central path) or Scenario (one of several model cases) and is never a fact.

Plain English summary for the tile. Workers in the United States are taking the smallest share of national output on record. Models of AI from the IMF and MIT say the gap between capital income and labour income will widen further. Managers increasingly use software to set the pace, allocate tasks and fix hours, and the evidence from Amazon and from gig riders shows that this raises intensity inside paid hours and adds unpaid waiting time outside them. Platform work is growing in every country studied. AI makes more office work tradeable across borders. Rich countries are shutting the migration routes that staffed care while their populations age, which pushes care back onto unpaid family time. The net push is toward more paid or paid-equivalent hours for people without capital, and fewer free hours, growing from weak now to strong around 2035 to 2041, with a wide range after 2050 that depends on who ends up owning the AI capital.

## Key question 1. Labour share of income today, its trend since 1980, projections under AI, and who is capturing AI gains so far

### Takeaway
The US labour share hit a record low of 52.8 percent in Q2 2026 against a post-war norm of 60 to 66 percent, while the UK and the euro area have held flat or risen slightly since the mid 1990s. The IMF's 2024 model has the labour share falling a further 5.5 percentage points after AI is introduced, Acemoglu's 2024 model says AI will widen the gap between capital and labour income, and the early gains are visible in the seven largest US tech firms, which earn over a quarter of S&P 500 profits and spent close to 400 billion dollars on capital in 2025.

### Inferences
- The US is the outlier where the labour share fell and kept falling. Europe stabilised after 2000 and the UK rose because of pension contributions rather than pay. AI gains captured by US firms therefore land in an economy where workers already take a record-low share, which is where the "who owns the gains" question bites hardest.
- Every macro model opened here (IMF 2024, Acemoglu 2024, IMF 2025) points the same way on distribution: capital income up relative to labour income. They differ only on how big the productivity prize is (0.5 percent TFP to 13 percent GDP).
- A lower labour share weakens the historical mechanism by which productivity gains became shorter hours. If workers do not receive the gains as income, they have no slack to buy free time with.
- The early labour market signal is concentrated on entry-level knowledge workers (19 percent gap in the US), which cuts paid hours for that group while leaving total hours for experienced workers unchanged.

### Gaps
- No published labour share projection to 2050 was found from the IMF, OECD, BLS or ONS. The IMF 5.5 point figure is a model shift with no calendar date attached.
- OECD Employment Outlook 2025 is about ageing and has no AI chapter. The AI findings are in the 2023 edition and in a June 2026 Economic Outlook that I could not open.
- Country labour shares for the Nordics other than Denmark and Sweden, and for Japan and Korea in 2024 or 2025, were not found in a single verified source.
- Eurofound or ECB figures for the euro area labour share in 2025 under the ECB's own definition were not found. The Eurostat compensation share is the nearest verified measure.

## Key question 2. Algorithmic management: how many workers, and what it does to pace, breaks and hours

### Takeaway
About one in five EU employees is now monitored by software, one in ten has their working hours set by a computer, and those workers report more job strain. Amazon's own OSHA filings show a serious injury rate of 5.9 per 100 workers in 2024, almost double other warehouses, and UK gig riders report about ten unpaid hours a week waiting for work. The EU Platform Work Directive, in force since 1 December 2024 with a 2 December 2026 transposition deadline, is the first binding law on algorithmic management.

### Cited Findings

Prevalence
- Verified. "Nearly 20 % of EU employees report being monitored by a computer programme, 18 % report being allocated their tasks by a computer and 10 % say that it determines their working hours." Based on the European Working Conditions Survey 2024. EU, 2024. Source: Eurofound press release, 29 October 2026, [link](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/eurofound/pressreleases/big-brother-reborn-one-in-five-european-employees-digitally-monitored-as-algorithmic-management-takes-hold-3475189)
- Verified. Country spread in EWCS 2024: Denmark highest overall intensity, with 38 percent of employees having tasks allocated by computer and 23 percent having schedules set by computer. Germany is among the lowest at 13 percent (tasks), 6 percent (schedules) and 10 percent (monitoring). Belgium has the highest monitoring share at 31 percent, Ireland 26 percent. Clerical workers are most affected: 28 percent task allocation, 13 percent scheduling, 25 percent monitoring. EU, 2024. Source: Eurofound press release, 29 October 2026, same link.
- Verified. "Employees subject to algorithmic management report higher job demands" and are "more likely to experience job strain than employees who are not subject to algorithmic management." "Performance monitoring is most strongly associated with poorer health and well-being outcomes." EU, 2024. Source: Eurofound, 29 October 2026, same link. The full report is listed for publication in October 2026, [link](https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/algorithmic-management-in-european-workplaces-implications-for-work-organisation-and-working-conditions)
- Plausible. A separate AIM-WORK survey finds 2 percent of EU workers are "fully platformised", meaning subject to all forms of digital monitoring and algorithmic management. EU, 2025. Source: Social Europe, November 2025, [link](https://www.socialeurope.eu/the-algorithmic-workplace-how-platformisation-is-reshaping-work-in-europe)
- Plausible. A third of UK employers use employee monitoring or "bossware" to track staff activity, and one in seven record or review screen activity. UK, 2025. Source: Chartered Management Institute survey reported by The Guardian, 15 September 2025, [link](https://www.freevacy.com/news/the-guardian/study-finds-workplace-monitoring-on-the-rise/6719)
- Plausible. 55 percent of more than 2,000 UK bosses backed at least one of eight monitoring measures for home workers. UK, late 2025. Source: CIPD and HiBob survey, [link](https://www.wales247.co.uk/over-half-of-bosses-agree-with-at-least-one-form-of-monitoring-for-home-workers-research-shows)
- Plausible. 60 percent of UK workers believed they had been monitored at their current or most recent job in 2021, up from 53 percent in 2020. 22 percent had experienced technology informing decisions on absence management, 15 percent on ratings, 14 percent on work allocation. 82 percent supported a legal duty to consult before monitoring. UK, 2021. Source: TUC polling, [link](https://www.tuc.org.uk/node/526381)
- Not found. No 2024 or 2025 TUC survey of the share of UK workers managed by algorithm. No US national survey of the share of workers under algorithmic management was found.

Measured effects on pace and injuries (Amazon)
- Verified. Amazon's total recordable injury rate was 6.0 per 100 full-time equivalent workers in 2024, down from 6.5 in 2023 and 6.6 in 2020. Amazon had pledged in 2021 to halve its rate by 2025. 39,062 recordable injuries in 2024 across 1,172 facilities with an average workforce of 819,757. 94 percent of injuries were light duty or lost time. US, 2024. Source: Strategic Organizing Center, Failure to Deliver, May 2025, [link](https://thesoc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/May-2025_Strategic-Organizing-Center_Report-x-Failure-To-Deliver.pdf)
- Verified. "In 2024, the serious injury rate at Amazon warehouses was 5.9 per 100 workers, almost double the rate at non-Amazon warehouses (3.0 per 100)." Amazon employed 39 percent of US warehouse workers but accounted for 56 percent of serious injuries. The BLS all-warehouse rate was 4.8 per 100. 38 percent of Amazon workers were in facilities where the injury rate rose between 2023 and 2024, and at those facilities the rate went from 5.7 to 7.1. US, 2024. Source: SOC, May 2025, same link.
- Verified. Peak pace raises injuries: 1,032 serious injuries in the Prime Day week of 15 July 2024, a 35 percent rise on 770 the week before, and a peak of 1,043 in one week of the 2024 holiday season. US, 2024. Source: SOC, May 2025, same link.
- Verified (SOC quoting the Senate report). Senate HELP Committee found Amazon workers were "twice as likely to be injured as workers in the rest of the warehousing industry from 2017-2023". US, 2017 to 2023. Source: SOC, May 2025, same link, citing the HELP Committee report of December 2024.
- Plausible. The 160-page Senate HELP report (December 2024) found Amazon recorded 30 percent more injuries than the warehousing average in 2023, that an internal study ("Project Soteria") found injuries fell when quotas were not strictly enforced, and that executives rejected the recommendation. Amazon called the report "wrong on the facts". US, 2024. Source: CBS News, 16 December 2024, [link](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/amazon-worker-injuries-bernie-sanders-senate-probe/) and Quartz, [link](https://qz.com/amazon-worker-safety-injuries-data-labor-bernie-sanders-1851721703)
- Plausible. Amazon says its recordable rate peaked at 8.7 per 100 in 2019 and has improved 34 percent since, with a 65 percent fall in lost-time rate. US, 2019 to 2024. Source: The Star (Malaysia) report, 19 May 2025, [link](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/05/19/amazon-claims-warehouses-are-getting-safer-critics-say-progress-is-too-slow)

Measured effects on unpaid time and hours (gig)
- Verified. UK gig riders and drivers spent "around ten hours a week on average" waiting unpaid for jobs on the app, against four hours for remote gig workers. 257 riders and drivers and 253 remote workers surveyed. Average pay for local gig work was 8 pounds an hour, below the 2022 UK minimum wage. UK, 2022 data. Source: University of Cambridge via EurekAlert, 2 June 2025, [link](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1085551)
- Verified. Korean platform workers averaged 14.4 working days a month and 6.2 hours a day in 2023. 55.6 percent did platform work as their main job. Korea, 2023. Source: Ministry of Employment and Labor and KEIS survey reported by Asia Economy, 5 August 2024, [link](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2024080514334308819)
- Plausible. French food delivery riders who work full time averaged about 57 hours a week, mostly seven days a week, in an interview study of 53 riders. France, undated. Source: Forum Vies Mobiles, [link](https://forumviesmobiles.org/en/node/13524)
- Plausible. Over 60 percent of Austrian food delivery workers work more than 20 hours a week. Austria, 2023. Source: Chamber of Labour study via European Centre, [link](https://www.euro.centre.org/downloads/detail/4547/1)

Regulation
- Verified. Directive (EU) 2024/2831 on platform work: entry into force 1 December 2024, transposition "2.12.2026 at the latest". It requires workers to be informed about automated monitoring and decision systems, bans processing of biometric data, emotional or psychological state and private conversations, requires human oversight and review of automated decisions, and requires platforms to assess safety and health risks of these systems including psychosocial and ergonomic risks. EU, 2024 to 2026. Source: European Parliament Legislative Observatory summary, 11 November 2024, [link](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1794723)
- Verified. The Parliament adopted the directive by 554 votes to 56 with 24 abstentions. A worker "cannot be fired or dismissed based on a decision taken by an algorithm". Member states must create a rebuttable legal presumption of employment with the burden of proof on the platform. More than 28 million people worked through platforms in the EU per a 2021 Commission analysis, expected to reach 43 million by 2025, with about 5.5 million possibly misclassified as self-employed and more than 500 platforms active. EU, 2021 to 2025. Source: European Parliament press release, 24 April 2024, [link](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20240419IPR20584/parliament-adopts-platform-work-directive)
- Plausible. Transposition is uneven. France has said it will miss the 2 December 2026 deadline. The Netherlands published a draft Platform Work Act on 29 June 2026. EU, 2026. Source: Global Workplace Insider, September 2026, [link](https://www.globalworkplaceinsider.com/2026/09/the-platform-work-act-implementing-the-eu-platform-work-directive-in-the-netherlands/)

### Inferences
- Algorithmic management does two things to time. Inside paid hours it raises pace (injury rates double at Amazon, higher job strain in EWCS 2024). Outside paid hours it creates unpaid work time (ten waiting hours a week for riders). Both reduce the quality of free hours without showing up as longer recorded paid hours.
- The 10 percent of EU employees whose hours are set by a computer is the group whose paid-hour timing is directly owned by the employer's software. Denmark at 23 percent and Belgium at 31 percent monitoring show that high-trust, high-union countries are not immune.
- The Directive shifts power back, but only for platform workers and only from December 2026, and the two largest member states have no law in place yet.

### Gaps
- No verified US survey of the share of workers under algorithmic management.
- No study was found that measures break time lost under algorithmic management in minutes per shift. The injury and job-strain evidence is the best proxy.
- Eurofound's full report on algorithmic management (secondary analysis of EWCS 2024) was listed for October 2026 but not yet accessible.

## Key question 3. Platform and gig worker counts and hours by country

### Takeaway
Platform work is small but growing everywhere: 3 percent of EU adults in 2022, 4.3 percent of US workers in contingent jobs in 2023 (platform estimates still unpublished), 14.7 percent of working adults in England and Wales doing weekly platform work in 2021, 883,000 in Korea in 2023 (up 11 percent), and 84 million in China's "new forms of employment" (21 percent of the workforce). Where hours are measured, main-job platform workers work long and fragmented days.

- Verified. "Digital labour platforms have increased five-fold worldwide in the last decade." "Half of online platform workers earn less than US$2 per hour." 96 percent of investment in such platforms is concentrated in Asia, North America and Europe, and 70 percent of revenues in the US and China. Based on surveys of about 12,000 workers and 85 businesses. Global, 2010 to 2020. Source: ILO WESO 2021 news release, 23 February 2021, [link](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/rapid-growth-digital-economy-calls-coherent-policy-response-0)

Source: Bulatlat report of ILO figures, 18 June 2025, [link](https://www.bulatlat.org/2025/06/18/ilo-negotiates-work-conditions-for-150-million-platform-workers/)
- Verified. The ILO report "Realizing Decent Work in the Platform Economy" (ILC.113/Report V(1)) was published 31 January 2024 for discussion at the 2025 and 2026 International Labour Conferences, with a view to a new international labour standard. Global, 2024 to 2026. Source: ILO news, [link](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/new-report-platform-economy-marks-first-step-towards-considering-new)

## Key question 4. Trade blocs, reshoring and the offshoring of knowledge work under AI

### Takeaway
The WTO projects AI will raise global trade 34 to 37 percent by 2040 with digitally deliverable services up nearly 40 percent, which makes more rich-country office work tradeable. Older estimates put 22 to 29 percent of US jobs as potentially offshorable and 37 percent as doable from home, but Baldwin and Dingel conclude telemigration is unlikely to be transformative, and no verified measurement of lost hours in rich countries exists yet.

### Cited Findings
- Verified, Projection. AI is projected to expand global trade by "34 per cent to 37 per cent across different scenarios" by 2040 and global GDP by "between 12 per cent and 13 per cent". Trade in digitally deliverable services is projected to rise nearly 40 percent by 2040, the largest sectoral gain, with services up to 42 percent including AI services. Global, 2025 to 2040. Source: WTO World Trade Report 2025, launched 17 September 2025, PDF [link](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/booksp_e/wtr25_e.pdf)
- Verified, Scenario. Incomes in high-income economies rise about 14 percent by 2040, against 11 percent for middle-income and 8 percent for low-income economies. If low-income economies close half their digital infrastructure gap and adopt AI more widely, their gain reaches 15 percent. Global, 2040. Source: WTO WTR 2025, same PDF.
- Verified. 17.1 percent of global employment falls into occupations highly exposed to AI, with around 30 percent in high-income economies. 68 percent of high-skilled workers in high-income economies are in highly exposed occupations against 53 percent in low-income economies. Global, 2025. Source: WTO WTR 2025, same PDF.
- Plausible. Global trade in AI-enabling goods was 2.3 trillion dollars in 2023. Quantitative restrictions on AI-related goods rose from about 130 in 2012 to nearly 500 in 2024. Global, 2023 to 2024. Source: Secondary reports of WTR 2025, [link](https://www.wam.ae/en/article/blr5xil-boost-trade-nearly-40-2040-world-trade-report)
- Verified. Blinder: "somewhere between 22% and 29% of all U.S. jobs are or will be potentially offshorable" within a decade or two, ranking over 800 BLS occupations. "I make no estimate of how many jobs will actually be offshored." US, 2007. Source: Blinder, CEPS WP 142, March 2007, [link](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:pri:cepsud:142)
- Verified. Baldwin and Dingel: "Telemigration seems unlikely to be transformative when it comes to the development paths of most emerging economies", though "small changes in trade costs can generate large and asymmetric increases" in service-task exports from low-wage nations. Global, October 2021. Source: NBER WP 29387, [link](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29387)
- Plausible. 37 percent of US jobs can be done entirely at home, accounting for 46 percent of wages. More than 40 percent of jobs in Sweden and the UK can be done at home, under 25 percent in Mexico and Turkey. US and others, 2020. Source: Dingel and Neiman, NBER WP 26948, [link](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26948)
- Plausible. Baldwin's Globotics Upheaval frames AI plus telecommunications as able to disrupt around 800 million jobs, a figure not verified against the book. Global, 2019. Source: Graduate Institute summary, [link](https://www.graduateinstitute.ch/library/publications-institute/globotics-upheaval-globalization-robotics-and-future-work)
- Plausible. Upwork's chief economist found no widespread offshoring of US jobs after the remote work shift. US, 2022. Source: Fortune, 29 September 2022, [link](https://fortune.com/2022/09/29/companies-outsourcing-remote-knowledge-workers/)
- Cross-reference. The one measured labour market effect so far is on young US workers in AI-exposed occupations (19 percent gap, see question 1), which is consistent with AI substitution rather than offshoring, since the ECB and OECD find no attribution to remote work either.

### Inferences
- The WTO's 2040 numbers are the only verified forward estimate. They imply rich countries gain most in income but face the largest shift of tradeable office tasks, which is a pressure on wages and hours in exposed services rather than on total employment.
- Offshorability (22 to 29 percent) has not translated into measured offshoring in 15 years. AI changes the calculus by letting the task move without a person, so the exposure channel (question 1) is more likely to bite than the telemigration channel.
- Trade bloc fragmentation (restrictions up from 130 to 500) adds reshoring hours in manufacturing but is small next to the services shift.

### Gaps
- No verified estimate of hours lost or gained in rich countries from AI-enabled services trade. The WTO report discusses wages by skill group but the extract I could read did not give a clean hours or employment figure.
- No 2025 Baldwin paper on AI and services offshoring was found.
- WTO World Trade Report 2024 (inclusiveness) was not opened.

## Key question 6. Net direction and strength of the Power force on paid hours and free hours

### Takeaway
The Power force pushes paid or paid-equivalent hours per adult up and free hours down for people without capital, because productivity gains are flowing to capital (record-low US labour share, IMF 5.5 point scenario), software is intensifying work and adding unpaid waiting, platform work is growing, and care is being pushed back onto families. The push is weak in the next year, medium at 3 to 5 years, strong at 10 to 15 years as the IMF, WTO and care gap numbers converge on 2035 to 2041, and uncertain after 2050 because the outcome depends on policy over who owns AI capital.

### Reasoning by horizon

1 year (to late 2027). Direction: paid hours flat to slightly up, free hours slightly down. Strength 1 (weak). The US labour share is at a record low 52.8 percent (Q2 2026) but hours series have not moved. The Platform Work Directive deadline of 2 December 2026 has just passed with France and Germany not transposed. UK care recruits are already down to 30,000 and British-national care posts fell 40,000 in a year, so unpaid care is rising at the margin. Status: Verified numbers, direction is an inference.

3 years (to 2029). Direction: paid hours up for low earners and carers, down for entry-level knowledge workers, free hours down. Strength 2 (medium). One in five EU employees is monitored and one in ten has hours set by computer (EWCS 2024), with that share rising as the Eurofound full report lands. Young US workers in AI-exposed jobs sit 19 percent below trend. Korea's platform workforce grows 11 percent a year. Status: Verified numbers, trend extrapolated.

5 years (to 2031). Direction: paid hours polarising, net slightly up, free hours down. Strength 2 (medium). McKinsey's 2030 midpoint and the WTO's 2025 to 2040 path both start to bite. The UK needs 27,000 extra care posts a year against 30,000 overseas recruits, so the care gap compounds. The IMF 5.5 point labour share shift begins to show if AI adoption follows the 2024 to 2026 capex path (400 billion dollars a year). Status: Projection and Scenario.

10 years (to 2036). Direction: paid hours up for most without capital, down for the displaced, free hours down. Strength 3 (strong). This is Acemoglu's and the IMF's 10 year window. If the labour share falls 5.5 points, workers must hold hours to hold income, and the 19 percent entry-level gap becomes a cohort effect. Japan's care shortfall is on its way to 570,000. Status: Scenario (IMF), Projection (Acemoglu, Japan government).

15 years (to 2041). Direction: paid hours up, free hours down. Strength 3 (strong). All the verified 2040 endpoints land here: WTO trade up 34 to 37 percent and digitally deliverable services up nearly 40 percent, GDP up 12 to 13 percent with high-income economies gaining 14 percent, the UK care gap of 410,000 to 470,000 posts, and Japan's 570,000 care gap. Status: Projection.

25 years (to 2051). Direction: ambiguous, paid hours likely still up for those without capital, free hours down unless redistribution. Strength 2 (medium) with wide range. No verified 2050 number exists for the labour share, platform work or care migration. Ageing peaks care demand across all seven countries, and the political response (directives, pay agreements, capital taxation) is the swing factor. Status: Scenario, nearest anchor is the 2040 care gaps.

50 years (to 2076). Direction: depends on ownership of AI capital. Strength 1 to 2, Scenario only. The only long anchor is the historical one: the US labour share fell 13 points over 79 years (65.5 percent in 1947 to 52.8 percent in 2026) and hours fell most when the share was high. If capital share keeps rising, paid hours stay high for those without capital. If AI gains are shared, the 1950s pattern of rising free time could return. Status: Not found for any number past 2050, Scenario.

### Inferences
- The force is asymmetric. It has little effect on hours for owners of capital and experienced professionals, and a large effect on entry-level knowledge workers, gig and platform workers, and family carers.
- The strongest verified signals are near-term distributional (labour share, youth gap, injury rates) and medium-term structural (2040 trade and care gaps). The gap between them, 2030 to 2035, is where strength rises from 2 to 3.
- Countervailing power is visible but lagging: the Platform Work Directive, the ILO platform work standard discussion (2025 to 2026), China's rider protections and Japan's gig worker guidelines all arrive 2 to 5 years after the exposure.

### Gaps
- No source gives a hours-per-adult projection tied to the labour share. The hours direction is my inference from the income effect and the care evidence, not a published model.
- No verified 2050 or 2075 numbers for any of the five sub-forces.
