# People force on time use: ageing, care, fertility, health, sleep and machines at home, 1965 to 2075

Research date: 1 November 2026. Scope: US, UK, EU (France, Germany, Nordics), Japan, Korea, China, with world totals as context.

How to read the status labels. Verified means the number was read in the source named. Plausible means it was read only in a secondary source that quotes the primary. Not found means no reliable source was opened. Every future number is a Projection (a statistical agency's central path) or a Scenario (an expert or market view), never a fact.

Unit notes. Dependency ratios are people aged 65 and over per 100 people aged 15 to 64 unless stated. Time use figures are averages across all people in the group on all days (so they include people who did none of the activity that day) unless marked "per participant". Hours per day times 7 gives hours per week.

## Key question 1: Old age and total dependency ratios today, 2050 and 2075

### Takeaway
Every country in scope roughly doubles its old age dependency ratio between 1965 and 2025 and does so again by 2075, with Korea, China and Japan moving fastest. Korea goes from 29 older people per 100 working age people in 2025 to 76 in 2050 and 105 in 2075, so by 2075 it has more people over 65 than people of working age. The US and UK age more slowly but still reach 46 and 49 by 2075.

### Inferences
- The number of working age people per older person falls from about 3.5 to 2.6 in the US between 2025 and 2050, from 2.0 to 1.4 in Japan, and from 3.4 to 1.3 in Korea. Each older person's care and support, paid or unpaid, has to come from fewer adults.
- Germany, Japan and France plateau between 2050 and 2075 because their baby boom cohorts have died, while Korea, China, Sweden, the UK and the US keep rising. The 50 year horizon is therefore a Korea and China story more than a Europe story.
- Because retired people count as adults, a rising 65+ share mechanically lowers paid hours per adult even if every working age person works the same hours. A 65+ share moving from 18% to 27% in the US (2025 to 2075) is by itself a 9 point shift of the adult population out of paid work.

## Key question 2: Hours of unpaid care per adult today and projected care demand

### Takeaway
Adults in the rich world put 2 to 4 hours a day into unpaid household and care work, women about 1 to 2.5 hours more than men, and total unpaid time per adult has been flat to slightly rising since 2003 even as populations age. Every official projection of paid care demand points the same way: Japan needs 570,000 more care workers by 2040, England 470,000 more posts by 2040, the US 772,000 more direct care jobs by 2034 and 9.7 million openings, and the OECD estimates a 60% rise in long term care workers by 2040 just to hold today's ratio.

### Inferences
- Converting the US figures to weekly hours, unpaid household and care work per adult was 16.7 hours a week in 2003 (2.38 hours a day) and 17.6 hours in 2024 (2.52 hours a day). That is a rise of less than one hour a week in 21 years, so at the population level the care load is almost flat even though the 65+ share rose from 12% to 18%. Child care falling with fertility has offset elder care rising.
- The paid care projections all assume today's ratio of workers to older people. Japan's 570,000 extra care workers by 2040 equal about 0.8% of its current working age population moving into paid care. England's 470,000 extra posts equal about 1.3% of its working age population. These are paid hours, so they push aggregate paid hours up for the care sector while pulling hours out of other sectors.
- No agency projects unpaid care hours directly. The nearest proxies are the 70% rise in full time unpaid carers in the UK over 20 years and the 20 million rise in US family caregivers over 10 years, both faster than population growth.

## Key question 3: Gender split of unpaid work since 1965 and how fast the gap closes

### Takeaway
In the US the housework gap between women and men fell from 25 hours a week in 1965 to 10 hours in 1985 and then to about 6 hours by 2010, so three quarters of the closing happened in the first 20 years and the pace has since dropped to roughly 1 to 1.5 hours a week per decade. Japan's gap closed by 30 minutes a day over 2001 to 2021 and the UK's by 18 minutes a day over 2020 to 2023, while the ILO's 23 country series closed 7 minutes a day in two decades.

### Inferences
- Pace of closing, US housework gap: 7.6 hours a week per decade from 1965 to 1985, then 1.5 hours a week per decade from 1985 to 2010, then about 1.6 hours a week per decade for all unpaid work 2003 to 2024. Japan: 15 minutes a day per decade (about 1.75 hours a week). ILO 23 country set: 3.5 minutes a day per decade. The UK's 18 minute fall in three years looks like a post pandemic correction rather than a new trend, the ONS flags the series as experimental.
- Almost all of the closing after 1985 came from men adding hours, not women dropping them. US men went from 4.9 to 10 hours a week of housework and from 2.6 to 7.2 hours of child care, while women's housework stopped falling at about 16 hours after 1995.
- If the post 1985 US pace of about 1.5 hours a week per decade continues, the remaining 6.4 hour gap closes in roughly 40 years, which is around 2065. If the ILO's 23 country pace holds, it does not close within the 50 year horizon.

### Gaps
- No continuous 1965 to 2024 series exists for any European country or Japan in the pages opened. Japan starts in 1976 (and the summary text starts in 2001), the UK and Eurostat series start in the 2000s.
- Men's and women's weekly hours for Germany, France and the Nordics were in Eurostat chart images and were not read.

## Key question 4: Fertility today, medium variant projections, and what fewer children do to parental time per child

### Takeaway
Every country in scope is below the 2.1 replacement rate and the UN's central path has all of them staying there through 2075, with Korea rising from 0.72 births per woman to only 1.03 by 2050 and 1.19 by 2075. The literature is consistent that time per child rises as family size falls: parents' child care per day roughly doubled between 1965 and 2012 while fertility halved, and within families each extra sibling cuts the time each child gets.

### Cited Findings

- Parental time per child rising as fertility falls, 11 Western countries 1965 to 2012: mothers 54 to 104 minutes a day of child care, fathers 16 to 59 minutes, over a period when the US fertility rate fell from 2.98 to about 1.9. [BLS ATUS 2024](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_06262025.htm) and [BLS ATUS 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm)
- Siblings and time per child (Price, Journal of Human Resources 2008, US): first born children get more parental time than later borns and the gap widens with wider spacing, each additional child reduces the time available to the others. Plausible (abstract and press summaries only). [NLS bibliography entry for Price 2008](https://nlsinfo.org/bibliography/search/title=%22The+Effect+of+Parental+Time+Investments%3A+Evidence+from+Natural+Within-Family+Variation%22/id=8066)
- Japan: husbands with a child under 6 nearly doubled housework and child care time, 48 minutes to 1 hour 54 a day, 2001 to 2021, during which Japan's fertility rate moved from 1.33 to 1.30. ### Inferences
- Fewer children has not released parental time. Child care time per parent roughly doubled over 1965 to 2012 even though births per woman fell by a third to a half, so the time saved by having one child instead of two or three is reinvested in that child (intensive parenting). For the time budget this means the childcare component of unpaid work per adult is roughly flat, not falling with fertility.
- Looking ahead, the medium variant keeps births per woman within 0.1 to 0.3 of today's level everywhere except Korea and China, where it rises. Fertility is therefore a weak driver of time use change over the next 25 years compared with ageing. Its main effect is indirect: fewer children today means fewer adult children per older parent in 2050 to 2075, so each adult carer faces more elder care.
- Korea's path (0.72 today, 1.03 by 2050) means a 2050 adult in Korea has on average less than one sibling to share parental care with, against three or four siblings for the 1965 birth cohort.

### Gaps
- No peer reviewed estimate of hours per child per week as a function of number of children was opened in full. Price (2008) and Zuppann's NLSY79 analysis are cited from abstracts.
- The Sun and Wei model linking rising parental time to fertility decline was seen only in a search summary and is not cited above.

## Key question 5: Sleep and health time trends since 1965 and projections

### Takeaway
Time diaries show sleep stayed close to eight hours a night from 1965 to the mid 1990s in the US, then rose: the US diary average went from 8.57 hours a day in 2003 to 9.04 in 2024, Japan's weekly average rose 14 minutes to 7 hours 54 between 2016 and 2021 after decades of decline, and the UK's sleep and rest time rose from 8 hours 47 to 9 hours 3 between 2020 and 2023. No statistical agency projects sleep, and the only health time projection found is England's 2.5 million extra people with major illness by 2040.

### Cited Findings

- US, 1965 to 2005 (Robinson and Martin, University of Maryland, Not So Deprived: Sleep in America 1965 to 2005, March 2008): the eight hours a day (56 hours a week) norm stayed close to the diary figure from 1965 to 1995, then rose about three hours a week over the following decade to 59 hours. In 2003 to 2005 diaries, sleep was 8.2 hours on weeknights, 8.9 on Saturdays and 9.5 on Sundays. Verified in the ScienceDaily release of the study. [ScienceDaily, 25 March 2008](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/03/080324111509.htm)
- US, 2003 (BLS ATUS, people 15 and over): sleeping 8.57 hours a day, men 8.48, women 8.65. Verified. [BLS ATUS 2003](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/history/atus_09142004.txt)
- US, 2024 (BLS ATUS): sleeping 9.04 hours a day, men 8.96, women 9.11, weekdays 8.80, weekends and holidays 9.59. Verified. [BLS ATUS 2024](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_06262025.htm)
- US, 2025 (BLS ATUS, Latest Numbers box on the ATUS home page): sleeping 9.03 hours a day, household activities 1.99, leisure and sports 5.16, working on days worked 7.66. Verified. [BLS American Time Use Survey home page](https://www.bls.gov/tus/)
- US, 2003 to 2024 (USAFacts analysis of ATUS, 20 January 2026): sleep rose 28 minutes a day over the period for people 15 and over, in every age group. People 65 and over sleep about 9 minutes more than others. Full time workers sleep 44 minutes less than people not in the workforce. The survey cannot separate sleep from lying awake. Verified. [USAFacts](https://usafacts.org/articles/are-americans-sleeping-more-or-less/)
- US, 2003 to 2016 (Basner and Dinges, Sleep, 2018): sleep rose 1.4 minutes a year on weekdays and 0.8 on weekends, 17.3 minutes over 14 years. Plausible (abstract seen in search summary). [PubMed 29325164](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29325164/)
- US leisure: 5.11 hours a day in 2003 (men 5.41, women 4.83), 5.07 in 2024 (men 5.48, women 4.67), 5.16 in 2025 (men 5.6, women 4.8). Verified. [BLS ATUS 2003](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/history/atus_09142004.txt), [BLS ATUS 2024](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_06262025.htm), [BLS ATUS 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm)
- UK (ONS): sleep and rest 9 hours 3 minutes a day in 2023 against 8 hours 47 minutes in 2020. Leisure 3 hours 39 minutes in 2023 against 4 hours 17 minutes in 2020, described as a steady three year decline. Verified. [ONS Time use in the UK](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/incomeandwealth/bulletins/timeuseintheuk/latest)
- Japan (Statistics Bureau, 2021): sleep 7 hours 54 minutes a day weekly average, up 14 minutes from 7 hours 40 in 2016, men 7 hours 58 (up 13), women 7 hours 49 (up 14). The bureau notes sleep "had been on a declining trend" and "is now on the increase". Work 3 hours 28 minutes, down 5. Verified. [Statistics Bureau of Japan 2021](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/data/shakai/2021/pdf/timeuse-a2021.pdf)
- England health time, Projection (Health Foundation REAL Centre 2023): people living with major illness rise from about 6.6 million in 2019 to 9.1 million in 2040, nearly one in five adults, 80% of the rise among people 70 and over. Plausible. [The National, 24 July 2023](https://www.thenationalnews.com/health/2023/07/24/major-illnesses-will-afflict-25-million-more-people-by-2040)
- World life expectancy, Projection (UN WPP 2024): 73.3 years in 2024, 77.4 in 2054. Verified. [UN DESA key messages PDF](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/key_messages_wpp_2024_20240709.pdf)

### Inferences
- Sleep and personal care is the one large block of the day that has grown in every country with a recent series: US plus 28 minutes a day 2003 to 2024, Japan plus 14 minutes 2016 to 2021, UK plus 16 minutes 2020 to 2023. Ageing explains part of it (older people sleep about 9 minutes more a day in the US), remote work part (full time workers sleep 44 minutes less than non workers, so fewer commutes add sleep). An ageing population mechanically adds a few minutes of sleep per adult per decade.
- Time with illness rises with age even as life expectancy does. England's 2.5 million extra people with major illness by 2040 is 4% of its adult population moving into a state that, per the ATUS employment split, cuts paid hours and raises time in personal care and medical appointments.
- Nothing in the data suggests sleep falls as people get busier with care. The direction of the sleep block is up, slowly, taking roughly 10 to 15 minutes a day per decade from the sum of paid and free time.

### Gaps
- No agency projects sleep or personal care time. The only forward looking health time number is the Health Foundation illness count.
- 1965 sleep figures for the UK, Europe, Japan (before 2001) and Korea were not found. Japan's series back to 1976 exists in the Statistics Bureau tables but the summary PDF text only carried 2001 to 2021.
- No ATUS tables between 2004 and 2023 were opened, so the 2003 to 2024 comparison is two end points plus the USAFacts and Basner trend statements.

## Key question 6: What robots and AI are measured to do at home

### Takeaway
The only measured household saving found is a 10 home Oxford trial in which human floor cleaning time halved (3 hours 43 minutes before, 1 hour 50 minutes with a robot vacuum over matching four week periods) while the floor was cleaned far more often. Adoption is still minority: about one in ten UK homes owns a cleaning robot and global consumer robot sales were 20 million units in 2024 and 34 million in 2025. The headline forecast is a 2023 expert Scenario that 39% of time on domestic work could be automated within ten years, so by 2033, with care tasks at only 28%.

### Cited Findings

- Expert Scenario, UK and Japan (Lehdonvirta, Shi, Hertog at Oxford Internet Institute with Nagase and Ohta at Ochanomizu University, PLOS ONE, 22 February 2023, 65 AI experts, 17 housework and care tasks): on average 39% of time spent on domestic work could be automated within 10 years. Housework overall 44%, care work overall 28%. Grocery shopping most automatable at 59%, physical childcare least at 21%. UK experts averaged 42%, Japanese experts 36%. UK male experts were more optimistic than UK female experts, in Japan male experts were less optimistic than female. Verified. [PLOS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281282)
- Measured trial, UK (Oxford household living lab, mixed methods, 10 households, four week pre trial diary then four weeks with a robotic vacuum): participants' floor cleaning time fell from 3:42:41 to 1:50:24 across the matched periods, a 50% cut, while total floor cleaning time including the device's 10:45 rose sharply. The poster notes extra cleaning by participants during the trial and transfer of the task between household members. Verified from the poster text, whether the totals are per household or summed across households is not stated. [Oxford Research Archive poster](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:607b7859-b18d-4351-bd18-cb6a6fdcc04c/files/s5m60qv36w)
- Global sales (IFR World Robotics 2025, Service Robots, 7 October 2025): about 20 million consumer service robots sold in 2024, up 11%, with robots for domestic tasks (floor cleaning, lawn mowing) by far the largest group. Professional service robots almost 200,000 units, up 9%. Data from a sample of 294 suppliers, not scaled to the whole industry. Verified. [IFR press release](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/service-robots-see-global-growth-boom)
- Global sales (IFR World Robotics 2026, released about 30 September 2026): consumer robot sales rose 37% to almost 34.2 million units in 2025, with household task automation, AI integration and smart home connection named as the key trends. Professional service robots almost 250,000 units, up 24%. Verified. The IFR discourages comparing figures across report years. [IFR news page](https://ifr.org/news/global-sales-of-professional-service-robots-surge-24-percent/)
- UK ownership, 2025 (Aviva How We Live report as reported by European Cleaning Journal, 27 June 2025): one in ten UK households owns a cleaning robot, a further one in seven planned to buy one within the year, and nearly one in five people aged 25 to 34 already own a robot vacuum. Plausible. The same article cites a market forecast of 16.97% a year growth for robotic vacuum cleaners over 2025 to 2033 (Scenario, market research). [European Cleaning Journal](https://www.europeancleaningjournal.com/magazine/articles/latest-news/household-robots-are-booming-thanks-to-the-youth-market)
- US and Western Europe ownership: estimates found range from 12.3% of US households (2023) to 19.5% (year unstated) and 15.2% for Germany (2023), all from low quality aggregator pages with no methodology. Not found (no authoritative source opened). [ZipDo aggregator](https://zipdo.co/robot-vacuum-industry-statistics/)
- Japan expert view on care: Japanese experts, who work in the country with the widest gender gap in the sample, expected only 36% automation and the authors link that pessimism to lived experience of household labour. Verified. [PLOS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281282)

### Inferences
- Scale check for the Scenario. US adults average 2.01 hours a day on household activities (2024). If 44% of housework time were automated by 2033 that is 53 minutes a day, about 6 hours a week per adult. Care at 28% of 0.51 hours a day is 9 minutes. The realistic measured saving today, from one small trial, is 1 hour 52 minutes of human floor cleaning per matched period, which is about half an hour a week if the poster totals are four weekly and nearly two hours a week if they are weekly, on one task, in the one in ten homes that own the device. At the population level that is roughly 3 to 12 minutes a week per adult.
- Sales growth of 11% then 37% a year suggests ownership could reach a quarter to a third of homes in the rich world within a decade, which would lift the population level saving towards 10 to 30 minutes a week, still far below the Scenario.
- The trial shows the classic rebound: the floor got cleaned about three times as much. Household automation tends to raise standards before it frees time, a pattern often noted for washing machines. Expect time freed by robots to be partly reinvested in cleanliness and in supervising the machines.

### Gaps
- No peer reviewed study measuring minutes saved per week across a representative sample of households was found for robot vacuums, dishwashers or any AI home assistant. The Oxford living lab is 10 households.
- No Ipsos or Pew household robot survey from 2024 or 2025 was opened. The IFR gives units, not minutes.
- The 2023 Oxford and Ochanomizu Scenario has no published follow up measuring progress against the 2033 date.

## Key question 7: Net direction and strength of the People force on paid hours and free hours

### Takeaway
The People force pushes paid hours per adult down and free hours per adult down over 10 to 50 years, weakly at first and strongly after 2040, because ageing raises the share of adults who are retired or ill and raises the care load on the adults in the middle. Fertility and sleep are near neutral, and home automation is the only element pushing the other way, with a small measured effect today and a large but unproven Scenario for the 2030s.

### Cited Findings
The reasoning below reuses numbers already cited in questions 1 to 6. The one number behind each horizon is given in the table.

Definitions used. Paid hours per adult is paid work averaged over all people aged 15 and over, including the retired and the ill. Free hours per adult is leisure plus social time after sleep, personal care, paid work and unpaid work. Direction is the sign of the People force alone, not the net of all forces.

Horizon reasoning (two to three lines each)

- 1 year (to 2027). Paid: flat, strength 0. The old age dependency ratio moves about half a point a year (US 28.5 in 2025, 37.9 in 2050 is 0.4 a year) and unpaid work per US adult moved 0.4 minutes a day per year over 2003 to 2024. Free: flat, strength 0. Nothing in one year is visible against survey noise of several minutes a day.
- 3 years (to 2029). Paid: down, strength 1. In 2029 people 65 and over outnumber children under 18 in the US for the first time (Census 2023 Projection). Japan needs 250,000 more paid care workers by FY2026 and England about 470,000 more posts by 2040, so a few tenths of a percent of the workforce move into care each year. Free: flat, strength 0 to 1. US sleep is rising about 1.3 minutes a day a year and takes it from free time.
- 5 years (to 2031). Paid: down, strength 1. US direct care openings run at nearly 1 million a year (9.7 million over 2024 to 2034) and the UK's full time unpaid carers grew about 40,000 a year over 20 years. Free: down, strength 1. Robot ownership near one in ten homes returns about 3 to 12 minutes a week per adult, too small to offset even the sleep drift of about 9 minutes a week per year.
- 10 years (to 2036). Paid: down, strength 2. Korea's old age ratio roughly doubles between 2025 and 2040 on its path from 29 to 76 by 2050, Japan reaches about 60, and the OECD needs 60% more long term care workers by 2040 to hold today's ratio. Free: down, strength 2 for adults aged 45 to 64, who carry both rising parent care and later retirement. The 2033 Scenario of 39% automation would return up to 6 hours a week, but at current adoption it is unproven.
- 15 years (to 2041). Paid: down, strength 2. England has 9.1 million people with major illness in 2040 (plus 2.5 million on 2019), Japan needs 2.72 million care workers (plus 570,000 on 2022), and the EU's old age ratio on the 20 to 64 base is near 50 on its path from 36 (2022) to 55 (2050). Free: down, strength 2. Unpaid care per adult rises as the 80 plus group grows, with 265 million people aged 80 and over worldwide by the mid 2030s.
- 25 years (to 2051). Paid: down, strength 3 in Korea (76), Japan (73), Germany (54) and China (52), strength 2 in the US (38), UK (41), France (49) and Sweden (43). The 65 and over share is 40% in Korea and 38% in Japan, so two in five adults are past working age before any illness is counted. Free: down, strength 2. If household automation reached the 39% Scenario the housework block would shrink by about 6 hours a week per adult, which would roughly offset the care rise, so the free hours sign is less certain than the paid hours sign.
- 50 years (to 2076). Paid: down, strength 3. Korea has 105 older people per 100 working age people, China 84, Japan 72, Germany 52, Sweden 53, the UK 49 and the US 46, and the global population is at or past its peak of about 10.3 billion. Free: down, strength 2, with wide uncertainty. Fewer siblings (Korea at 1.0 to 1.2 births per woman) means each adult carer serves more older relatives, while a fully automated home is a Scenario with no measured base.

### Inferences
- For working age adults the paid hours sign flips. Per adult of all ages paid hours fall because retirees grow as a share. Per working age adult, labour shortages in care and elsewhere push hours and participation up, and the care workforce alone absorbs about 1% of working age people in Japan and England by 2040.
- The People force is the slowest moving of the forces but the most certain. The people who will be 65 in 2050 are already 40. Only migration and health improvements can change the ratios materially before 2050, and the UN medium variant already assumes continued migration into the US and Europe.
- The gender dimension matters for who loses free hours. Women still do about 60% of unpaid work in the US and 80% in Japan, so the care rise lands mostly on women's free hours unless the closing pace of 1.5 hours a week per decade accelerates.

### Gaps
- No published model was found that converts dependency ratios into unpaid care hours per adult for 2040 or 2050. The strengths above are judgement anchored on the cited ratios and care workforce projections.
- Home automation at 25 and 50 years rests on a single 2023 expert Scenario and one 10 household trial, so the free hours direction after 2040 is the least certain cell in the table.
