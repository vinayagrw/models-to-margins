## 2. What the trend says

- Rise then plateau. The US "always rushed" share went from about a quarter in 1965 to about a third by the 1990s. Paid hours per worker fell over the same span. This is the core rebound partner story.
- Parents carry it. Parents are 14 points above non parents (34% vs 20%). Felt pressure tracks care load, not only paid hours.
- Time poverty rose fast in the 2010s. Employed Americans saying they never had enough time went from 70% to 80% in seven years, the same years smartphones became universal.
- Attention got chopped up. Screen focus fell by about two thirds between 2003 and 2020, and Mark dates the drop across the smartphone era. This is the clearest link from faster tools to fragmented time.

## 3. Theory: why faster tools can raise felt pressure

- Gershuny (2005, ISER WP 2005-9, Verified, https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/working-papers/iser/2005-09.pdf). States the paradox directly: people feel busier while diary data show work time fell or stayed flat. His answer: busyness, not leisure, is now the "badge of honour" for a class whose income comes from human capital. Feeling busy is partly a status signal, not only a time fact.
- Wajcman, Pressed for Time (2015, Plausible, review summaries only, https://technologyandsociety.org/pressed-for-time/). Devices sold as time savers sit next to feeling rushed. She rejects pure tech blame and points to blurred work and home boundaries and constant connectivity. Hours worked have not clearly risen since computers.
- Rosa, social acceleration (Plausible, not opened). Faster tools raise the pace of life because volume of tasks grows faster than the speed gain. This is time rebound at the level of a whole society.
- Perlow (1999), "time famine" (Plausible, cited in the Giurge 2020 reference list). Engineers' constant interruptions created a shortage of time that more hours did not fix.

## 4. Where data contradicts "we feel busier"

- The rushed share fell after 2004. Robinson's UMD work finds fewer "always rushed" by 2010 (the release says "about 8 percent", which may be the share, not the drop), likely tied to the recession and job loss. Less pressure came with less happiness, not more.
- Pew parents flat since 2005: 31% (2005), 34% (2012), 31% (2015). No steady climb.
- Time diaries show free time rose slightly from 1965 to 1985 (40 hours a week for men and 39 for women in 1985, Spokesman Review 1996 report of UMD data, Verified, https://www.spokesman.com/stories/1996/jun/16/americans-feeling-better-about-being-rushed/). Felt pressure is not a simple readout of hours.
- Robinson and Gershuny both argue part of the rise is talk and status, not lost time. Survey wording also matters: Gallup "never enough time" (80%) and Pew "always rushed" (about 30%) measure different things and should not be joined into one series.
- Mark's 47 seconds is a lab and workplace field measure on small samples, not a population survey.

## 5. Gaps still open

- GSS "rushed" item year by year from 1982 to 2018 not pulled. Needs the GSS Data Explorer (variable RUSHED).
- UK ONS and Eurofound time pressure series not checked.
- ATUS wellbeing module (2010, 2012, 2013, 2021) not checked for stress by activity.
- No measured link yet from screen hours or phone pickups to felt pressure at population level.
