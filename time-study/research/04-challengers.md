# 04. Challengers to the AI productivity forecasts

Purpose: the researchers who argue against the mainstream analyst numbers, put in their own words, plus the fast-takeoff side so the full range is on the table.

Mainstream forecasts under attack in this file:
- Goldman Sachs (2023): AI could lift global GDP by 7% over ten years and raise US labour productivity growth by about 1.5 points a year. Plausible. Goldman page returned 403 on 2026-10-27. Secondary coverage matches the 7%. https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/generative-ai-could-raise-global-gdp-by-7-percent
- McKinsey (2023): generative AI could add 2.6 to 4.4 trillion dollars a year, plus 0.1 to 0.6 points of labour productivity growth a year to 2040. Verified. https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-digital/our-insights/the-economic-potential-of-generative-ai-the-next-productivity-frontier

Labels: Verified means checked against the primary source. Plausible means consistent with the source but paraphrased or not rechecked line by line. Estimate means our own reading or calculation.

## 1. Daron Acemoglu (MIT): the small-number macro case

- Core claim: AI will add no more than 0.66% to total factor productivity over ten years, under 0.07 points a year. Verified. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487 (published as Economic Policy, 2025). The GDP range of about 1.1 to 1.6% is Plausible, not rechecked. Note the April 2024 draft at https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2024-04/The%20Simple%20Macroeconomics%20of%20AI.pdf gives 0.71%, so cite the May 2024 or published version.
- Evidence: Hulten's theorem. Gains equal the share of tasks exposed (about 20% of US tasks, from Eloundou et al.) times the share that is profitable to automate within ten years (about 23%, from Svanberg et al.) times the average cost saving per task (about 27%, from lab studies). Plausible. PDF text could not be extracted in this pass.
- He also argues that "hard tasks" with no clear success metric will see smaller gains, which pulls the figure down to under 0.53% (0.55% in the April 2024 draft). Verified. https://shapingwork.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Acemoglu_Macroeconomics-of-AI_May-2024.pdf
- What it attacks: Goldman's 7% and McKinsey's top range. Acemoglu says those numbers assume a far wider share of tasks gets automated, and far faster. Plausible. https://www.project-syndicate.org/commentary/ai-productivity-boom-forecasts-countered-by-theory-and-data-by-daron-acemoglu-2024-05
- Weakest point: the model is static. It takes today's models and today's adoption costs and holds them fixed for ten years. It leaves out new products, new tasks and faster science, which are exactly where optimists expect the gains. Estimate.

## 2. Robert Gordon (Northwestern): the growth ceiling

- Core claim: the great inventions of 1870 to 1970 (electricity, the engine, plumbing, antibiotics) were one-time gains. Computers and digital tech since 1970 gave one short burst (1996 to 2004) and then faded. AI is likely the same kind of narrow lift. Verified. https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691147727/the-rise-and-fall-of-american-growth
- Evidence: US TFP grew about 1.9% a year from 1920 to 1970, against about 0.6% a year after 1970 outside the 1994 to 2004 window. Plausible, figures from the book and his NBER work. https://www.nber.org/papers/w18315
- He has added headwinds that AI cannot fix: demography, education plateaus, inequality and debt. Verified, same NBER paper.
- What it attacks: any forecast that projects a lasting rise in the growth rate. Gordon has said AI will look more like the internet boom than electricity. Plausible.
- Weakest point: he judges the future by the past and cannot rule out a general purpose technology that speeds up invention itself. His pessimism on the 1990s internet looked too strong for that decade. Estimate.

## 3. David Autor (MIT): new work and expertise

- Core claim: the main long-run effect of technology is new work, not just lost tasks. About 60% of US employment in 2018 was in job titles that were not in the Census index of titles in 1940. Verified via the QJE 2024 listing and consistent summaries. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:139:y:2024:i:3:p:1399-1465. https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/NewFrontiers-Paper-Final-Sept2022.pdf (Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller, QJE 2024)
- On AI he argues it could rebuild the middle class by letting less expert workers do expert tasks, if it is used to extend expertise rather than replace it. Verified. https://www.noemamag.com/how-ai-could-help-rebuild-the-middle-class/ and NBER w32140 https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140
- What it attacks: headcount and "hours saved" forecasts that treat jobs as fixed bundles of tasks that only shrink. Time saved does not equal jobs removed or margin gained. Estimate.
- Weakest point: the same paper shows new work in recent decades has gone mostly to the top and bottom of the wage scale, and that automation has displaced faster than new work has been created since 1980. The good outcome is a choice, not a default. Verified, same QJE paper.

## 4. Erik Brynjolfsson (Stanford): the optimist counterweight

- Core claim: AI is a general purpose technology that follows a productivity J-curve. Early years show little because firms invest in unmeasured intangibles (process redesign, training, data). Gains show up later. Verified. https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20180386
- Evidence: a field study of 5,179 support agents found a 14% average lift in issues resolved per hour and 34% for novices, with little gain for top workers. Verified, abstract rechecked. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161 (QJE 2025)
- He has publicly bet Gordon 400 dollars that US private nonfarm business productivity growth will average above 1.8% a year from Q1 2020 to Q4 2029. Verified via coverage. The earlier link longbets.org/856 is a different bet (pilot-wave physics) and has been removed. https://www.marketplace.org/2025/03/20/economists-are-betting-on-an-ai-productivity-boom
- What it attacks: Acemoglu and Gordon. He argues task based models miss complementary innovation and reorganisation. Plausible.
- Weakest point: the J-curve is hard to falsify. Any slow start can be read as "still in the dip". Micro gains in one call centre may not add up to the economy. Estimate.
- Joel Mokyr (Nobel 2025) gives the deeper historical form of this view: useful knowledge feeds itself, so there is no reason to expect a ceiling. Plausible. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2025/summary/

## 5. The capability skeptics: Narayanan and Kapoor, and Gary Marcus

- Core claim (Narayanan and Kapoor): AI is a normal technology. Its impact will come over decades, like electricity, because adoption and diffusion are slow, set by firms, rules and safety, not by model benchmarks. Verified, the essay cites the electric dynamo lag of nearly 40 years. https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology
- Evidence: they separate invention, innovation and diffusion. They point out that even in fast moving areas, use in high stakes fields like medicine and law is slow and gated. Verified, same essay.
- Core claim (Marcus): large language models hallucinate and fail at reliable reasoning, so many claimed time savings vanish under checking and rework. Plausible. https://garymarcus.substack.com/
- Supporting data: a METR trial of 16 experienced open source developers on 246 tasks found they were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they were about 20% faster. Verified. https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/
- What it attacks: vendor and analyst "hours saved" numbers built on self-report or task demos, and the fast-takeoff timelines below.
- Weakest point: capability has kept rising faster than skeptics predicted in 2022 and 2023. Marcus in particular has had specific limits passed by newer models. Diffusion arguments explain delay but do not cap the end state. Estimate.

## 6. The fast-takeoff side (completes the range)

- Epoch AI GATE: an integrated model where investment buys compute, compute buys automation, and automation funds more investment. Default runs show very large investment and growth rates near 30% a year once automation is broad. Plausible, the 30% figure and the not-a-forecast line were not found on the landing page or abstract. The model and its scenario framing are Verified. https://epoch.ai/gate and https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04941
- AI 2027 (Kokotajlo, Alexander and others): a scenario in which AI that automates AI research arrives around 2027, leading to superhuman systems soon after. Verified. https://ai-2027.com/ The authors have since pushed some timelines later. Plausible.
- Anton Korinek (Virginia): sets out scenarios from business as usual to full automation within 5 to 20 years, and shows wages can collapse if all tasks are automated and compute is the binding input. Verified. https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255 (Korinek and Suh, Scenarios for the Transition to AGI)
- What it attacks: both Acemoglu (too small) and Goldman (too modest, too linear).
- Weakest point: results hinge on assumed returns to compute and on near-full task automation, which have no empirical base yet. Physical bottlenecks (power, chips, robots, permits) are thinly modelled. Estimate.

## Summary table

| Claim | Mainstream view (Goldman, McKinsey) | Challenger view | Better evidence and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size of macro gain in 10 years | About 7% GDP, 1 to 1.5 pts a year productivity | Acemoglu: no more than 0.66% TFP, about 1% GDP. GATE: far above both | Acemoglu for the next 10 years. His inputs are measured, theirs are assumed. Weak beyond 10 years |
| Will growth rise for good | Yes, AI lifts the trend | Gordon: no, a short burst like 1996 to 2004 | Tied. History favours Gordon, the GPT case favours Brynjolfsson. The Long Bet settles it in 2030 |
| Time saved equals value | Hours saved convert to output | Autor: hours shift to new work. METR: self-report overstates | Challengers. METR is a controlled trial and self-report is not |
| Who gains | Broad gains across roles | Brynjolfsson and Autor: novices gain most, experts least | Challengers. Field RCT data from 5,179 agents |
| Speed of adoption | Fast, few years | Narayanan and Kapoor: decades, set by diffusion | Challengers for regulated fields. Mainstream for coding and support |
| Jobs lost | Up to 300 million jobs exposed | Autor: 60% of jobs are new since 1940, exposure is not loss | Autor. Long time series versus exposure scores |
| Upper tail | Not modelled | GATE, AI 2027, Korinek: explosive growth possible | Neither side. No data yet. Treat as a scenario, not a forecast |
