What this means for the study (Estimate):
- The "200 years of falling hours" story is mostly a story of paid hours for men in rich countries. In China, India and Ghana the larger time block for women is unpaid, and AI tools barely touch water fetching, cooking or child care.
- Japan shows hours can fall on paper (part-time growth) while overwork harm rises. Average hours are a weak proxy for felt time.
- No long-run non-Western hours series was found in this pass. Gap stays open. Best lead is the Huberman and Minns style data or Maddison project work for Japan after 1870.

How this shapes who gets the time (Estimate):
- Working-time law decides whether saved time becomes shorter hours or more output. Only where there is a cap, a right to disconnect or a union contract does the worker have a lever. In most of the Global South informal work has none of these.
- AI Act delays mean EU workplace AI runs mostly unregulated until late 2027.
- Compute and chip access sit with a few US firms and allies. Low-income countries get access through rented APIs, so the margin from saved time flows to the provider.

Lessons (Estimate):
- Headline job-loss forecasts from 2013 to 2018 have so far overstated loss. None was built to be scored, which is itself a finding.
- Task-exposure numbers (Frey and Osborne, ILO, Goldman) measure what could be done, not what will be done or when. File 03 should label them "exposure", not "saved".
- Suggested weighting: give forecasts with stated dates and checkable units more weight, and treat brand alone as no evidence.
