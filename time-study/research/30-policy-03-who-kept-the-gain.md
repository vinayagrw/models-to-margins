## Key question 1. Labour share of income, levels and trends 1970 to 2024, and what the IMF, OECD and ILO blame

### Takeaway
The labour share fell by around 4 points in advanced economies between 1970 and the mid 2010s, with the US, Japan and Korea falling most and France flat and the UK rising. The IMF puts about half of the advanced-economy fall on technology and about a quarter on global integration, the OECD says technology and globalisation explain most of a 3.5 point fall over 1995 to 2013 with superstar firm entry doing the firm-level work, and the ILO says the global share has fallen 1.6 points since 2004 with automation-oriented technology the leading suspect and AI the next risk.

### Cited Findings

IMF, World Economic Outlook April 2017, chapter 3
- Advanced economies, 1970 to 2015. Labour shares began trending down in the 1980s, reached their lowest level in half a century just before the 2008 crisis, and are "almost 4 percentage points lower" than in 1970. Verified. [IMF blog summarising WEO chapter 3, April 2017](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2017/04/12/drivers-of-declining-labor-share-of-income)
- Advanced economies. "About half of the decline in labor shares in advanced economies can be traced to the impact of technology." Global integration's contribution is "about half that of technology", so roughly a quarter. Verified. [IMF blog](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2017/04/12/drivers-of-declining-labor-share-of-income)
- US. Technology and global integration together explain "close to 50 percent" of the US decline. Germany and Italy. Together they explain "close to 75 percent". Verified. [IMF blog](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2017/04/12/drivers-of-declining-labor-share-of-income)
- China, roughly 1995 to 2015. Labour share fell "almost 3 percentage points" despite large poverty reduction. Verified. [IMF blog](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2017/04/12/drivers-of-declining-labor-share-of-income)
- Emerging markets. Global value chain integration "predominantly" explains labour share movements by raising capital intensity. Advanced economies. Technological progress, seen in a steep fall in the relative price of investment goods, plus exposure to routine occupations "explain about half the overall decline". Verified from the WEO landing page. [IMF WEO April 2017](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WEO/Issues/2017/04/04/world-economic-outlook-april-2017)

OECD, Employment Outlook 2018, chapter 2 (Decoupling of wages from productivity)
- 24 OECD countries, 1995 to 2013. Labour share fell about 3.5 percentage points, from roughly 71.5 percent to 68 percent. US fell about 8 points. In about half the covered countries it was flat or rose, including France, Italy and the UK. Plausible, OECD site blocked, figures from a search summary of the chapter. [OECD Employment Outlook 2018](https://doi.org/10.1787/empl_outlook-2018-en)
- Decoupling split. The labour share accounts for roughly half of the decoupling of median wages from productivity, with rising wage inequality the other half. Plausible, same caveat. [OECD Employment Outlook 2018](https://doi.org/10.1787/empl_outlook-2018-en)
- The underlying working paper (Schwellnus, Pak, Pionnier, Crivellaro). Technological change in the investment-goods sector and greater global value chain participation compressed labour shares. The effect of technology is considerably weaker for high-skilled workers. In countries with falling labour shares, the fall at the technological frontier came mainly from low-labour-share firms entering the frontier, not from incumbents cutting their labour share, which the authors read as technological dynamism rather than anti-competitive behaviour. Verified abstract. [OECD Economics Department Working Paper 1503](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oec:ecoaaa:1503-en)

ILO, World Employment and Social Outlook, September 2024 update
- Global, 2004 to 2024. The labour income share fell 1.6 percentage points over two decades, of which 0.6 points in the last five years. Verified. [ILO WESO September 2024 update PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)
- Global levels. 52.9 percent in 2019, 52.3 percent in 2022, and the 2023 and 2024 estimate "remains at that level, some 0.6 percentage points below the pre-pandemic situation". Verified. [ILO WESO September 2024 update PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)

Africa minus 1.2 points, Americas minus 1.2, Arab States minus 0.8, Asia Pacific minus 0.2, Europe and Central Asia minus 1.0 with a trough of minus 1.8 in 2022 and a rebound in 2023 and 2024. Verified. [ILO WESO September 2024 update PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)
- Attribution. The ILO "find[s] evidence compatible with automation-oriented technological progress causing labour income share declines" and flags recent AI advances as making this "particularly relevant". Verified. [ILO WESO September 2024 update PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)

Academic anchors the official bodies lean on
- Karabarbounis and Neiman 2014, QJE 129(1). The labour share "has significantly declined since the early 1980s" within the large majority of countries and industries, and the lower relative price of investment goods, often tied to IT, "explains roughly half of the observed decline". Verified abstract. [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:129:y:2014:i:1:p:61-103). The size of the global corporate decline, about 5 percentage points from the mid 1970s to the late 2000s, is Plausible from a search summary of the NBER text. [NBER w19136](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19136.pdf)
- Autor, Dorn, Katz, Patterson, Van Reenen 2020, QJE 135(2) 645 to 709. US Economic Census micro data since 1982 plus international data. Sales concentrated in fewer firms, industries with the biggest concentration rises had the biggest labour share falls, and the fall came mainly from reallocation of output between firms toward high-profit low-labour-share "superstar" firms rather than from falling labour shares within firms. "We find support for all of these predictions." Verified. [NBER w23396 page](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23396)
- Lawrence 2015 (Peterson Institute and NBER). With capital and labour gross complements (elasticity below one), labour-augmenting technical change fast enough to cut the effective capital-labour ratio explains much of the US decline, and "increased capital formation would raise labor's share". Verified. [NBER w21296](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21296)

Country series
- US, nonfarm business labour share (BLS). 1947 about 65 percent (Q1 65.5), Q4 2000 62.8 percent, "generally in the range of 60 to 66 percent" through most of the post-war period, a "sustained decline" after 2000 with big drops after the 2001 and 2007 to 2009 recessions, 2010s lows of 55.1 (Q4 2013, Q3 2014), a 2020 spike to 58.9, and 52.8 percent in Q2 2026, "the lowest level ever recorded". Verified. [BLS TED, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm)
- US, Kansas City Fed. Pre-2000 the share fluctuated in a narrow range around 63 percent, then the employee-only measure fell 5.8 points over 2000 to 2010. Plausible, search summary. [Kansas City Fed bulletin](https://www.kansascityfed.org/documents/5620/macrobulletins-Trends%20in%20the%20Labor%20Share%20Post-2000.pdf)
- UK (ONS preferred Method 5). 53.8 percent in 1996 to 59.5 percent in 2023, up 5.8 points. Employers' social contributions grew about 340 percent over 1996 to 2023 against 220 percent for wages, driven by auto-enrolment (workplace pension participation 47 percent in 2012 to 79 percent in 2021), deficit contributions and employer National Insurance. G7 index comparison 1995 to 2023: UK up about 8 percent, Japan down about 5 percent, US down about 10 percent, Germany, France and Italy within plus or minus 5 percent. Verified. [ONS, Trends in the UK labour share 1997 to 2023, 25 Nov 2024](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/output/articles/trendsintheuklabourshare1997to2023/2024-11-25)
- France, Germany, Japan, US (French Treasury, Trésor-Economics 234, January 2019). Labour share has declined since the 1990s in most leading OECD countries, "mainly to the benefit of higher profit margins". In Germany, the US and Japan low real wage growth did the work and since the mid 1990s part of the distributable surplus went to capital. In France the share has been broadly stable and "the entire surplus has gone to workers' hourly compensation". In the UK it increased. Verified. [Trésor-Economics 234](https://www.tresor.economie.gouv.fr/Articles/2019/01/17/tresor-economics-no-234-the-change-in-the-labour-share-in-value-added-in-advanced-economies)
- Japan, 1995 to 2017. Real wages fell about 1 percent while productivity rose 30 percent, the only rich country where wages fell outright. Japan, the US and Korea had the largest labour share declines among 30 OECD countries and the smallest collective bargaining coverage. Non-regular workers rose from about 15 percent of the labour force in the 1980s to nearly 40 percent. Hourly pay about 2,500 yen for regulars, 1,660 yen for temporaries, 1,050 yen for part-timers. Verified. [Katz, Toyo Keizai, 13 Dec 2021](https://toyokeizai.net/articles/print-tbt/476082)
- Germany (Penn World Table via FRED). Labour compensation share about 66.8 percent in 1990 and 64.2 percent in 2019. Plausible, search summary of the FRED table. [FRED LABSHPDEA156NRUG](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/LABSHPDEA156NRUG)
- OECD median, 1990 to 2009. Share fell in 26 of 30 developed economies, median from about 66.1 to 61.7 percent. Plausible, search summary citing the OECD. [Conversable Economist summary](https://conversableeconomist.com/2013/06/06/labors-falling-share-everywhere/)
- China, 1995 to 2007 (Bai and Qian 2010). Official statistics report a 12.45 percentage point fall. A 5.25 point drop between 2003 and 2004 (42.16 percent of the total fall) came from the NBS changing how it split self-employed and state farm income. Over 1995 to 2003 the share fell 5.48 points, with structural change from agriculture to non-agriculture explaining 61.31 percent and within-sector falls (mainly industry) 38.69 percent. After 1998, SOE restructuring and expanded monopoly power are the main reasons for the fall within industry. Verified abstract. [Bai and Qian, China Economic Review 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:chieco:v:21:y:2010:i:4:p:650-670). The raw levels of 51.9 percent in 1995 and 39.7 percent in 2007 are Plausible from a secondary summary. [Search summary](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/53e9b259b7602d9703cf945d)

### Inferences
- The three official bodies converge on technology first and trade second, and all three treat policy and institutions as a residual. The UK's rising share, driven by employer pension and National Insurance contributions rather than take-home pay, shows that a rising labour share can be a compensation-mix effect rather than workers keeping more of the hour's output as cash.
- The IMF's split (US close to 50 percent explained by technology plus trade, Germany and Italy close to 75 percent) implies the US has a larger unexplained residual, consistent with the Autor et al superstar firm channel and with weaker US bargaining coverage noted by Katz and the OECD.
- The ILO numbers for 2004 to 2024 (productivity per hour up 58 percent, labour income per hour up 53 percent) mean that at the global level most of the productivity gain did reach workers as pay, with a 1.6 point share shift on top. The distribution story is sharper inside the US, Japan and Korea than globally.

## Key question 2. Overtime rules and thresholds, and whether higher premiums cut hours

### Takeaway
The US has moved the white-collar overtime salary line four times since 2004 and courts in Texas have struck down the two largest increases (2016 and 2024), leaving the 2019 line of $35,568 a year in force. Europe caps the week at a 48 hour average with a shrinking opt-out, Japan moved from no cap to criminal-penalty caps in 2019 and a 50 percent premium above 60 hours a month for all firms in 2023, Korea cut its legal maximum from 68 to 52 hours over 2018 to 2021, and China's top court declared 996 illegal in 2021. The evidence says premiums do cut hours but firms claw part of it back through lower base wages.

### Cited Findings

United States, FLSA salary threshold history
- 2004 rule. Threshold $23,660 a year (the standard weekly figure of $455 is the annual divided by 52). Verified annual figure. [SHRM, 31 Aug 2017](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/federal-judge-strikes-obama-dols-overtime-rule)
- 2016 rule. Raised the line to $47,476 a year, would have covered "more than 4 million" exempt employees. Preliminary injunction in November 2016, then on 31 August 2017 Judge Amos Mazzant (Eastern District of Texas) granted summary judgment to more than 55 business groups and struck the rule down, reasoning that the salary line was set so high it made the duties test irrelevant. Verified. [SHRM, 31 Aug 2017](https://shrm.org/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/federal-judge-strikes-obama-dols-overtime-rule). The weekly figure of $913 is Plausible from a search summary. [Snell and Wilmer blog](https://blog.swlaw.com/labor-and-employment/?p=105)
- 2019 rule. $35,568 a year (about $684 a week), highly compensated employee line $107,432. Verified. [JD Supra on Texas v DOL](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-judge-blocks-dol-rule-raising-8983455)
- 2024 rule. $43,888 a year from 1 July 2024 (about $844 a week) and $58,656 from 1 January 2025 (about $1,128 a week, set at the 35th percentile), highly compensated line $151,164, indexed updates every three years from 2027. Verified annual figures, weekly are conversions. [JD Supra](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-judge-blocks-dol-rule-raising-8983455)
- 2024 vacatur. Texas v. US Department of Labor, No. 4:24-cv-00499, Eastern District of Texas, Judge Sean Jordan, 15 November 2024, 62-page opinion, vacated the 2024 rule nationwide including the automatic updates, holding that the rule "effectively displace[d] the FLSA's duties test" and that automatic increases outside APA rulemaking exceeded delegated authority. The 2019 rule applies again. Verified. [JD Supra](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-judge-blocks-dol-rule-raising-8983455)

European Union, Working Time Directive 2003/88/EC
- Rules. Average weekly working time including overtime "must not exceed 48 hours", averaged over a reference period of up to 12 months depending on national law or collective agreement, 11 consecutive hours of daily rest, a break if on duty more than 6 hours, 24 uninterrupted hours of weekly rest on top of daily rest, "paid annual leave of at least 4 weeks per year", night work capped at an 8 hour average, and an individual opt-out "subject to strict conditions on the worker's consent". Verified. [European Commission, Working Time Directive page](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/node/1257_en)
- 2023 implementation report (COM(2023) 72, Council document 7514/23 of 17 March 2023). Fifteen Member States provide for the opt-out. Four (Bulgaria, Estonia, Cyprus, Malta) allow it in any sector, eleven (Belgium, Germany, Spain, France, Croatia, Hungary, Netherlands, Austria, Poland, Slovenia, Slovakia) limit it to jobs with extensive on-call time such as health or emergency services. Twelve (Czechia, Denmark, Ireland, Greece, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Portugal, Romania, Finland, Sweden) do not use it. Czechia abrogated the health opt-out on 30 July 2020 and Latvia stopped applying it to doctors from 1 January 2022. "The number of Member States permitting use of the opt-out has slightly decreased." Most common annual leave problems are leave in the first year of employment, accrual during sick leave and carry-over. Verified. [Council of the EU, document 7514/23](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-7514-2023-INIT/en/pdf)
- 2017 report. Ten Member States did not apply the opt-out, so the non-users rose from ten to twelve by 2023. Plausible, from an EPSU summary seen only in search. [EPSU summary PDF](https://www.epsu.org/sites/default/files/article/files/2023%20Report%20and%20Interpretative%20Communication_SSDC%208%20June%202023.pdf)
- The Commission's 2023 package also updated the 2017 Interpretative Communication, and Article 24 requires an implementation report every five years. Verified. [European Commission page](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/node/1257_en)

Japan
- Overtime premium. 50 percent premium for monthly overtime above 60 hours, previously large firms only with a grace period for SMEs, extended to SMEs by the amendment "implemented in April 2023". Overtime above 60 hours during late-night hours carries 75 percent or more. Statutory holiday work does not count toward the 60 hours. Employers may instead grant paid "alternate leave" under a labour-management agreement, to be taken within two months. Verified. [HLS Global, 2023](https://hls-global.jp/en/?p=6074). The base premium of 25 percent and the 2010 Labor Standards Act amendment that created the 50 percent tier are Plausible from secondary guides. [Rivermate guide](https://rivermate.com/guides/japan/working-hours)
- Work Style Reform law, enacted 6 July 2018, amending eight labour laws. Hard caps: no more than 100 hours of overtime in any month, no more than an 80 hour average over two to six months, no more than 720 hours a year. The prior principle of 45 hours a month and 360 a year remains the default with these as the exceptional ceilings. Effective 1 April 2019 for large employers and 1 April 2020 for SMEs. Criminal penalties for breaching the caps. From 1 April 2019 employers must designate five days of paid leave for workers with at least 10 unused days, with fines up to 300,000 yen. Equal pay rules for regular and non-regular workers from April 2020 (large) and April 2021 (SMEs). Verified. [Mercer, Japan adopts work-style labor reforms](https://www.mercer.com/en-us/insights/law-and-policy/japan-adopts-work-style-labor-reforms/)

Korea
- 2018 to 2021 reform cut the statutory weekly maximum from 68 to 52 hours. Among large firms subject to the limit, the share of overtime-working employees exceeding 52 hours fell by about 5 percentage points, roughly a fifth of its pre-reform level. About two in five workers remain exempt after full implementation in 2021. Verified abstract. [Hijzen and Thewissen, OECD SEM Working Paper 248](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oec:elsaab:248-en)
- Phase-in. Firms with 300 or more employees from July 2018, 50 to 299 from January 2020, 5 to 49 from July 2021. Plausible, search summary of press reports. [Inquirer, 2018](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/?p=1005866)
- Korea annual hours per worker fell from 2,163 in 2010 to 2,018 in 2017, 1,992 in 2018, 1,966 in 2019, 1,872 in 2023 and 1,865 in 2024. Verified, OECD data as republished by JILPT. [JILPT Databook 2026, Table 6-1](https://www.jil.go.jp/kokunai/statistics/databook/2026/06/d2026_6T-01.pdf)
- 2023 policy. The Yoon government announced measures to "reform" the 52 hour system toward "more flexibility and choice". Plausible, search summary of a Korea Times editorial. [Korea Times editorial, March 2023](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/opinion/editorial/20230308/ed-return-to-overwork)

China

- Actual hours. Enterprise employees "worked an average of 49.0 hours per week in December" 2024 (NBS, released 17 January 2025). Verified. [Global Times, Jan 2025](https://globaltimes.cn/page/202501/1327076.shtml). The 2024 half-year average of 48.6 hours is Plausible from a search summary. Evidence that premiums cut hours
- Trejo 1991, American Economic Review 81(4). If firms cut straight-time wages enough to hold the original weekly pay and hours package, a premium would have no real effect. Empirically "wage differentials do arise to mitigate the purely demand-driven effects" but they are "not large enough to neutralize overtime pay regulation completely". Verified abstract. [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:81:y:1991:i:4:p:719-40)
- Hamermesh and Trejo 2000, Review of Economics and Statistics 82(1). California required time-and-a-half beyond eight hours a day for most women and extended it to men in 1980. Using CPS data for 1973, 1985 and 1991, the penalty "substantially reduced" daily overtime by California men relative to other states, with stronger effects when women are used as the control, implying "a price elasticity of demand for overtime hours of at least -0.5". Verified abstract. [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:82:y:2000:i:1:p:38-47)
- Barkume (BLS) re-test. Among hourly jobs on 40 hour schedules (covered), more usual overtime is associated with lower wage rates, but not among reduced-hour jobs, supporting partial wage offset. Plausible, search summary. [BLS working paper EC070010](https://www.bls.gov/osmr/research-papers/2007/ec070010.htm)
- Bell and Hart 2003 (UK) find a negative correlation between base pay and overtime pay consistent with the fixed-job model. Plausible, cited in a University of Tokyo discussion paper seen in search. [ISS discussion paper f-147](https://web.iss.u-tokyo.ac.jp/discussion/dpf/pdf/f-147.pdf)

### Inferences
- The US pattern since 2004 is that the salary line only moves when an administration moves it, and the two attempts to move it far (2016 and 2024) were both vacated by the same district in Texas on the same reasoning. The line in force in November 2026 ($35,568) is therefore the 2019 number, worth less in real terms every year.
- The combination of Trejo (partial wage offset) and Hamermesh and Trejo (elasticity of at least minus 0.5) means a premium converts part of saved time into fewer hours and part into lower base pay. Policy that raises the premium moves saved time toward leisure but not one for one.
- Korea's result (a fifth less incidence of very long weeks at large firms, two in five workers exempt) is the cleanest recent natural experiment that a statutory cap works at the margin but leaks through exemptions.

## Key question 3. Minimum wage and hours worked

### Takeaway
The best US evidence finds no net loss of low-wage jobs after 138 state increases over 1979 to 2016, but the Seattle study with hours data found a 6 to 7 percent hours cut after the 2016 move to $13 (9.4 percent in the first version), so the hours margin can absorb a large increase even where headcount does not.

### Cited Findings
- Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer (NBER w25434, 2019). 138 prominent state-level minimum wage changes, 1979 to 2016, bunching estimator. "The overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged over five years following the increase." Direct earnings effect amplified by modest spillovers at the bottom. No evidence of job loss at higher minimum wage levels, some evidence of reduced employment in tradable sectors. The abstract is silent on hours. Verified. [NBER w25434](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434). Publication in the QJE 2019 is Plausible from search listings. [LSE record](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/88682/)
- Jardim et al (NBER w23532, 2017, revised May 2018, published AEJ Economic Policy 14(2) 263 to 314, 2022). Seattle's increase to $13 in 2016 "reduced hours worked in low-wage jobs by 6-7 percent", hourly wages in those jobs rose 3 percent, and total payroll fell, lowering pay by an average of $74 a month per job in 2016. No employment effect in restaurants at any wage level. Data from Washington State Employment Security Department, one of few states that collects hours. Verified. [NBER w23532](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532)
- The June 2017 version reported a 9.4 percent hours fall for workers under $19 an hour and a loss of $125 a month. Plausible, search summaries of the first version and its critics. [Governing summary](https://www.governing.com/archive/tns-seattle-minimum-wage-study.html)
- EPI critique of the Seattle study. The comparison group assumes Seattle's low-wage market would have grown like the rest of Washington, the sample excludes multi-site employers, and the hours decline is about 45 minutes a week. Plausible, search summary. [EPI blog, 2017](https://www.epi.org/blog/six-reasons-not-to-put-too-much-weight-on-the-new-study-of-seattles-minimum-wage/)
- Berkeley study of the same period found no adverse employment effect and positive wage effects. Plausible, search summary. [Vermont JFO issue brief](https://ljfo.vermont.gov/assets/Subjects/Minimum-Wage/ab309981a4/Issue_Brief_Two_Minimum_Wage_Studies.pdf)

### Inferences
- Headcount studies (Cengiz et al) and hours studies (Jardim et al) are measuring different margins. Where administrative hours data exist, the adjustment shows up in hours first. A minimum wage that lifts hourly pay 3 percent while cutting hours 6 to 7 percent converts the gain into unpaid time, which is the time-versus-pay trade this study cares about.
- The revision from 9.4 to 6 to 7 percent between 2017 and 2018 is the largest single move in the Seattle number and should be cited with the version date.

### Gaps
- European or Asian evidence on minimum wage and hours (for example Germany's 2015 minimum wage and hours of mini-jobbers, or Korea's 2018 to 2019 increases and the 52 hour week overlapping). Not searched, budget exhausted.
- Cengiz et al's threshold of 59 percent of the median wage as the highest level tested. Not found in the abstract.

## Key question 4. Paid leave, holidays and parental leave, and how much of the US versus Europe hours gap they explain

### Takeaway
Statutory paid annual leave is 0 days in the US, 28 in the UK, 25 in France, 20 in Germany, 10 rising to 20 in Japan, 15 in Korea, 5 to 15 in China and 25 in the main Nordics. Paid leave for mothers runs from 0 weeks in the US to 39 in the UK, 42 in France, 58 in Germany and Japan and 91 in Korea. Mandated holidays and vacation explain about 80 percent of the US versus Europe difference in weeks worked and roughly 40 percent of the total hours gap for France and Germany, with employment rates and part-time work making up the rest.

## Key question 5. Retirement and pension age, policy moves 2000 to 2026, and the lifetime hours effect

### Takeaway
Every country in scope except Korea has legislated a later pension age since 2000: France 62 to 64 (2023 law, full effect 2030), Germany 65 to 67 (2007 law, cohorts 1947 to 1964, complete in 2031), Japan 60 to 65 (phased 2001 to the fiscal year ending March 2026 for men, March 2031 for women), China 60 to 63 for men and 50 or 55 to 55 or 58 for women (2024 decision, effective 1 January 2025, over 15 years). Korea's statutory retirement age has been 60 since 2016 and bills to lift it to 65 by 2033 had not passed as of December 2025.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Lifetime hours. Taking the 2024 annual hours per worker, a two-year rise in the retirement age adds roughly 2,980 hours in France (2 x 1,491), 2,660 in Germany (2 x 1,331), 3,230 in Japan (2 x 1,617) and 3,730 in Korea (2 x 1,865). - Korea is the outlier: the pension age moves to 65 in 2033 while the retirement age stays at 60, so the gap is being filled by re-employment contracts rather than statute, and the Bank of Korea estimate of 0.4 to 1.5 youth jobs per retained older worker is the number the employers are using against the bill.

## Key question 6. Productivity policy and who kept it. The US gap since 1973, Germany's wage restraint, Korea's long hours, China's 996 and hukou

### Takeaway
EPI's series shows US nonsupervisory productivity up 80.9 percent and hourly pay up 29.4 percent over 1979 to 2024, Feldstein and Lawrence answer that consistent deflators and total compensation close most of the gap through 2006 and that the later fall in labour's share reflects labour-augmenting technology with capital and labour as complements. Germany's turnaround rested on decentralised bargaining and wage restraint from the mid 1990s, Japan's wages fell as non-regular work spread, and China's 996 was ruled illegal in 2021 while measured weekly hours stayed near 49.

### Cited Findings
- US, EPI. "Productivity increased 80.9% between 1979 and 2024, while their hourly pay increased just 29.4%" for nonsupervisory workers, as cited by Fortune on 10 November 2024. Verified in Fortune, Plausible as EPI's figure because the EPI site blocked fetches. [Fortune, 10 Nov 2024](https://fortune.com/2024/11/10/american-worker-labor-trump/). EPI's own chart notes define pay as compensation of production and nonsupervisory workers and productivity as net output per hour less depreciation. Plausible. [EPI, The Productivity-Pay Gap](https://www.epi.org/productivity-pay-gap/)
- EPI's three wedges. Compensation inequality, the labour share shift, and the gap between consumer prices and output prices (terms of trade), with net effective productivity already adjusting for depreciation and deflators. Plausible, search summary. [EPI decomposition table](https://www.epi.org/chart/productivity-pay-table-1-decomposing-the-growing-wedges-between-productivity-and-median-pay-since-1973-selected-years/)
- Feldstein 2008 (NBER w13953, Journal of Policy Modeling 30(4)). Nonfarm business output per hour grew about 1.9 percent a year over 1970 to 2006 and real compensation per hour about 1.7 percent a year when deflated with the same output price index. - Germany. Dustmann, Fitzenberger, Schönberg and Spitz-Oener 2014, Journal of Economic Perspectives 28(1) 167 to 188. ## Key question 7. Rebound and Jevons applied to time. Which policymakers or official reports discuss time rebound, induced demand or "work expands"

### Takeaway
No official report from the UK Government Office for Science, OECD, EU JRC or Japan Cabinet Office explicitly discussing time rebound was found within the search budget. The concept lives in the sustainability literature (Buhl and Acosta 2015, Knight, Rosa and Schor 2013), and the measured policy "leakage" that resembles rebound appears in Trejo's wage offset, Korea's exemptions and Alesina's social multiplier.

### Cited Findings
- Buhl and Acosta 2015, Sustainability Science. Mixed semi-standardised interviews with German national surveys on time use and expenditure. "Time savings due to a reduction in working time trigger relevant rebound effects in terms of resource use", though both qualitative and quantitative results "put the rebound effects following a reduction in working time into perspective", and time-use rebound is linked to more voluntary social engagement and greater life satisfaction (the second and third dividends). Verified summary. [degrowth.info library entry](https://degrowth.info/en/library/work-less-do-less)
- Knight, Rosa and Schor 2013 distinguish a scale effect (less output) from a compositional effect (time-use rebound into more or less carbon-intensive activity) in OECD panel data 1970 to 2007. Plausible, search summary. [Springer chapter listing](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-319-38807-6_10)
- Kaplan and colleagues 2025, Industrial and Organizational Psychology. Full-time workers in high-income countries still work 35 to 42 hours a week, the decline in hours "was most pronounced in the earlier- and mid-20th century and has plateaued in recent decades", and gains in income, education and productivity "sometimes have paradoxically translated into increased, not decreased, work hours". Plausible, search summary. [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/311DE1B2CED69DC226AF5DD9AB04FD90)
- Policy leakage evidence that functions like rebound. Overtime premiums are partly offset by lower straight-time wages (Trejo 1991, Verified), two in five Korean workers are exempt from the 52 hour cap (Hijzen and Thewissen, Verified), only 62.1 percent of Japanese paid leave was taken in 2022 (HRM Asia, Verified), and Alesina et al argue returns to leisure rise as more people take longer vacations, a social multiplier that amplifies mandated leave (Verified). Sources as cited above.
- ILO 2024 on AI. The finding on automation "is not a prediction about AI effects" but "highlights the importance of ensuring that any benefits of AI are widely distributed". Verified. [ILO WESO September 2024 update PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- Policymakers in the sources opened discuss who keeps the gain (share, pay, hours caps) but not whether saved time is re-absorbed by new work. The closest official framing is the ILO's point that productivity per hour rose 58 percent while pay per hour rose 53 percent, which is a distribution statement, not a rebound statement.
- The measurable rebound in policy terms is the gap between statute and practice: Japan's 19 public holidays and 10 to 20 leave days against 10.9 days taken, China's 44 hour week against 49 hours worked, Korea's 52 hour cap with 40 percent exempt. These are the numbers a writer can use for "time expands back into work".

## Key question 8. Welfare-state spending and hours. Do countries with higher public spending work fewer hours

### Takeaway
Across the ten countries in scope the correlation between public social spending (15 to 31 percent of GDP) and annual hours per worker (1,331 to 1,865) is strongly negative (r about minus 0.75, roughly 27 fewer hours a year per extra point of GDP), with Korea and the US at the low-spend long-hours corner and France, Finland and Germany at the high-spend short-hours corner. Prescott reads this as taxes, Alesina et al as regulation and unions with taxes explaining about 10 percent.

- Public social expenditure, percent of GDP, 2021 to 2024 range (OECD SOCX as tabulated by Wikipedia). ### Inferences
- My own calculation on the ten pairs above gives a Pearson correlation of about minus 0.75 and a slope of about minus 27 hours a year per percentage point of GDP in public social spending. With ten observations this is descriptive only, and the hours series is not designed for cross-country level comparison (JILPT note), so present it as a pattern, not an estimate.
- Japan and the UK sit close to the fitted line, Korea and the US below it in spending and above it in hours, Germany above it in spending and well below it in hours. Germany's position fits the collectively agreed 30 leave days and the 1,331 hour figure rather than its statutory 20 days.
- The spending-hours link runs partly through the mechanisms in questions 4 and 5: paid leave, parental leave and earlier pension ages are all public spending that buys time out of the market. The Prescott versus Alesina debate is about whether the tax that funds that spending, or the regulation that comes with it, is the channel.
