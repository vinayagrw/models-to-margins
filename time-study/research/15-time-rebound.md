# 15. Time rebound: measured cases

Question: when a tool saves time per task, does total time on that activity fall, stay flat, or rise?

This file fills the gap named in 14-focus-topics.md. It does not repeat Jevons, Parkinson or the energy rebound range from 02-paradoxes.md.

Tags. Verified means the page was opened in this pass and the figure was read there. Plausible means the figure is widely cited but the page was not opened or did not show the number.

## Cases

### 1. Travel time budget (Zahavi, Marchetti)
- Tool: horse, rail, car, faster roads.
- Saved: speed rose many times over 200 years, so each km takes far less time.
- Spent on: longer distance. People live farther from work and travel farther for shops and leisure.
- Net: daily travel time stays near 1 hour (half an hour each way). Zahavi named this the travel time budget. Metz used UK National Travel Survey data to support it. The England NTS 2023 shows about 353 hours a year, around 58 minutes a day, against 370 hours in 2019.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marchetti%27s_constant. Verified (1 hour figure). UK NTS 2023: https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/national-travel-survey-2023/nts-2023-factsheet-accessible-version. Verified.

### 2. Induced traffic (Duranton and Turner 2011)
- Tool: more lane km of road.
- Saved: shorter trip times right after the road opens.
- Spent on: more driving. Vehicle km rise in proportion to interstate lane km.
- Net: congestion and trip time return to where they were. The abstract says vehicle km rise proportionately with interstate lane km, so elasticity about 1.
- Source: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.6.2616. Verified (abstract).

### 3. Housework and appliances (Ramey 2008, also Vanek 1974)
- Tool: washing machine, running water, electric stove, fridge, vacuum.
- Saved: large cuts in minutes per load and per meal.
- Spent on: higher standards (clean clothes daily, cleaner homes), more child care, and more men's home time.
- Net: prime age women lost only about 6 hours a week of home work from 1900 to 1965, despite the appliance wave. They then lost 12 more from 1965 to 2005, mostly from paid work entry. Men rose about 13 hours. Per person home time, all ages, rose slightly over the century.
- Source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w13985. Verified. Vanek's finding that full time housewives spent about 52 hours a week in 1924 and 55 in the 1960s is Plausible (not opened).
- Bittman, Rice and Wajcman 2004, "Appliances and their impact": in the 1997 Australian Time Use Survey, appliances rarely cut women's unpaid work time and sometimes raised it. Verified (abstract), https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/69e28c23-1065-4788-a26f-39fc09408f25.

### 4. Meetings after video tools (Microsoft Work Trend Index)
- Tool: Teams and Zoom remove travel and room booking.
- Saved: minutes per meeting set up and travel.
- Spent on: more meetings, longer days, more chat after hours.
- Net: the 2022 index says meeting time for the average Teams user rose 252 percent since February 2020, and weekly meeting count rose 153 percent.
- Source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/great-expectations-making-hybrid-work-work. Verified (252 and 153 percent both on the page).

### 5. AI coding (METR 2025, one example only)
- Tool: early 2025 AI coding assistants.
- Saved: developers expected 24 percent faster.
- Spent on: prompting, waiting and checking AI output.
- Net: 19 percent slower on real tasks, while developers still felt about 20 percent faster. METR notes newer 2026 data exists.
- Source: https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/. Verified.

### 6. Email and messaging
- Tool: email, then chat.
- Saved: send cost falls to near zero per message.
- Spent on: more messages, more people copied, faster reply norms.
- Net: office workers report a large share of the week on email and chat, McKinsey Global Institute (The social economy, July 2012) puts it at 28 percent of the workweek for interaction workers. Plausible: the McKinsey page timed out, figure confirmed only in secondary coverage.

## How to read the cases
- Full rebound (net flat): travel time budget, induced traffic.
- Backfire (net up): meetings, AI coding in the METR test, whole population housework.
- Partial rebound: women's housework 1900 to 1965, saving mostly eaten by higher standards.

## Two best academic framings
1. Linder, The Harried Leisure Class (1970): as time gets more productive at work, each hour gets more valuable, so people cram more goods and tasks into the same hours and feel more rushed. Plausible.
2. Binswanger, "Technological progress and sustainable development: what about the rebound effect?" (Ecological Economics 2001) and related work on time saving innovations: a tool that saves time frees hours that are spent on other energy and time using activities, so savings get re-spent. Plausible.
- Runner up: Wajcman, Pressed for Time (2015): devices do not cause haste by themselves, people's norms and choices set how the saved time is used. Plausible.
