## The ten personas

### 1. Labour economist

- **Verdict:** Not by default. Measured saving is small, about 1.4% to 1.6% of all US hours, and in Denmark it did not move hours or pay at all.
- **Weakest evidence (weakest first):**
  1. Self-reported savings, like the 26 minutes a day in the UK trial, with no record of where the time went.
  2. Exposure figures such as 57% of US hours or 300 million jobs, which are ceilings and not forecasts.
  3. Field studies from 2023 to 2025 tools only, before agents.
- **Would watch:** Average annual hours per worker. Pay and hours in the Danish style register studies as they repeat.
- **Advice to business leaders:** Decide up front how saved hours are shared, or they will default to the firm.

### 2. Economic historian

- **Verdict:** History says no in the short run and yes over lifetimes. Paid hours roughly halved since 1870, yet weekly leisure rose only 4 to 5 hours. The gains came as longer schooling and retirement.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. Every 1826 figure is an estimate.
  2. Commute history before 1950 rests on the Marchetti idea, not surveys.
  3. Electricity took decades to show in productivity, so two years of AI data says little.
- **Would watch:** Whether hours per worker fall faster after AI than after electricity.
- **Advice:** Plan for a decade of reorganisation, not a one year payback.

### 3. AI technologist

- **Verdict:** Yes for some tasks, and the share grows fast. Novices gained 34% in the call centre study, and directive hand-off use rose from 27% to 39% of Claude conversations in about nine months.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. The METR slowdown used early 2025 tools on 16 developers, so it ages quickly.
  2. Studies measure chat tools, not agents.
  3. Fast takeoff numbers such as Epoch GATE are scenarios with no data behind them yet.
- **Would watch:** The directive share in each new Anthropic Economic Index, and agent task length.
- **Advice:** Measure saved time from logs and output, never from how fast people feel.

### 4. Union leader

- **Verdict:** Only if workers bargain for it. Denmark shows 3% to 7% of gains passed to workers. Iceland and the UK four-day trials show time is freed when the hour cut is fixed first.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. Four-day trials were self-selected firms with no random control.
  2. No study tracks where saved time goes inside a firm.
  3. The 13% to 16% fall in jobs for 22 to 25 year olds is early and may have other causes.
- **Would watch:** Collective agreements that trade AI gains for hours. Junior hiring in exposed jobs.
- **Advice:** Put a share of AI savings into hours in writing, before the tools roll out.

### 5. Enterprise CFO

- **Verdict:** The firm gets the time first, as output. Email time fell about 1.4 hours a week in one field study, but meetings did not move, and seat revenue in IT services is already under pressure.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. Vendor numbers dominate retail, manufacturing and farming.
  2. Price per token falls about 10 times a year, but cost per finished task can rise.
  3. Infosys growth guidance fell to 1% to 2%, but linking that to AI is analyst opinion.
- **Would watch:** Cost per finished task, review hours, and revenue per employee.
- **Advice:** Budget the review and rework time AI creates, or the business case will not hold.

### 6. Sociologist of time

- **Verdict:** Felt time will not improve on its own. About 275 interruptions a day already, and about 6 hours 38 minutes online a day. Cheaper messages mean more messages.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. No study measures felt time and AI together.
  2. The harried leisure idea is an old theory with modest effects.
  3. Meeting growth of about 250% after 2020 is still not confirmed.
- **Would watch:** Message and meeting volume per worker, and survey measures of time pressure.
- **Advice:** Cap messages and meetings when you add AI, or the saved time leaks into noise.

### 7. Global South policymaker (India and Kenya)

- **Verdict:** Not from office AI. In India women spend about 305 minutes a day on unpaid domestic work against about 88 for men. Women and girls spend about 200 million hours a day collecting water. About 58% of the world's workers are informal.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. Almost every AI time study is from rich countries in English.
  2. The M-Pesa poverty finding (about 194,000 Kenyan households) is contested.
  3. China's time use data was not researched at all.
- **Would watch:** Water, power and cooking fuel access, local language voice AI, and loss of outsourcing jobs.
- **Advice:** Your outsourcing partners' workforces carry the first hit, so share the gains with them.

### 8. Feminist care economist

- **Verdict:** Not for most women. Unpaid care is 16.4 billion hours a day, 76.2% done by women, and only about 21% of physical childcare looks automatable in ten years.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. The 39% household automation figure comes from 65 experts in a small survey.
  2. The PwC household estimate was sponsored by Meta.
  3. Home production today is slightly higher than in the early 1900s, so past appliances did not free this time.
- **Would watch:** Women's and men's unpaid hours in national time use surveys.
- **Advice:** Count staff care loads when you plan AI gains, and use saved time for flexible hours.

### 9. Disability advocate

- **Verdict:** Possibly the biggest gain per person, but nobody has measured it. File 09 lists disabled people as near absent from the study.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. There is still no number on AI time saved for disabled people. The only hard number is on harm, from CV screening (see Evidence below).
  2. Gains go to people with access, and access is uneven.
  3. Cognitive offloading studies are small, such as the 54 person EEG preprint.
- **Evidence:** In a University of Washington study (Glazko et al., ACM FAccT 2024), GPT-4 ranked a CV that mentioned a disability award first in only about a quarter of 60 trials, roughly 15 of 60. After the team gave a custom GPT written instructions against ableism, those CVs ranked higher than the plain CV in 37 of 60 trials. Two of the six disability CVs, depression and autism, still did badly. Source: https://www.washington.edu/news/2024/06/21/chatgpt-ai-bias-ableism-disability-resume-cv/ . Verified (source opened).
- **Would watch:** Accessibility features in workplace AI, and time to complete daily tasks for disabled users.
- **Advice:** Test every AI rollout with disabled staff first, the gains may be largest there.

### 10. Ecological economist

- **Verdict:** Time saved in offices is partly paid for by power and lost to heat elsewhere. Data centres go from about 415 TWh to about 945 TWh by 2030. Heat stress takes about 2.2% of global working hours by 2030.
- **Weakest evidence:**
  1. No study links energy per saved hour.
  2. Rebound in energy is usually partial, not full, so the Jevons case for AI is still open.
  3. The 2.2% heat figure is called conservative by the ILO itself.
- **Would watch:** Energy per finished task, and hours lost to heat in farm and building work.
- **Advice:** Report the energy cost next to every hour your AI claims to save.

## Synthesis

### Where they agree

1. Measured AI time saving today is small, about 1.4% to 3% of all hours, and far below exposure figures.
2. Saved time does not become free time by default. It turns into more output, more messages and higher targets.
3. Fixed rules on hours are the only lever with real evidence, from Iceland and the UK trials.
4. Self-report overstates. The METR gap between belief and result proves it.
5. Averages hide who gets the time.

### Where they split

| Question | Side A | Side B |
|---|---|---|
| How fast will savings grow | AI technologist: fast, agent use is rising quickly | Historian and labour economist: decades, like electricity |
| Who should capture the gain | CFO: the firm first, as output | Union leader and care economist: workers, as hours |
| Where the biggest gain is | Technologist and disability advocate: per task and per person | Global South and care economist: in water, fuel and care, outside AI |
| Is the net positive | Most panelists: yes over a lifetime | Ecological economist and sociologist: not once energy, heat and felt time count |

### Four-column view of time saved

| Column | What it means | Today's evidence | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employer time | Paid hours the firm recovers as output | 5.4% for US users, 1.4% to 1.6% of all hours, 14% to 34% in call centres | Rising, kept by the firm |
| Worker time | Paid hours that come back as shorter weeks or the same pay for less | Denmark: no change in hours, 3% to 7% of gains to workers. Iceland and UK trials only where hours were cut first | Flat unless bargained |
| Unpaid home time | Cooking, care, admin, water, fuel | 16.4 billion care hours a day. India women about 305 minutes. About 39% of household tasks automatable in 10 years, childcare about 21% | Slow, needs robots and infrastructure |
| Felt time | Whether people feel less rushed | 275 interruptions a day. 6 hours 38 minutes online. 50% of Americans more concerned than excited | Likely worse in the short run |

### Conclusion by horizon

| Horizon | Will people get more free time | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year (2027) | No. Employers gain small output. Hours flat. Felt time worse | High |
| 3 years (2029) | Not on average. Some four-day firms. Junior hiring falls in exposed jobs | High |
| 5 years (2031) | A small fall in hours where contracts lock it in. Most gains still kept as output | Medium |
| 10 years (2036) | Some shorter weeks in white collar work, if bargained. Home time gains begin with robots | Medium |
| 15 years (2041) | Free time likely higher in rich countries. Gaps by gender and country remain | Low |
| 25 years (2051) | Large gains possible, depending on who owns the surplus and on energy limits | Low |
| 50 years (2076) | Keynes may be partly right a century late. Unknown for the Global South | Speculative |

### Bottom line

AI will save time long before it frees time. The saving goes to the employer first, the worker only by agreement, the home only after robots and infrastructure, and felt time last. Leaders who want staff to gain time must fix hours, cap messages, and count review work from day one.
