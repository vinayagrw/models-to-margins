# How human time use changed, 1826 to 2026

Scope: rich world means Western Europe, the US, Canada and similar. Global means world average where any data exists. Most series before 1950 cover rich countries only.

Confidence tags:
- **V** Verified. Two independent sources agree.
- **P** Plausible. One source.
- **E** Estimate. Our own interpolation or reasoning from nearby data. Treat as a placeholder.

The lifetime numbers multiply annual hours by working years and divide by waking hours at 16 per day. They are our own sums. They show the biggest gain in free time came from longer lives and retirement, not from a shorter week.

## Five themes

1. **Paid work fell by half.** Annual hours dropped from about 3,000 in 1870 to about 1,600 to 1,800 in rich countries today. The US now works more than most of Europe. S1 shows the gap is older than the 1980s.
2. **More work for mother.** Appliances cut drudgery but standards rose. Vanek and Cowan find full time housewives worked about the same hours in the 1960s as in the 1920s. Ramey and Francis (S4) find per capita home production in 2005 was close to 1900, because men took on more and women moved into paid work. The big fall in chores came after 1965 and is mostly about women's paid work, not machines.
3. **Travel time stays near one hour.** Faster transport bought distance, not time. The Marchetti constant (S7) holds from walking cities to car suburbs. London is an outlier near 90 minutes.
4. **Leisure per person barely grew per week.** S4 says per capita leisure in 2005 was close to 1900 once you count children, students and the old. Leisure grew as years of life, not hours in the week.
5. **Saved time went to screens.** TV took 2 to 3 hours a day from the 1960s. Online time now adds on top. UK adults spend 4 hrs 30 min a day online for personal use (S10). Sleep in the US is about 9 hours a day in ATUS, which counts time in bed (V, S9 gives 9.04 hrs). HETUS (S12) gives about 8 to 8.5 hours in Europe.

## Weakest data

- 1826 has no wage hours series. Every 1826 figure is E.
- Lebergott chore figures differ by source, 58 against 70 hours in 1900. The gap comes from which tasks count.
- Commute minutes before 1950 rest on the Marchetti idea, not surveys.
- Global chores, leisure and commute have no series before about 2000.
- Lifetime shares are our own arithmetic.

## Missing claims (completeness)

- Ramey and Francis find 70 percent of the fall in hours worked was offset by more time in school. This matters for theme 4.
- The US was below Europe in hours in 1870 but above it from about 1900 (S1). Theme 1 says the gap is older than the 1980s, which fits.
- Weekly hours in the US and UK stopped falling after about 1970 to 1990 (US 38.8 in 1970, 40.25 in 2000). This flat line is worth stating.
