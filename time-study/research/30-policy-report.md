# Law Capped the Tail, Wages Moved the Mean

Across two centuries and eight economies, hours law mostly ratified what wages and bargaining had already delivered, and it bit only at the long-hours tail. In Britain and the United States the working week fell from 57 to 62 hours in 1870 to 47 to 48 hours in 1929 with no general adult hours statute (UK and US, Verified), and the one US federal cap, the 44-hour Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, arrived when production workers already averaged 37.3 hours a week (US, 1938, Verified). France and Germany are the cases where law led. France fell from 62 hours in 1913 to 48 in 1929 after its 1919 eight-hour law, then to 39 by 1938 after the 1936 forty-hour law, and the second cut coincided with stagnating output (France, Verified). Japan, Korea and China legislated later, and hours fell only where enforcement followed the statute, which in China it has not: enterprise employees worked about 49 hours a week in 2023 and 2024 against a legal 40 (China, Verified). Who kept the productivity gain changed around 1980. Until then workers took it as pay and as time in every country in scope. After it, Europe kept converting gains into time, the United States flattened at about 1,800 hours a year per worker, and after 2000 the labour share slid in the US, Japan and Korea, reaching a record low of 52.8 percent in the US in the second quarter of 2026 (Verified). As of November 2026 no G7 or EU government has legislated a shorter week in response to AI. Korea alone has a dated target, annual hours in the 1,700 range by 2030 (Proposal, Verified), Spain's 37.5-hour bill failed 178 to 170 (Spain, 2025, Verified), and the five governments that measured AI time savings in their own staff found between 13 minutes a week and 81 minutes a task, with none able to say where the time went. The best macro estimate gives AI no more than 0.53 to 0.66 percent of total factor productivity over ten years (US, 2024, Projection, Verified), too little to pay for a four-day week, and the only quantified 2050 scenario puts hours per worker between 1,600 and 2,070 a year (global, 2026, Projection, Verified). Five challenges, rebound, Baumol's care sector, employer capture, unequal leisure and ageing, explain why the realistic path to 2076 runs through pension ages, overtime rules and firm-level subsidies rather than a statutory week.

## Britain and America bargained hours down, France and Germany legislated them

The starting point matters because it was a peak, not a plateau. Voth's reconstruction from court witness accounts puts English annual hours at **2,576 in 1760, 3,328 in 1800 and 3,356 in 1830** (England, Verified), so the first Factory Acts arrived when English hours were the highest they have ever been ([Voth, The Longest Years](https://www.crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/users/working-papers/voth_longestyears.pdf)). Those acts were narrow. From 1833 to 1874 they capped hours only for children, young persons and women, and only in textiles until the extension acts of 1864 to 1867. The legal textile week ran 69 hours under the 1825 structure, 63 from July 1847, 58 from May 1848, back up to 60 under the 1850 compromise and down to 56.5 in 1874 (UK, Plausible for the sequence, 56.5 Verified in Webb and Cox 1891) ([Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts), [Webb and Cox 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt)). Adult men were never directly covered before the 1908 Coal Mines Regulation Act set eight hours below ground (UK, Plausible) ([Wikipedia Coal Mines Regulation Act 1908](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_Mines_Regulation_Act_1908)).

The whole-economy series shows how little those acts moved the average. Weekly hours of full-time production workers in Britain were **56.9 in 1870, 56.0 in 1913 and 47.0 in 1929** (UK, Verified) ([Huberman and Minns 2007](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)). The nine-hour fall between 1913 and 1929 happened with no adult hours statute and no ratified ILO convention. The government told the Commons on 28 April 1932 that Britain "maintains in practice a normal working week of 48 hours or less" without ratifying the Washington Convention (UK, 1932, Verified) ([Hansard HC Deb 28 April 1932](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1932/apr/28/washington-hours-convention)). Collective bargaining, not law, did the work in Britain.

The United States followed the same pattern with more paperwork. New Hampshire in 1847 and Pennsylvania in 1848 declared ten hours the legal day but let contracts set any length, so the laws "had little impact" (US, Verified) ([Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)). Massachusetts passed the first enforceable ten-hour law in 1874, covering only women at 60 hours a week (US, Verified). By 1920 the share of states with maximum-hours laws had reached 76 percent, yet only **12 percent of employees were covered and the average legal cap was 56.7 hours** when manufacturing already averaged 51.2 (US, 1920, Verified). Whaples' city-level regressions for 1914 to 1919 attribute about half the fall in hours to real wages rising over 18 percent, about a fifth to wartime immigration restriction and about a seventh to unionisation, with state maximum-hours laws showing little measured effect once other factors are included (US, Verified). A 1995 survey found over 80 percent of US economic historians agreeing that the pre-Depression decline in manufacturing hours was "primarily due to economic growth and the increased wages it brought" (US, Verified) ([Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)). Large firms moved first: US Steel cut its day from twelve to eight hours in 1923 and Ford adopted the five-day week in 1926 for more than half of about 400,000 workers, arguing that "we can get at least as great production in five days as we can in six" (US, Verified for Ford's adoption, Plausible for the quote) ([Wikisource, Ford 1926](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Henry_Ford:_Why_I_Favor_Five_Days%27_Work_With_Six_Days%27_Pay)).

The Fair Labor Standards Act, signed 25 June 1938, set a 44-hour maximum in covered industries employing about one fifth of the labour force (US, Verified) ([US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)). Production workers already averaged 37.3 hours that year (US, 1938, Verified), so on average the cap bound almost nobody. It did bind at the tail. Costa's comparison of covered wholesale trade with uncovered retail trade finds that "a 5% reduction in the standard workweek cut the share of men and women working more than 40 hours per week by at least 18%", that employers offset part of the cost by trimming straight-time wages, and that "the hours reductions did not produce higher employment" (US, 1938 to 1950, Verified) ([Costa, NBER WP 6855](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6855)). That is the signature of hours law in the Anglophone world: it compresses the top of the distribution and leaves the mean to wages.

France and Germany look different, and the arithmetic says why. Huberman and Minns estimate a wage elasticity of weekly hours of about minus 0.11 to minus 0.12 across their 1870 to 1900 panel, so a 10 percent wage rise bought roughly 1 percent fewer hours (14 countries, Verified) ([Huberman and Minns 2007](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)). On that elasticity a 20 percent cut in hours would need wages to double. France cut 14 hours a week between 1913 and 1929 and Germany cut nine between 1913 and 1919. No plausible wage path explains that. Law and bargaining do.

France's history also shows the limit of legislating ahead of politics. The decree of 2 March 1848 cut the adult day to ten hours in Paris and eleven in the provinces and was revoked on 9 September 1848 with the maximum reset to twelve (France, Plausible) ([Wikipedia Reduction of working hours in France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_of_working_hours_in_France)). The 1919 law, eight hours a day and 48 a week with full wage compensation, held because employers and unions had both signed up (France, Plausible) ([fr.wikipedia Durée du travail en France](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dur%C3%A9e_du_travail_en_France)). The forty-hour law of June 1936, passed 408 to 160 in the Chamber and cutting the week from 48 to 40 "sans diminution de salaire", is the one pre-war case with modern econometric evidence, and the evidence is unkind: "Deflation ended but output stagnated," with the 40-hour law named as a contributor to "French stagflation" (France, 1936 to 1938, Verified from the abstract) ([Cohen-Setton, Hausman and Wieland, NBER WP 22140](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22140)). Germany's eight-hour order of 23 November 1918 was decreed eight days after the Stinnes-Legien agreement between employers and unions had promised it (Germany, Plausible) ([de.wikipedia Achtstundentag](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achtstundentag)). Weekly hours in industry held at 48 from 1919 to 1923, then rose to about 50.4 in 1924 after the December 1923 ordinance let collective agreements reopen the ten-hour day (Germany, Plausible) ([Statista](https://de.statista.com/statistik/daten/studie/1081110/umfrage/woechentliche-arbeitszeit-in-der-industrie-in-der-weimarer-republik)). A legal eight hours lasted exactly as long as the coalition behind it.

The ILO was weak as a direct lever and strong as a focal point. Of the four countries, only France ratified Convention 1 of 1919, in 1927 and conditionally, and none ratified the Forty-Hour Week Convention of 1935 before 1940 (Plausible) ([Wikipedia Hours of Work Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hours_of_Work_(Industry)_Convention,_1919), [Wikipedia Forty-Hour Week Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-Hour_Week_Convention,_1935)). Yet by 1929 the UK at 47.0, the US at 48.0, France at 48.0 and Germany at 46.0 had converged on the eight-hour day by four different routes (Verified). Huberman and Minns put the regional split plainly: in Europe "labor power came to represent the voice of the average worker and legislation to reduce the work day had the effect of narrowing wage gaps," while in the New World "market forces determined outcomes" (Verified) ([Huberman and Minns 2007](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)).

The economists of the period disagreed about mechanism, not direction. Mill in 1848 described a nine-hour day as a coordination problem that might need "an engagement under penalty" enforced by law, while declining to endorse one (UK, Verified) ([Mill, Principles Book V](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mill/mlP73.html)). Marshall accepted shorter hours only where expensive plant could run in shifts and rejected the "permanent fixed work-fund" idea that less work means more jobs (UK, 1890, Verified) ([Marshall, Principles Book VI](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)). Keynes in 1930 expected "a fifteen-hour week" within a century and worried that "to those who sweat for their daily bread leisure is a longed-for sweet, until they get it" (UK, Verified) ([Keynes 1930](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm)). Marshall's work-fund critique is the one that aged best. The share-the-work laws of the 1930s, from the Black thirty-hour bill that passed the Senate 53 to 30 in April 1933 to the FLSA, were sold as job creation, and the measured record (Costa for the US, Cohen-Setton and colleagues for France) finds hours falling and employment not rising.

## After 1980 Europe converted productivity into time and America into income

Every country in scope cut annual hours per worker sharply between 1950 and 1980. The divergence came after.

The US series is flat for forty years: **1,840 hours in 1980, 1,888 in 2000, 1,797 in 2019** on the PWT basis (US, Verified). Over the same window Germany fell from 1,756 to 1,372 and France from 1,776 to 1,504 (Verified). The US did not legislate shorter hours after 1940 and let its one hours lever erode. The white-collar salary threshold below which the overtime premium applies was set at $155 a week in 1975 and not moved until 2004 (US, Verified) ([CRS RL32088](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL32088.html)). The two attempts to lift it substantially, to $47,476 a year in 2016 and to $58,656 a year from January 2025, were both vacated by the Eastern District of Texas, so the line in force in November 2026 is the 2019 figure of $35,568 (US, Verified) ([JD Supra on Texas v DOL](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/federal-judge-blocks-dol-rule-raising-8983455)). Four explanations compete for the US to Europe gap and the numbers can be stacked. Prescott's tax model fits the 1993 to 1996 cross-section within about one hour a week, with effective marginal labour tax rates of 0.59 in Germany and France against 0.40 in the US (Verified) ([Prescott, NBER w10316](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10316/w10316.pdf)). Alesina, Glaeser and Sacerdote reply that tax effects disappear once unionisation or regulation is controlled for, that Americans work 46.2 weeks a year against 40.5 in France, and that "legally mandated holidays can explain 80 percent of the difference in weeks worked between the U.S. and Europe and 30 percent of the difference in total labor supply" (US and Europe, 2005, Verified) ([NBER w11278](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11278/w11278.pdf)). Blanchard's summary is that Europe "has used some of the increase in productivity to increase leisure rather than income" (Verified) ([Blanchard, NBER w10310](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10310)). Aguiar and Hurst add the measurement point that US leisure rose 6 to 8 hours a week for men and 4 to 8 for women between 1965 and 2003 even as market hours per working-age adult stayed flat, because women entered paid work as men left it (US, Verified) ([Aguiar and Hurst, NBER w12082](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12082)). Using harmonised labour force surveys, Bick and colleagues put the Europe to US hours gap at 19 percent, with weeks worked and educational composition each explaining a third to a half (Verified) ([Bick et al, IZA DP 10179](https://docs.iza.org/dp10179.pdf)).

France ran the largest statutory experiment of the post-war period twice. The 1982 ordinance cut the legal week from 40 to 39 hours and generalised a fifth week of paid leave (France, Plausible) ([Larousse archive 1982](https://www.larousse.fr/archives/journaux_annee/1982/44/relations_sociales)). The Aubry laws set 35 hours from January to February 2000 for firms over 20 employees and from 2002 for the rest (France, Plausible) ([Assemblée nationale written question](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/13/questions/QANR5L13QE111830.pdf)). The firm-level evaluation by Crépon, Leclair and Roux finds that firms moving to 35 hours cut the week by about **10.2 percent, lost 3.7 percent of total factor productivity, and grew employment 9.9 percent faster** than firms still at 39 hours, with the authors attributing the hiring to payroll tax relief and wage moderation rather than work sharing (France, 2000, Verified) ([Crépon, Leclair, Roux](https://ideas.repec.org/a/prs/ecstat/estat_0336-1454_2004_num_376_1_7234.html)). The official DARES estimate of about 350,000 jobs created or preserved over 1996 to 2002 sits well below the ex-ante range of 350,000 to 700,000 and includes the earlier Robien scheme (France, Verified via IRES) ([Lerais, IRES 2014](https://ires.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Effets_des_35_heures_octobre_2014_final.pdf)). The Alsace-Moselle natural experiment, where two extra local holidays made the law less binding, shows "no significant difference in employment with the rest of France" (France, Verified) ([Chemin and Wasmer](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/journl/hal-03461753.html)), and Estevão and Sá find dual job holding rose 3.3 percentage points as workers routed around the cap (France, Verified) ([Estevão and Sá, IZA DP 2459](https://docs.iza.org/dp2459.pdf)). The honest reading is that about two thirds of the hours cut was absorbed by higher output per hour, a sixth or so became jobs, and the rest was paid for by the state and by wage restraint.

Germany took the bargaining route and then invented the one hours policy that clearly protected jobs. IG Metall won a staged cut from 40 to 38.5 hours after a seven-week strike in 1984, to 37 in 1987 and to 35 from 1 October 1995 (West Germany, Plausible) ([IG Metall milestones](https://www.igmetall.de/ueber-uns/ig-metall--a-strong-community/what-we-have-already-achieved-our-milestones)). Hunt's evaluation of 1984 to 1989 finds each one-hour cut in standard hours reduced total hours by 2 to 3 percent and raised employment by only 0.3 to 0.7 percent, with the wage bill rising (Germany, Verified) ([Hunt, NBER w5724](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5724)). Kurzarbeit is the opposite case, a scheme that cuts hours to keep heads. Cyclical short-time workers peaked at 1.516 million in May 2009 with an average hours loss of 31 percent (Germany, Verified) ([IAB Stellungnahme 2/2010](https://doku.iab.de/stellungnahme/2010/sn0210.pdf)) and at 6.83 million in April 2020 (Germany, Verified) ([Bundesregierung, June 2020](https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/suche/arbeitsmarkt-juni-2020-1765548)). Counterfactual estimates for 2009 range from 0.7 to 0.8 percent of jobs saved to about 400,000 (Verified) ([Cahuc, IZA World of Labor](https://wol.iza.org/articles/short-time-work-compensations-and-employment/long)), and the 2.2 million figure for 2020 is an extrapolation from hours data, an upper bound rather than a measured effect (Verified as reported) ([IamExpat, May 2021](https://www.iamexpat.de/career/employment-news/study-germanys-kurzarbeit-scheme-saved-22-million-jobs)).

The Nordics bargained rather than legislated, and their short hours are mostly long holidays and part-time work rather than a falling trend. Denmark moved from 40 to 37 hours in the late 1980s through a social-partner compromise aimed at curbing wage growth (Denmark, Verified) ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/spls/10/1/10_62/_article/-char/en)), and 37 is still the norm. Sweden's hours per worker rose from 1,370 in 1980 to 1,489 in 2000 (Sweden, Verified), the opposite of the rest of Europe, as very high part-time shares among women shifted toward longer hours. Gothenburg's six-hour-day trial at the Svartedalen care home needed 17 extra staff at about 12 million kronor to cover a 25 percent cut in hours, cut sick leave among trial staff while the control home's rose, and was ended because the city judged it too expensive to scale (Sweden, 2015 to 2017, Verified) ([Irish Times, April 2017](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/economy/how-the-six-hour-working-day-can-actually-save-money-1.3052533)). Where output is hours of attendance, there is no productivity offset to find.

Britain's one post-war hours law has a hole in it. The Working Time Regulations 1998 cap average weekly working time at 48 hours over a 17-week reference period, and regulation 5 lets any worker agree in writing that the cap does not apply (UK, Verified) ([legislation.gov.uk, reg 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1998/1833/regulation/5/made)). In 2003 the government said about 3.8 million workers exceeded 48 hours and admitted "there is no central record kept of how many workers in the UK who have signed such agreements" (UK, Verified) ([Hansard, 20 May 2003](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/2003/may/20/working-hours-2)). UK annual hours show no step in 1998 to 2000.

East Asia legislated late and hard, and the series moved where the law was enforced. Japan's 1947 Act set a 48-hour week, the 1987 revision cut the standard to 40 with implementation from 1988 and full effect by 1994 for most and 1997 for all (Japan, Verified) ([Hamermesh, Kawaguchi and Lee](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20398/w20398.pdf)). Japanese annual hours fell from 2,093 in 1985 to 1,884 in 1995 (Japan, Verified), about the size of the cut after allowing for overtime. The 2018 Work Style Reform law then put the first legal cap on overtime, 45 hours a month and 360 a year, with exceptional ceilings of 720 a year, under 100 in any month and an 80-hour average over two to six months, the last being the health ministry's karoshi risk line (Japan, Verified) ([Japan Labor Issues 2018](https://www.jil.go.jp/english/jli/documents/2018/010-01.pdf)). Before it, employers could sign special agreements requiring unlimited overtime (Japan, Verified) ([Proskauer 2018](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/japans-labor-reform-caps-overtime-in-a-bid-to-curb-karoshi)). In 2016, 20.8 percent of Japanese employees worked 49 or more hours a week against 16.4 percent in the US, 10.1 in France and 9.6 in Germany (Verified, secondary) ([HKS Student Policy Review](https://studentreview.hks.harvard.edu/karoshi-and-japans-work-style-reform/)).

Korea cut about 960 annual hours per worker between 1980 and 2022, the largest fall of any country in scope, in two legislated steps. The 2004 reform cut the statutory week from 44 to 40, phased by firm size from July 2004 to 2011 (Korea, Verified) ([Legal500 reprint](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/south-korea/employment-and-hr/government-overhauls-labour-standards-act)). The 2018 reform cut the effective maximum from 68 to 52 hours, binding from July 2018 for firms with 300 or more employees, January 2020 for 50 to 299 and July 2021 for 5 to 49 (Korea, Verified). Its evaluation shows a cap working at the margin: the incidence of working over 52 hours fell 1.3 percentage points, a tenth of its pre-reform level, while 4.7 points of workers moved into the 41 to 52 band (Korea, Verified) ([Carcillo, Hijzen, Thewissen](https://docs.iza.org/dp16023.pdf)). Among large firms the over-52 share fell about five points, a fifth, and two in five workers remain exempt (Korea, Verified) ([Hijzen and Thewissen](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oec:elsaab:248-en)). A March 2023 plan to allow 69-hour weeks by averaging overtime over longer periods was withdrawn within eight months after a government survey of 6,030 people, and the ministry confirmed on 13 November 2023 that "the government has decided to maintain the current 52-hour workweek system" (Korea, Verified) ([Korea Times, 13 November 2023](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/www/nation/2024/11/113_363107.html)).

China is the country where the statute and the clock have never met. The State Council regulation of 1995 set eight hours a day and 40 a week from 1 May 1995 (China, Verified) ([Lehman Law translation](https://lehmanlaw.com/resource-centre/laws-and-regulations/labor/state-council-regulations-on-working-hours-of-employees-1995.html)). On 26 August 2021 the Supreme People's Court and the labour ministry published ten model cases declaring the 996 schedule unlawful (China, Verified) ([Sixth Tone, 27 August 2021](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1008375)). Measured weekly hours of enterprise employees were 47.9 in 2022 and about 49 in 2023 and 2024 (China, Verified) ([China Daily, March 2025](https://workinchina.chinadaily.com.cn/s/202503/20/WS67dbc325498eec7e1f732540/chinese-firms-remind-employees-not-to-unnecessarily-work-long-hours.html)), and the PWT series rose from 2,243 hours a year in 1980 to 2,357 in 2019 (China, Verified). The 1995 rule changed the legal standard and the 2021 cases changed the enforcement signal. Neither has yet shown up as a fall in the series.

## Workers kept most of the hour's output until 2000, then the labour share slid

The question of who kept the gain has two answers depending on the window. Over the long run, most of the productivity gain reached workers. The ILO finds that between 2004 and 2024 global labour productivity per hour rose 58 percent and labour income per hour rose 53 percent, with the labour income share falling 1.6 percentage points over the two decades, 0.6 of them since 2019 (global, Verified) ([ILO WESO September 2024 update](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/WESO%20September%202024%20Update%20-%20Final.pdf)). Feldstein finds US nonfarm output per hour grew about 1.9 percent a year over 1970 to 2006 and real compensation per hour about 1.7 percent when deflated with the same price index, with employee compensation at 66 percent of national income in 1970 and 64 percent in 2006, "remarkably stable" (US, Verified) ([NBER Digest, October 2008](https://www.nber.org/digest/oct08/total-compensation-reflects-growth-productivity)).

The break is after 2000, and it is sharpest in three countries. The IMF puts the labour share in advanced economies "almost 4 percentage points lower" in 2015 than in 1970, with "about half" of the decline traced to technology and about a quarter to global integration (advanced economies, Verified) ([IMF blog, April 2017](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2017/04/12/drivers-of-declining-labor-share-of-income)). The OECD's 2018 chapter finds a 3.5-point fall across 24 countries between 1995 and 2013 with the US down about 8 points while France, Italy and the UK were flat or rising (Plausible, OECD site blocked) ([OECD Employment Outlook 2018](https://doi.org/10.1787/empl_outlook-2018-en)). The BLS nonfarm business labour share, about 65 percent in 1947 and 62.8 percent at the end of 2000, fell to **52.8 percent in the second quarter of 2026, "the lowest level ever recorded"** (US, Verified) ([BLS TED, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm)). Japan is the only rich country where wages fell outright: real wages down about 1 percent while productivity rose 30 percent over 1995 to 2017, as non-regular workers rose from about 15 percent of the labour force in the 1980s to nearly 40 percent (Japan, Verified) ([Katz, Toyo Keizai, December 2021](https://toyokeizai.net/articles/print-tbt/476082)). The French Treasury's cross-country reading is that in Germany, the US and Japan part of the distributable surplus went to capital from the mid-1990s, while in France "the entire surplus has gone to workers' hourly compensation" and in the UK the share increased (Verified) ([Trésor-Economics 234](https://www.tresor.economie.gouv.fr/Articles/2019/01/17/tresor-economics-no-234-the-change-in-the-labour-share-in-value-added-in-advanced-economies)). The UK rise, from 53.8 percent in 1996 to 59.5 percent in 2023, is a compensation-mix effect: employers' social contributions grew about 340 percent against 220 percent for wages, driven by pension auto-enrolment and employer National Insurance, so a rising share did not mean workers kept more of the hour's output as cash (UK, Verified) ([ONS, November 2024](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/economicoutputandproductivity/output/articles/trendsintheuklabourshare1997to2023/2024-11-25)). China's official share fell 12.45 points between 1995 and 2007, though 5.25 points of that came from a 2004 change in how the statistics bureau classified self-employed income (China, Verified from the abstract) ([Bai and Qian 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:chieco:v:21:y:2010:i:4:p:650-670)).

The US productivity-pay dispute is largely about measurement, and both camps agree on the direction after 2000. EPI's series shows nonsupervisory productivity up 80.9 percent and hourly pay up 29.4 percent over 1979 to 2024 (US, Verified as reported in Fortune, Plausible as EPI's own figure) ([Fortune, November 2024](https://fortune.com/2024/11/10/american-worker-labor-trump/)). Feldstein's answer is that consistent deflators and total compensation close most of the gap through 2006. Lawrence's reading of the post-2000 fall is labour-augmenting technology with capital and labour as complements, which implies that "increased capital formation would raise labor's share" (US, Verified) ([NBER w21296](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21296)). Autor and colleagues find the fall came mainly from output shifting toward high-profit, low-labour-share "superstar" firms rather than from falling shares within firms (US, Verified) ([NBER w23396](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23396)).

Three policy levers decide how much of a saved hour becomes time, pay or output, and the evidence on each is consistent. Overtime premiums cut hours but firms claw part of it back. Trejo finds wage differentials "do arise to mitigate the purely demand-driven effects" of overtime rules but are "not large enough to neutralize overtime pay regulation completely" (US, 1991, Verified) ([Trejo, AER](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:81:y:1991:i:4:p:719-40)), and Hamermesh and Trejo find California's daily overtime penalty "substantially reduced" men's daily overtime, implying "a price elasticity of demand for overtime hours of at least -0.5" (US, Verified) ([Hamermesh and Trejo, REStat](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:82:y:2000:i:1:p:38-47)). Minimum wages adjust on the hours margin first where hours data exist. Seattle's move to $13 in 2016 "reduced hours worked in low-wage jobs by 6-7 percent" while hourly wages rose 3 percent, lowering pay by about $74 a month per job (US, 2016, Verified) ([Jardim et al, NBER w23532](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532)), even as the 138-increase national study found "the overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged" (US, 1979 to 2016, Verified) ([Cengiz et al, NBER w25434](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434)). Paid leave is the lever that explains most of the US to Europe gap, and it is purely statutory and bargained.

The table also shows where the statute does not bind. Japan grants 10 to 20 statutory days plus 19 public holidays, yet private-sector employees were granted 17.6 days and took 10.9 in 2022, a take-up rate of 62.1 percent and the highest on record (Japan, Verified) ([HRM Asia, 2023](https://hrmasia.com/employees-in-japan-take-more-paid-leave-in-2022/)). Pension age is the one lever that every government in scope except Korea has pulled since 2000, and it moves saved time toward output by design. France's 2023 law raises the legal retirement age by three months per cohort to reach 64 in 2030 and lengthens the full-rate contribution period to 43 years from 2027 (France, Verified) ([La finance pour tous, April 2023](https://www.lafinancepourtous.com/2023/04/18/reforme-des-retraites-promulgation-de-la-loi-le-14-avril-2023/)). Germany's 2007 law lifts the standard age from 65 to 67 in stages that complete for the 1964 cohort, with section 35 of the social code now reading "Die Regelaltersgrenze wird mit Vollendung des 67. Lebensjahres erreicht" (Germany, Verified) ([dejure.org, section 35 SGB VI](https://dejure.org/gesetze/SGB_VI/35.html)). Japan's pension eligibility age "is gradually being raised from 60 to 65," with completion for men by the fiscal year ending March 2026 and women by March 2031 (Japan, Verified for the direction, Plausible for the end dates) ([JILPT chapter 15](https://www.jil.go.jp/english/lsj/detailed/2005-2006/chapter15.pdf)). China's NPC Standing Committee decided on 13 September 2024 to raise men's retirement age from 60 to 63 and women's from 50 or 55 to 55 or 58 over 15 years from 1 January 2025, and to lift the minimum contribution period from 15 to 20 years from 2030 (China, Verified) ([NPC Observer, September 2024](https://npcobserver.com/2024/09/china-npc-raise-retirement-age/)). Korea's statutory retirement age has been 60 since 2016, bills to lift it to 65 by 2033 were still in committee in December 2025, and the Bank of Korea's estimate that each retained older worker may eliminate 0.4 to 1.5 youth jobs is the number employers use against them (Korea, Verified) ([Korea Times, 9 November 2025](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/politics/20251109/debate-heats-up-over-extending-retirement-age-to-65)). As arithmetic illustration only, a two-year rise at 2024 annual hours adds roughly 2,980 lifetime hours per worker in France, 2,660 in Germany, 3,230 in Japan and 3,730 in Korea, ignoring lower participation at older ages. The 2023 French and 2024 Chinese reforms both came with longer contribution periods, so the state takes a larger share of the lifetime hour in both cases.

Across the ten countries in scope, public social spending runs from 15.3 percent of GDP in Korea to 31.4 percent in Finland (2021 to 2024, Plausible) ([Wikipedia, social welfare spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_social_welfare_spending)) and annual hours from 1,865 in Korea to 1,331 in Germany (2024, Verified). The correlation across those ten pairs is about minus 0.75, roughly 27 fewer hours a year per extra point of GDP, a descriptive pattern on ten observations rather than an estimate. Paid leave, parental leave and earlier pensions are all public spending that buys time out of the market. The Prescott and Alesina debate is about whether the tax that funds that spending or the regulation that comes with it is the channel.

## AI policy in 2023 to 2026 measures saved minutes and leaves them with employers

Three things are true at once as of November 2026. The shorter-week trials that reported results all found output held and sickness fell. No national government legislated on the back of them. And every government that measured AI time savings in its own workforce stopped short of asking whether hours fell.

The pattern in the table is that governments treat positive results as grounds for flexibility, not for cutting hours. Scotland, France's state civil service experiment, Tokyo and Belgium all chose compression or optional schemes that leave contracted hours unchanged. Where hours were cut, the cut was 8 to 20 percent and the scheme was local, voluntary or subsidised. Voluntary rights with no pay or hours change get almost no uptake, as Belgium's 0.8 percent shows. None of the trials involved AI or measured whether automation created the slack.

The political positions are mostly against. The UK government told councils in October 2023 that "removing 20% of a local authority's potential capacity does not offer value for money for residents" and that they "should cease immediately" (UK, Verified) ([gov.uk, 26 October 2023](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/government-calls-time-on-councils-running-four-day-weeks)). The Labour government stopped pressing South Cambridgeshire in November 2024 but said a four-day week on full pay is not government policy (UK, Plausible), dropped the promised "right to switch off" from the Employment Rights Bill in March 2025 (UK, Plausible) ([Startups.co.uk](https://startups.co.uk/news/right-to-switch-off-scrapped/)), and passed an Employment Rights Act in December 2025 with no AI, hours or monitoring provisions, though a consultation on workplace monitoring technologies closed on 30 September 2026 (UK, Verified) ([GOV.UK consultation](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/make-work-pay-workplace-monitoring-technologies)). Germany's coalition is moving from a daily to a weekly maximum, with a working draft leaked in June 2026 that keeps the eight-hour day as the rule, allows deviation by collective agreement and adds electronic time recording, over union objections (Germany, Verified, secondary) ([DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/germany-prepares-shift-to-weekly-working-time-limits)). Sweden's LO wants a 35-hour week in the 2027 bargaining round and the employers' confederation has refused to put hours on the table, with both sides agreeing hours belong to social partners rather than statute (Sweden, Verified) ([Nordic Labour Journal, April 2026](https://www.nordiclabourjournal.org/five-decades-on-sweden-reopens-the-fight-over-working-hours/)). In the US, Sanders and Takano reintroduced the Thirty-Two Hour Workweek Act on 8 September 2026, cutting the FLSA standard from 40 to 32 hours "with no loss in pay or benefits," with Sanders citing a moment "when artificial intelligence and robotics will radically transform our economy" (US, Verified) ([Capitol Releases, 8 September 2026](https://capitolreleases.com/releases/617f6d35-ad6b-47d4-9142-5aad3a59d9aa)). No bill number, cosponsor count or committee action was found (Not found).

Korea is the exception, and its design is instructive. President Lee's roadmap, announced 17 September 2025, runs in three stages: pass a Reduced Working Hours Support Act and launch pilots in 2025, prohibit widespread use of the comprehensive wage system that folds overtime into salary in 2026, and open a nationwide debate on extending the 4.5-day week from 2027 (Korea, Verified) ([Korea Times, 19 September 2025](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/politics/20250919/govt-seeks-passage-of-45-day-workweek-bill-this-year-despite-industry-pushback)). The target is actual annual hours in the "1,700-hour range" by 2030, from 1,865 in 2024 and 1,833 in 2025 (Korea, Verified) ([Financial News, 5 July 2026](https://en.fnnews.com/news/202607051045555290)). The instrument is a subsidy, 200,000 to 600,000 won per worker per month for adoption and 600,000 to 800,000 won per replacement hire in the 2026 draft budget (Korea, Verified) ([JILPT Korea topics, November 2025](https://www.jil.go.jp/foreign/jihou/2025/11/korea_01.html)). It is the one instrument in scope that explicitly pays firms for the cost of shorter hours, and its enabling law was still stalled in the National Assembly in mid-2026 (Korea, Verified). The Korea Employers Federation's reply is that hourly productivity was $54.60 in 2023, 77.4 percent of the OECD average and below the G7's $80.60, so "simply reducing statutory working hours will undermine corporate competitiveness" (Korea, Verified) ([Korea Herald, 29 September 2025](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10585955)).

Japan and China frame AI as a labour supply problem, not a leisure dividend. METI's revised 2040 employment projection, published March 2026, expects a shortage of 3.39 million people able to use AI and robotics against a surplus of 4.37 million clerical workers, with the workforce falling from about 67.06 million in 2022 to about 63.03 million in 2040 and AI and robots substituting for about 2 million people's labour (Japan, Verified in a practitioner blog citing METI, secondary) ([ITmedia, August 2026](https://blogs.itmedia.co.jp/taps/2026/08/2040437ai339----.html)). Tokyo's four-day option from April 2025 keeps 155 hours per four-week period, so it compresses rather than cuts (Japan, Plausible) ([Human Resources Online](https://www.humanresourcesonline.net/tokyo-to-introduce-a-four-day-workweek-and-flexible-working-hours-for-government-staff-from-april-2025)). China's 15th Five-Year Plan employment chapter, running 2026 to 2030, sets unemployment within 5.5 percent and 50 million subsidised training places and says nothing about hours, rest or leave (China, Verified) ([Xinhua via Henan Government, July 2026](https://english.henan.gov.cn/2026/07-10/3374443.html)). The labour ministry said in January 2026 it is drafting a document on AI's effect on the job market (China, Verified) ([China Daily, 27 January 2026](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202601/27/WS69781edaa310d6866eb35ec2.html)), and the pressure on hours comes from firms: Midea told staff to leave by 6:20 pm and banned "performative overtime" in January 2025 and Haier moved to a five-day week, while official data showed 49.1 hours a week in January 2025 (China, Verified) ([Reuters via Tuoi Tre, April 2025](https://news.tuoitre.vn/in-china-whispers-of-change-as-some-companies-tell-staff-to-work-less-103250409105527749.htm)). China is also the only government that forced a change in algorithmic scheduling intensity, through guidance that led Meituan to pilot automated rest prompts and forced log-off for riders exceeding cumulative working time in December 2024 (China, Verified) ([TechNode, December 2024](https://technode.com/2024/12/18/meituan-pilots-policy-to-log-off-overworked-delivery-riders-automatically/)).

The binding AI-at-work rules govern hiring, firing, monitoring and scheduling fairness, never the quantity of work. The EU AI Act's high-risk duties for employment uses were pushed from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus that entered into force on 27 July 2026 (EU, Verified, secondary) ([Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/european-union/technology/eu-digital-omnibus-on-ai-enters-into-force)). The Platform Work Directive's transposition deadline is 2 December 2026 (EU, Verified) ([Ius Laboris](https://www.iuslaboris.com/insights/eu-platform-work-directive-which-countries-have-implemented/)). New York City's bias audit law has been in force since July 2023, and the State Comptroller's audit of December 2025 found the city's enforcement "ineffective," with auditors finding 17 likely non-compliance instances where the agency found one (US, Verified) ([NY State Comptroller](https://www.osc.ny.gov/press/releases/2025/12/dinapoli-new-yorkers-deserve-transparent-hiring-process-when-artificial-intelligence-used-vet-their)). Colorado repealed its AI Act before it took effect and replaced it with a disclosure law from 1 January 2027 (US, Verified, secondary) ([Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/what-to-know-about-colorados-repeal-and-replacement-of-its-landmark-ai-act/)). Korea's AI Basic Act took effect 22 January 2026 with fines deferred at least a year (Korea, Verified) ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)) and Japan's AI Promotion Act of June 2025 contains no penalties (Japan, Verified) ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)). The regulatory centre of gravity has moved from risk assessment to disclosure, and none of it touches hours.

No government has linked its own AI savings to any change in staff hours, overtime or leave (Not found anywhere). The self-reported headlines are larger than the controlled findings, adoption rather than capability is the binding constraint, and the Australian Treasury's framing, 13 minutes a week redirected to justify a licence, treats the saved minute as employer property by default. That default is the policy. It was not legislated, it was assumed.

The income-sharing side of the AI debate is better measured than the hours side, and the measured effects are small. Seven cash pilots in five countries give a labour supply range from a small positive to a small negative. OpenResearch's $1,000 a month for three years found recipients worked **1.3 fewer hours a week** and were about 2 percentage points less likely to be employed in years two and three, while employment still rose from 58 to 72 percent among recipients against 59 to 74 percent among controls (US, 2020 to 2023, Verified) ([OpenResearch employment findings](https://openresearchlab.org/findings/key-findings-employment-and-income)). Finland's 560 euros a month for 2,000 unemployed people raised employment by six days to an average of 78 over the year (Finland, 2017 to 2018, Verified) ([Finland Toolbox](https://toolbox.finland.fi/?p=3883)). Wales' £1,600 a month for 644 care leavers reports "varied" qualitative effects on work and publishes quantitative outcomes in 2027 (UK, Verified) ([Welsh Government report, February 2026](https://www.gov.wales/sites/default/files/statistics-and-research/2026-02/basic-income-care-leavers-wales-pilot-evaluation-annual-report-2025-2026-summary-380041.pdf)). On those numbers a cash dividend alone frees 3 to 5 percent of a working week at most. Of the AI-specific sharing proposals, only Altman's has rates: a 2.5 percent annual levy on large companies payable in shares plus 2.5 percent on privately held land, estimated at about $13,500 per adult a year a decade on (US, 2021, Proposal, Verified) ([moores.samaltman.com](https://moores.samaltman.com/)). Sanders' June 2026 AI sovereign wealth fund names no percentages (US, Proposal, Verified) ([Baton Rouge Business Report](https://www.businessreport.com/article/bernie-sanders-proposes-national-ai-wealth-fund)). The optimal robot tax in the literature is 1 to 3.7 percent of value, from Costinot and Werning, and it falls as robots saturate the economy (US, 2022, Projection, Verified) ([MIT News, December 2022](https://economics.mit.edu/news/should-we-tax-robots-0)). The only enacted "robot tax" anywhere was Korea's 2017 cut of two points to an automation investment deduction, "certainly not a full-on robot tax" (Korea, Law, Verified) ([MIT Technology Review, August 2017](https://www.technologyreview.com/2017/08/09/242207/south-korea-is-decreasing-tax-breaks-on-automation-and-thats-probably-a-bad-idea)), and the European Parliament rejected a robot tax in February 2017 (EU, Verified) ([AK Europa](https://www.akeuropa.eu/en/first-proposal-rules-robots-still-robot-tax-rejected-parliament)). The IMF's 2024 fiscal note tells governments to prepare for "both business as usual and highly disruptive scenarios" and, per press coverage, rejects a special AI tax in favour of stronger capital income taxes and unemployment insurance (international, Verified for the quote, Plausible for the detail) ([IMF SDN 2024/002](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:imf:imfsdn:2024/002)).

The challenges are not equal and they interact. Rebound is the weakest on numbers: the sustainability literature agrees the effect exists and no source quantifies the share of saved time that refills, so the usable evidence is the gap between statute and practice in Japan, China and Korea. Baumol and the Swedish care trial are the strongest on numbers, and they cut hardest against an economy-wide statutory week because the sectors that would have to hire most are the ones where AI saves the least. The political economy and ageing challenges reinforce each other: Japan and Korea most need productivity, their employer federations hold the strongest fiscal argument against hours cuts, and their governments are lifting pension ages while debating shorter weeks. The measurement challenge cuts against the simplest design, a uniform cut, more than against hours reduction in general. It points toward the instruments already on the books in small form: Belgium's time credit, which the 2012 reform cut from five years to the equivalent of one year across a career (Belgium, Verified) ([Mind, July 2012](https://www.mind.eu.com/rh/en/article/belgium-social-partners-agree-to-reform-time-credit/)), Germany's working-life time accounts from the 2000 IG Metall agreement, reported as low uptake (Germany, Plausible), the UK's day-one right to request flexible working from 6 April 2024 (UK, Verified) ([SI 2024/438](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2024/438/made)), and Korea's per-worker subsidy. None of these moves the average. All of them move the tail, which is what hours law has always done.

## Conclusion

The two centuries of evidence say something more specific than "law followed" or "law led." Hours law works at the long-hours tail and only when a coalition of employers, unions and the state has already agreed the standard, and it holds exactly as long as that coalition holds. Britain and America reached the eight-hour day by bargaining and wages, France and Germany by decree with employer consent, Japan and Korea by statute enforced against a culture of overtime, and China by statute never enforced. In every case the mean moved with wages and bargaining power, and the statute capped the outliers. The one post-1980 break, the US plateau at 1,800 hours while Europe kept converting productivity into holidays, was a policy choice too, made by letting the overtime threshold rot for 29 years and never legislating leave. The AI era so far repeats the pattern in miniature. Firms and governments measure minutes saved, keep them by default, and write rules about fairness in hiring rather than about the quantity of work. The only government paying firms to cut hours is Korea, and its enabling law is stalled.

What this implies for the next fifty years is that the saved hour will be decided at the firm and at the pension office, not in the hours statute. Pension ages are the laws actually moving, and they move time toward output. The firm-level trials show the hours cut is cheap where output is knowledge and expensive where output is attendance, so any dividend that reaches workers will be uneven by sector and will widen the leisure gap Aguiar and Hurst already found. The instruments that fit that reality are the ones that let a worker bank and spend time across a life, that pay firms for the transition where productivity is thin, and that make the ownership of a saved minute an explicit term of employment rather than an assumption. The test cases to watch are Korea's 2026 to 2027 pilots, Germany's weekly-maximum bill, the EU Quality Jobs Act, and whether any government, anywhere, measures hours worked after AI arrives in its own offices. Until one does, the right answer to "who keeps the saved hour" is the one the Australian Treasury gave by accident: whoever bought the licence.
