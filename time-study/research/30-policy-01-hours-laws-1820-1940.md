## Key question 1: UK Factory Acts 1833, 1844, 1847, 1850, 1874, 1878 (and the 1908 miners' act). What each capped, who was covered, actual hours before and after, and whether the Ten Hours Act cut output

### Takeaway
UK law before 1908 capped hours only for children, young persons and women, and only in textiles until 1864 to 1867. The legal textile week fell from 69 hours (1833) to 58 (1848), then back up to 60 (1850) and down to 56.5 (1874). Adult men were never directly covered before the 1908 Coal Mines Act, so the UK fall from about 57 hours a week (1870) to 47 (1929) ran mostly through collective bargaining, not statute. Opponents of the 1847 Ten Hours Act said profit came from the "last hour" and would vanish, but opened sources agree output and employment held up, though no opened source gives a measured output series for 1847 to 1850.

### Cited Findings

Caps and coverage, act by act

- 1802 Health and Morals of Apprentices Act (UK): applied to textile mills with three or more apprentices or twenty employees. Apprentices' daily hours capped at 12 excluding breaks, night work (9pm to 6am) banned. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1819 Cotton Mills and Factories Act (UK): cotton only. No children under 9, children 9 to 16 limited to 12 hours a day. Enforcement needed two sworn witnesses before magistrates could inspect. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1825 Act (UK, cotton): 12 hours Monday to Friday and 9 hours Saturday, which is a 69 hour legal week for those covered. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1831 Hobhouse's Act (UK, cotton only): 12 hour limit extended to all under 18, night work banned under 21. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1833 Althorp's Act (UK, textile mills, silk partly exempt): no children under 9 (except silk). Children 9 to 13 limited to 8 hours a day (the page also gives 48 hours a week for 9 to 12), with a relay system intended to let adults still work the full day. Young persons 13 to 18 limited to 12 hours a day. No night work under 18 (about 8.30pm to 5.30am). Created a paid Factory Inspectorate. Sadler's defeated 1832 bill had proposed 10 hours (8 on Saturday) for all under 18. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1835 (UK): inspectors "found no deformity or health injury from the twelve-hour day" and were too few for about 4,000 factories. Relay working for children was "difficult, there not being enough children." Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1844 Graham's Act (UK, textiles): extended the 12 hour weekday cap to women of all ages (the key change). Ashley's amendment to cut the cap to 10 hours was defeated 295 to 198. Plausible. The page summary also mentions a shorter day for children under the 1844 act but the fetched text was garbled on the exact children's hours. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1847 Ten Hours Act (UK, textile mills, lace and silk excluded): royal assent 8 June 1847. Women and young persons 13 to 18 limited to 63 hours a week from 1 July 1847 and 58 hours a week from 1 May 1848, described as a 10 hour day. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factories Act 1847](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factories_Act_1847) and [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1847 to 1850 evasion (UK): "millowners used legal loopholes to evade its provisions and the courts proved reluctant to intervene." The Court of Exchequer ruled in 1850 that the act was too weakly worded to make relay (split shift) systems illegal. Ashley in 1850: "The Ten Hours' Act nullified. The work to be done all over again." A Colne case of 1849 showed a 15 year old spinner working 6am to 7.15pm with breaks under a relay system. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factories Act 1847](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factories_Act_1847) and [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1850 Compromise Act (UK, textiles, women and young persons): legal day 6am to 6pm (or 7 to 7 in winter with inspector approval), 1.5 hours of breaks, so a maximum 10.5 hour day. All work to end Saturday at 2pm. Weekly total raised from 58 to 60 hours. Children were not covered until the 1853 act set "similar limits". Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1864 Factory Acts Extension Act (UK): first non-textile trades brought in (potteries, lucifer matches, percussion caps and cartridges, paper staining, fustian cutting). 1867 Factory Acts Extension Act: all establishments with 50 or more workers. 1867 Workshop Regulation Act: under 50 workers, from 1 January 1868, with a "less restrictive" hours window. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1874 Factory Act (UK, textiles): weekday 10 hours in sessions of no more than 4.5 hours separated by meal breaks of at least an hour, Saturday 6 hours plus half an hour on other duties. Minimum age to rise to 10. Arithmetic on these caps gives 56.5 hours a week. Plausible (the 56.5 total is computed, not quoted). [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1874 (UK, Webb and Cox writing in 1891): cotton operatives had "a legal week of about 56 1/2 hours". Verified in the 1891 text. [Webb and Cox, The Eight Hours Day, 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt)
- 1878 Factory and Workshop Act (UK): consolidated and repealed 16 earlier factory acts. No new hours caps recorded in the opened source. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1908 Coal Mines Regulation Act (UK): section 1, "a worker shall not be below ground in a mine for the purpose of his work, and of going to and from his work, for more than eight hours." Royal assent 21 December 1908, commencement 1 July 1909 (1 January 1910 in Northumberland and Durham). The Coal Mines Act 1919 later substituted seven hours. Plausible (statute text seen only in a search snippet of legislation.gov.uk). [legislation.gov.uk, 8 Edw. 7 c. 57](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/8/57) and [Wikipedia Coal Mines Regulation Act 1908](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_Mines_Regulation_Act_1908)
- 1919 to 1932 (UK): the UK never passed a general adult hours law and never ratified the 1919 ILO Washington Convention. Government statement in the Commons, 28 April 1932: "practical difficulties and not lack of sympathy have prevented the ratification of the Convention in a country which, in spite of long depression, maintains in practice a normal working week of 48 hours or less." Verified. [Hansard HC Deb 28 April 1932](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1932/apr/28/washington-hours-convention)
- 1928 (UK): Under-Secretary Betterton said "95 per cent. of the people in Britain worked only forty-eight hours per week." Plausible (search snippet of Hansard, page not opened). [Hansard HC Deb 27 February 1928](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1928/feb/27/civil-estimates)

Actual hours before and after

- 1760, 1800, 1830 (England, annual hours per worker, Voth from court witness accounts, London and the North): 1760 2,576 (London 2,582, North 2,518), 1800 3,328 (London 3,319, North 3,411), 1830 3,356 (London 3,367, North 3,294). The 95 percent confidence interval for the 1760 to 1830 rise is 138 to 1,422 hours. "Annual working hours were only marginally higher than those documented in wage books for Britain in 1856." Sample areas held 29 percent of English males in 1841. Verified. [Voth, The Longest Years, JEH 2001 (CREI PDF)](https://www.crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/users/working-papers/voth_longestyears.pdf)
- 1750s to 1800 and 1830 (England): a JEH review summarises Voth as finding male hours rose about 30 percent from the 1750s to 1800 and about 20 percent from the 1750s to 1830, with the 3,500 hour estimate for 1800 "the all-time high for England." Clark's review notes that if farm workers had worked a steady 3,300 hours national hours would have risen only about 8 percent 1750 to 1830. Plausible (search snippets). [Clark review of Voth](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/Book_Reviews/Voth%20-%20jeh.pdf)
- 1860 (UK): "most artisans were still working about 60 hours a week." Verified in the 1891 text. [Webb and Cox 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt)
- 1870 to 1938 (UK, weekly hours of full-time production workers, non-agricultural): 1870 56.9, 1880 56.6, 1890 56.3, 1900 56.0, 1913 56.0, 1929 47.0, 1938 48.6. Verified. [Huberman and Minns 2007, Table 1](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)
- 1870 to 1938 (UK, annual hours): 1870 2,755, 1880 2,740, 1890 2,669, 1900 2,656, 1913 2,656, 1929 2,257, 1938 2,200. Verified. [Huberman and Minns 2007, Table 3](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)
- 1870 to 1938 (UK, days of vacation and holiday per year): 1870 14, 1900 20, 1938 30. Verified. [Huberman and Minns 2007, Table 2](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)
- 1870 (UK): "In 1870, Australia had the shortest workweek, followed closely by the U.K." and "In Britain time at work hardly fell before 1913 in contrast to its continental neighbors, but it converged to European levels by the mid 1970s well ahead of EU directives." Verified. [Huberman and Minns 2007](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)
- 1890 (UK, long-hours trades named by Webb and Cox): omnibus drivers and conductors "work fourteen to sixteen hours a day", railway drivers and guards often on duty "for fifteen or eighteen hours at a stretch", sweated East End tailors sometimes sixteen hours of twenty four. Verified in the 1891 text. [Webb and Cox 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt)

Did the Ten Hours Act cut output?

- 1837 (UK, the opponents' case): Nassau Senior, as quoted by Marx, argued that in a cotton mill "the whole net profit is derived from the last hour", that "if the hours of working were reduced by one hour per day (prices remaining the same), the net profit would be destroyed", and "if they were reduced by one hour and a half, even the gross profit would be destroyed." Marx adds that Senior "at a later period, to his honour be it said, energetically supported the factory legislation." Marx also quotes Inspector Hovell's 1855 report observing that if Senior's arithmetic were right "every cotton factory in the United Kingdom would have been working at a loss since the year 1850." Verified (Senior's words as reproduced in Marx, Capital vol. 1 ch. 9, section 3). [Marx, Capital ch. 9](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch09.htm)
- 1863 (UK): a search snippet reports Senior withdrew the "last hour" teaching at the Social Science Congress in Edinburgh and recommended extending the ten hours law to other trades. Plausible (snippet only). [Bibliomania, Marx Capital commentary](https://www.bibliomania.com/2/1/261/1294/24744/7.html)
- 1852 (UK): Nasmyth's letter to inspector Leonard Horner reported that "at least as much is produced in the shortened (by two hours or one-sixth) working-day as was previously produced during the longer one", credited to faster engines and more intense work. The same passage gives spindle speed increases of 32 percent for 1838 to 1850 and 86 percent for 1850 to 1856. Plausible (search snippet of Marx Capital ch. 15, page not opened). [Bibliomania, Marx Capital](https://www.bibliomania.com/2/1/261/1294/24750/19.html)
- 1844 (UK, Commons debate): supporters argued that "about fifteen years ago, working-time was reduced from sixteen to twelve hours a day, and no reduction of wages or diminished production followed the change." Plausible and partisan (Spectator report, snippet only). [Spectator, 11 May 1844](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/11th-may-1844/10/postscript)
- 1847 to 1874 (UK, after the act): the Ten Hours Act "had no obvious detrimental effect on the prosperity of the textile industry or on that of millworkers", and its introduction "proved to have none of the dire consequences predicted by its opponents." Shaftesbury's 1874 review gave textile mill employment of 354,684 in 1835 (56,455 under 13) and 880,920 in 1871 (80,498 under 13). Plausible. [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1847 (UK): the ten hour limit was achieved "without the negative economic effects its opponents had feared to come to pass", with no data given. Historians disagree whether law or technology ended child labour. Plausible. [Wikipedia Factories Act 1847](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factories_Act_1847)
- 1880 (US cross-check on the elasticity): Margo, using 1880 US census data, found hours elasticities of value added and wages "less than one", so "a reduction in hours from, say, ten to nine per day, a 10 percent reduction, would result in a less than proportionate reduction in value added and in wages." Plausible (search snippet, punctuation adjusted). [Levy Institute WP 317 (Margo)](https://levyinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/wp317.pdf)

### Inferences
- The UK statutory sequence for textile women and young persons is 69 hours (1825 to 1833 structure), 63 (July 1847), 58 (May 1848), 60 (1850 compromise), 56.5 (1874). Adult men were caught only indirectly because mills could not run without the protected workers, which is why the 1850 "normal day" of 6am to 6pm mattered more in practice than the 1847 nominal 58.
- Because the UK figure for the whole non-agricultural workforce barely moved between 1870 (56.9) and 1913 (56.0) while the textile legal week fell to 56.5 in 1874, the law appears to have set a standard for textiles that the rest of the economy had already roughly reached, or reached by bargaining. The big UK drop, 56.0 (1913) to 47.0 (1929), happened with no statute and no ratified ILO convention, which points to collective agreements as the mechanism.
- On output, the opened evidence is qualitative. Opponents predicted ruin, contemporaries recorded none, and 1880 US data imply a hours elasticity of output below one. No opened source gives a measured output or profit series for UK cotton before and after 1 May 1848, so "the Ten Hours Act did not cut output" is well supported as a narrative and unquantified as a number.

### Gaps
- The parliament.uk living heritage pages on the 1833 Act and later factory legislation returned HTTP 403, so the act texts were not seen directly. The caps above rest on Wikipedia and should be checked against the statutes before publication.
- No measured series of cotton output, output per worker or profits for 1845 to 1855 was found in any opened source. Factory inspector reports (Horner) for 1848 to 1852 were not opened.
- The 1844 act's children's hours (the half-time system) were not confirmed in the opened text.
- The 1871 engineers' nine hour movement (Newcastle) and the 1919 national 47 or 48 hour agreements, which are the likely route of the UK fall for adult men, were not sourced in this pass.

## Key question 2: US hours laws 1840 to 1938 and average US manufacturing hours 1830 to 1940

### Takeaway
US law mostly trailed the market. State ten hour laws from 1847 had contract loopholes, the enforceable laws from 1874 covered only women, and by 1920 only 12 percent of employees were covered with an average legal cap of 56.7 hours when manufacturing already averaged about 51. The first binding federal laws were the 1916 Adamson Act (rail only) and the 1938 FLSA (44 hours, about one fifth of the labour force), the latter passed when Depression hours were already far below the cap. Manufacturing weekly hours ran 69.1 (1830), 61.1 to 63.0 (1870), 59.6 (1900), 51.2 (1920), 37.6 (1940).

State and federal laws 1840 to 1917

- 1840 (US federal): Van Buren's executive order mandated a ten hour day for federal manual workers. Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1847 New Hampshire and 1848 Pennsylvania (US): declared ten hours the legal day, but contracts could set any length, so the laws "had little impact." Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1866 to 1867 (US): eight states adopted general eight hour laws that allowed longer days by mutual consent. Illinois's 1867 eight hour law had "many loopholes" and a Chicago strike began 1 May 1867. Verified (Whaples) and Plausible (Wikipedia for Illinois). [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/) and [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day)
- 1868 (US federal): Congress passed an eight hour law for federal labourers and mechanics on 25 June 1868 over Johnson's veto. Wages were then cut by 20 percent until Grant's proclamation of 19 May 1869 barred pay cuts when the day was shortened. Plausible. The German Wikipedia page instead says Johnson signed the law on 29 June 1868, so the exact date and the veto story conflict between sources. [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day) and [de.wikipedia Achtstundentag](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achtstundentag)
- 1874 Massachusetts (US): first enforceable ten hour law, covering only women, 60 hours a week, fully effective 1879. Later Massachusetts caps for women: 58 (1892), 56 (1908), 54 (1911). Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1886 (US): eight hour strikes shortened the week for about 200,000 industrial workers. 1 May 1886, about 80,000 marched in Chicago and within days 350,000 workers at 1,200 factories struck. 1890: carpenters' strikes gave nearly 100,000 workers the eight hour day. Verified (Whaples) and Plausible (Wikipedia strike totals). [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/) and [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day)
- 1900 to 1930 (US, spread of state maximum hours laws): share of states with such laws 26 percent (1900), 58 percent (1910), 76 percent (1920), 84 percent (1930). Share of employees covered 4 percent (1900), 7 percent (1910), 12 percent (1920 and 1930). Average legal maximum 59.3 hours (1900) falling to 56.7 (1920). Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1898 to 1917 (US courts): Holden v. Hardy 1898 upheld an eight hour day in mining and smelting. Lochner v. New York 1905 struck down a ten hour bakers' law. Muller v. Oregon 1908 upheld a ten hour law for women. Bunting v. Oregon 1917 upheld overtime pay for long hours. Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1908 (US): the Muller brief, known as the Brandeis Brief, was compiled largely by Josephine Goldmark (about 98 of its 118 pages) and "was instrumental in the Supreme Court ruling that state maximum-hours laws were constitutional." [Wikipedia Josephine Goldmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Clara_Goldmark) and [Wikipedia Muller v. Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller_v._Oregon)
- 1912 (US federal): Public Works Act required an eight hour clause in federal contracts. 1915: La Follette's bill set maximum hours for maritime workers. Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1916 Adamson Act (US): signed by Wilson 3 September 1916 (one source line also says 5 September). Established an eight hour standard day for interstate railroad employees operating trains, with additional pay for overtime. Excluded roads under 100 miles, electric street and interurban railways. "Congress passed the Act in order to avoid a nationwide strike." Upheld in Wilson v. New, 243 U.S. 332 (1917), after which railroads entered settlement talks. The page does not say whether it cut hours or only raised pay. Plausible. Whaples adds that it "set eight hours as the basic rail workday, with higher overtime pay." Verified for that sentence. [Wikipedia Adamson Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamson_Act) and [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)

Ford, the 1920s, and the Depression laws

- 1914 (US, Ford): announced 5 January 1914 that from 12 January the day would fall from nine to eight hours and the daily minimum wage would rise from $2.34 to $5.00. Plausible. [de.wikipedia Achtstundentag](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achtstundentag) and [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day)
- 1926 (US, Ford): Ford adopted the five day week, covering more than half of its roughly 400,000 workers. In 1927, 262 large establishments had a five day week against 32 in 1920. Verified. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1926 (US, Ford's own argument): "we can get at least as great production in five days as we can in six." "Now we can look at leisure as a cold business fact." "The people who consume the bulk of goods are the people who make them." "A full week's wage for a short week's work will pay." "It is not necessary to bring in sentiment at all in this whole question of leisure for workers." During the transition "whenever a department was especially rushed it went back to six days, to forty-eight hours" (punctuation adjusted). "We have been going toward it for three or four years." Plausible: the Wikisource transcription of the October 1926 World's Work interview states its original source is unknown. [Wikisource, Ford 1926](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Henry_Ford:_Why_I_Favor_Five_Days%27_Work_With_Six_Days%27_Pay)
- 1920s (US, business and economist opinion): Hunnicutt argues businessmen and economists saw shorter hours as a threat to growth in the 1920s, and businessmen attacked the shorter hours movement as a ploy to raise wages. Verified as Whaples's summary. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1930 to 1932 (US, share the work): Hoover's President's Emergency Committee for Employment (1930 to 1931) and President's Organization for Unemployment Relief (1931 to 1932) made shorter hours central. Hoover launched the Share-the-Work movement in September 1932. Hoover's Commission for Work Sharing estimated voluntary cuts saved three to five million jobs. Plausible (encyclopedia.com) and Verified (Whaples for the 3 to 5 million estimate). [Encyclopedia.com, Black Thirty-Hour Bill](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/black-thirty-hour-bill) and [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- December 1932 to April 1933 (US, Black thirty hour bill): introduced by Senator Hugo Black in December 1932. It would have barred interstate shipment of goods made where workers worked "more than five days per week or more than six hours per day." Black framed it as an alternative to public relief and a way to spread purchasing power. Senate passed it 6 April 1933 by 53 to 30. Roosevelt called it a "one paragraph bill" that would not aid recovery and worried the hours cap was too rigid. Perkins drafted a substitute adding minimum wages. The bill was overtaken by the NIRA, signed 16 June 1933. Plausible (encyclopedia.com) and Verified (Whaples for the 53 to 30 vote). [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/black-thirty-hour-bill) and [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1933 to 1935 (US, NIRA codes): the President's Reemployment Agreement set a workweek of 35 to 40 hours, a minimum wage of $12 to $15 a week and generally no workers under 16. The Cotton Textile Code set 40 hours, $13 a week in the North and $12 in the South, and abolished child labour in textiles. About half of NRA covered employees were set at 40 hours and nearly 40 percent had weeks longer than 40. Schechter struck down the NRA on 27 May 1935. Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938) and [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1936 (US federal contractors): Walsh-Healey Act, 30 June 1936, required an eight hour day and 40 hour week plus a prevailing minimum wage for government contractors. Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)
- 1937 to 1938 (US, FLSA): Black-Connery bill of 1937 proposed 40 cents, 40 hours, age 16 and a five member labor standards board. Roosevelt sent the bill 24 May 1937. Senate passed a weakened bill 56 to 28 on 31 July 1937. House recommitted it 218 to 198 in December 1937. House passed it 314 to 97 on 24 May 1938 and the conference version 291 to 89 on 13 June 1938. Signed 25 June 1938, effective 24 October 1938. Final act: 25 cent minimum wage and a 44 hour maximum workweek in covered industries, which employed about one fifth of the labour force. Work covered by collective bargaining was excluded at union request. Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)
- 1938 to 1940 (US, FLSA step down): the DOL history page gives only the 44 hour first year cap and does not mention 42 or 40. The 44 then 42 then 40 hour schedule in section 7 (40 from October 1940) is the standard account but was not seen in an opened source. Plausible for the 40 hour end point (Wikipedia says the FLSA set a 40 hour maximum with overtime beyond it). Not found for the 42 hour step. [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day)
- 1938 (US, FLSA purpose as stated by proponents): shorter hours would "create new jobs...for millions of our unskilled unemployed", and long hours "wear out part of the working population while they keep the rest from having work to do." Roosevelt's special session message said undercutting of wages and stretching of hours "has a serious effect on buying power." Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)
- 1938 (Europe looking at the US): "Before World War II, continental social reformers considered the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 a model piece of legislation." Verified. [Huberman and Minns 2007](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/minns/Huberman_Minns_EEH_2007.pdf)

### Inferences
- The state laws of 1900 to 1920 sat above the market. The average legal cap was 56.7 hours in 1920 while manufacturing averaged 51.2 and only 12 percent of employees were covered, so the state laws ratified rather than drove the fall. Whaples's regression finding (below) that state laws had little measured effect fits this.
- The FLSA's 44 hour cap in 1938 was above the 1938 average of 37.3 for production workers, so on average it bound few workers. Costa's wholesale versus retail comparison (Key question 4) shows it still cut hours where the cap bit, which is the pattern of a law that binds at the long-hours tail rather than moving the mean.
- Ford in 1926 and US Steel in 1923 show large firms cutting hours before any federal law, which supports the "rising productivity and wages" account for the 1920s.

### Gaps
- Average manufacturing hours for exactly 1870 and 1920 come from two different series (Weeks or Aldrich for 1870, census based for 1920). No single consistent series across 1830 to 1940 was found.
- The exact overtime rate under the Adamson Act and whether rail hours fell in practice were not found.
- The 42 hour intermediate step of the FLSA was not seen in an opened source.
- Average hours for 1830 cover manufacturing only. No opened source gave a national all-worker figure for 1830.

## Key question 3: France (1848, 1919, 1936), Germany (1918, 1923, 1927) and the ILO (Convention 1 of 1919, Convention 47 of 1935)

### Takeaway
France and Germany are the clearest cases of law leading. France went from about 62 hours a week (1913) to 48 (1929) after the April 1919 eight hour law, and from 48 to 39 (1938) after the June 1936 forty hour law, with the 1848 ten hour decree reversed within six months. Germany went from 57 (1913) to about 48 (1919 to 1923) after the November 1918 eight hour order, then back up to about 50.4 (1924) after the December 1923 ordinance relaxed it. Of the four countries only France ratified ILO Convention 1 (in 1927, conditionally), and none of the four ratified Convention 47 before 1940. The ILO text itself could not be opened.

### Inferences
- The French 1848 episode is the clearest case of law running ahead of what the economy would bear politically: a ten hour decree in March, twelve hours by September. The 1919 and 1936 laws, by contrast, moved national averages by 14 and 9 hours a week within a decade and two years respectively, which no bargaining or income effect would have produced that fast. Law led in France.
- Germany 1918 shows law and bargaining fused: the eight hour day was agreed between employers and unions on 15 November and decreed on 23 November. The reversal in December 1923, when hours rose to about 50 by 1924, shows that a legal eight hours held only while the political coalition behind it held.
- The ILO was weak as a direct lever in the four countries: only France ratified Convention 1, and eight years after adoption. Its stronger effect was as a focal standard, since by 1929 the UK (47.0), US (48.0), France (48.0) and Germany (46.0) had all converged near 48 with very different legal routes.
- The 1936 French output result (stagnation, with the 40 hour law a named cause) is the strongest opened evidence that a legally saved hour was not offset by productivity in the short run, which contrasts with the 1847 UK narrative and with Ford's 1926 claim.

## Key question 4: Did hours fall mainly through law, union bargaining, or rising wages? The main studies and their numbers

### Takeaway
The quantitative work says rising wages did most of the work in the US before 1930 (Whaples: over 80 percent of economic historians agree, and wages explain about half of the 1914 to 1919 drop against one seventh for unions), while in Europe "labor power" expressed through suffrage and legislation mattered more (Huberman and Minns). Costa shows the FLSA did cut hours where it bound but did not raise employment. Voth shows hours rose, not fell, in England from 1760 to 1830, so the 1820 to 1840 starting point was a historic peak.

- 1890s to 1991 (US men and women): in the 1890s the highest paid worked fewer hours than the lowest paid, across and within wage deciles and within industry and occupation groups. The 1890s labour supply curve "was strongly backward-bending" and the intertemporal elasticity of substitution was slightly negative, becoming positive by 1973. ### Inferences
- The two best studies split by region. For the US (Whaples, Costa, Margo) the fall to about 48 by 1929 came mainly from wages and firm level bargains, and laws trailed. For Europe (Huberman and Minns) suffrage driven legislation was part of the mechanism, most sharply in Belgium, France and Germany. The UK is the European exception, closer to the US pattern, with bargaining rather than statute.
- The French and German interwar jumps (France 62 to 48 between 1913 and 1929, Germany 57 to 48 between 1913 and 1919) are too large and too fast for the wage elasticity of about minus 0.11 to minus 0.12 to explain. A 10 percent wage rise buys about 1 percent fewer hours in these regressions, so a 20 percent cut in hours would need wages to double. Those jumps are law and bargaining, not income.
- Voth's series implies that the 1833 to 1850 Factory Acts arrived at or just after the historic peak of English hours (about 3,350 a year in 1830), so the long fall from 1830 to 1938 started from an unusually high base.

### Gaps
- Huberman and Minns's Table 4 wage coefficient for the pooled baseline was not read cleanly (only Tables 6 to 8 were legible), so the headline elasticity is quoted from Table 6.

## Key question 5: What Keynes (1930), Marshall, Mill, the Webbs and US progressive economists said about shorter hours, and whether saved time would go to leisure or output

### Takeaway
Mill (1848) framed the hours question as a collective action problem that might need law but declined to endorse a statute. Marshall (1890) accepted shorter hours only in trades with expensive plant, rejected the "work-fund" idea that less work means more jobs, and tied the value of leisure to using it well. The Webbs (1891) argued output per hour rises so that a legal eight hours would cost little output and absorb the unemployed. Keynes (1930) expected a fifteen hour week within a century and worried people would not know how to use leisure. US progressives (Goldmark, Brandeis) made the fatigue and efficiency case to the courts, while Ford (1926) argued leisure creates demand.

### Cited Findings

- 1848, Mill, Principles Book V ch. XI section 12 (UK): using a cut from ten to nine hours as the example, "A workman who refused to work more than nine hours while there were others who worked ten" would not be employed or "must submit to lose one-tenth of his wages." A general agreement might need "converting their supposed mutual agreement into an engagement under penalty, by consenting to have it enforced by law." Yet "I am not expressing any opinion in favour of such an enactment", the case being offered to show how classes of persons may need the help of law to act on their collective judgment. Verified. [Mill, Principles, Book V ch. XI (econlib)](https://www.econlib.org/library/Mill/mlP73.html)
- 1837, Senior (UK): the "last hour" claim, see Key question 1. Verified as quoted by Marx. [Marx, Capital ch. 9](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch09.htm)
- 1890 (8th edition 1920), Marshall, Principles Book VI ch. XIII (UK): the case for shorter hours "applies only to those trades which use, or can use, expensive plant" (section 3). In such trades, where workers are overstrained, "the national dividend would increase" over time as plant runs longer. Elsewhere "a reduction of the hours of labour would certainly lessen the output in the immediate present" and "in such cases the change would diminish the national dividend." "The change would need to be introduced gradually." Verified. [Marshall, Principles, Book VI ch. XIII (econlib)](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)
- Marshall on the work-fund (section 4): the argument that restricting labour raises wages or employment "rests on the assumption that there is a permanent fixed work-fund", for which there is "no foundation". "The demand for work comes from the national dividend. That is, it comes from work" (punctuation adjusted). Verified. [Marshall, Book VI ch. XIII](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)
- Marshall on measurement (section 6): "it may be as difficult to isolate the effects of reduction of the hours of labour" as to isolate the ripples from one stone in a rough sea. Verified. [Marshall, Book VI ch. XIII](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)
- Marshall on leisure (section 14): it "would probably be well that most people should work rather less", provided the lost income is offset by giving up the least worthy consumption. "It is only through freedom to use leisure as they will, that people can learn to use leisure well", and "some time free from the fatigue of work that tires without educating, is a necessary condition of a high standard of life." Verified. [Marshall, Book VI ch. XIII](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)
- 1891, Webb and Cox, The Eight Hours Day (UK): "The workman, in many cases, but not in all, produces as much in ten hours as in twelve, in eight hours as in ten", and "two from ten is likely to produce, not eight, but even eleven", with railways, trams and omnibuses as exceptions where output would fall. A shorter day would work by "transferring from irregular to full employment many thousands of those who can find no work to do", so total output could rise even if individual output fell. Legislation is needed because "the individual workman cannot by himself secure an Eight Hours Day for himself", trade union action is "equivalent to private war", and "an Act of Parliament would still be preferable". They cite the Scotch Railway Strike, five weeks ending in the men's "complete submission", and a ballot in which most trade unionists preferred an Act. A contemporary review described the book as advocating "a compulsory eight-hours day for all industrial employments except domestic service and agriculture." Verified for the quotes from the Introduction and chapter II, Plausible for the review line (snippet). [Webb and Cox 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt)
- 1897, Webbs, Industrial Democracy, chapter "The Normal Day" (UK): the chapter exists and deals with why some unions regulate hours, but its text was not opened. Not found for quotations. [Wikipedia Industrial Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Democracy)
- 1908 to 1912, Goldmark and Brandeis (US): see Key question 2. The Brandeis brief in Muller v. Oregon ran to 118 pages, about 98 of them compiled by Goldmark's team, and Fatigue and Efficiency (1912) argued long hours hurt both workers and productivity. Plausible. [Wikipedia Josephine Goldmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Clara_Goldmark)
- 1920s (US economists): "In the 1920s, some economists argued that a shorter workweek would improve workers' lives and offset job losses from technological change." The Technocracy movement of 1932 reinforced the fear of technological unemployment. Plausible. [Encyclopedia.com, Black Thirty-Hour Bill](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/black-thirty-hour-bill)
- 1926, Ford (US): leisure is "a cold business fact", production in five days at least equals six, and "The people who consume the bulk of goods are the people who make them." Plausible (Wikisource provenance). [Wikisource, Ford 1926](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Henry_Ford:_Why_I_Favor_Five_Days%27_Work_With_Six_Days%27_Pay)
- 1930, Keynes, Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren (UK): the standard of life in progressive countries a hundred years hence "will be between four and eight times as high as it is to-day." Technological unemployment "means unemployment due to our discovery of means of economising the use of labour" outrunning new uses for labour. "This means that the economic problem is not, if we look into the future, the permanent problem of the human race" (punctuation adjusted). "Thus for the first time since his creation man will be faced with his real, his permanent problem", how to use freedom from pressing economic cares. "Three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week may put off the problem for a great while." "For many ages to come the old Adam will be so strong in us" that everyone will need some work to be contented. "To those who sweat for their daily bread leisure is a longed-for sweet, until they get it" (punctuation adjusted). Verified. [Keynes 1930 (marxists.org text)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/keynes/1930/our-grandchildren.htm)
- 1930s (US officials): Roosevelt's 1937 message said all "able-bodied working men and women" deserve "a fair day's pay for a fair day's work", and he told a fireside audience "Do not let any calamity-howling executive with an income of $1,000 a day" warn against an $11 a week wage. Perkins said she took office only on condition she could push a law setting wage floors, hour ceilings and child labour limits, and called the December 1937 House defeat "the death knell of wage-hour legislation." Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)

### Inferences
- The British economists split on mechanism, not on direction. Mill and the Webbs saw a coordination failure that only law or very strong custom could fix. Marshall accepted that only where capital intensity let plant run in shifts, and he explicitly rejected the share-the-work logic that later drove the 1933 Black bill.
- On whether saved time goes to leisure or output, the 1891 Webb and Cox claim (output per hour rises enough to hold total output) and Ford's 1926 claim both predict the saved hour converts into output, while Keynes in 1930 assumed it converts into leisure and worried about that. The 1936 French evidence sides with neither fully: output stagnated rather than rising or being fully offset.

### Gaps
- Marshall's earlier 1873 lecture "The Future of the Working Classes" and his evidence on the eight hour day were not opened.
- The Webbs' Industrial Democracy chapter on the Normal Day and their 1891 chapter IV with the firm examples (Mather and Platt, Brunner Mond) were beyond the fetched portion of the archive.org text.
- Richard Ely, John R. Commons and the American Association for Labor Legislation were not sourced in this pass. The Hunnicutt argument is known only through Whaples's summary.

## Key question 6: Cases where a law or firm cut hours and the saved time went to more output or more jobs rather than leisure (share-the-work arguments of the 1930s)

### Takeaway
Four episodes are documented. The UK Ten Hours Act (1847 to 1850) is reported to have left output intact, so the saved hours went to leisure without loss of output. Ford's 1926 five day week is claimed by Ford to have kept output flat. The 1932 to 1938 US share-the-work programmes were explicitly meant to turn hours into jobs, with Hoover's committee claiming three to five million jobs saved, but Costa finds no employment gain from FLSA hours cuts. The French 1936 forty hour law is the one case with modern econometric evidence, and it found stagnating output and little fall in unemployment.

### Cited Findings

- 1847 to 1852 (UK, output held): Nasmyth to Horner, 1852, "at least as much is produced in the shortened (by two hours or one-sixth) working-day as was previously produced during the longer one", with faster engines and more intense work. Plausible (snippet). The Factory Acts page records "no obvious detrimental effect" on industry prosperity and employment of 354,684 (1835) rising to 880,920 (1871). Plausible. [Bibliomania, Marx Capital](https://www.bibliomania.com/2/1/261/1294/24750/19.html) and [Wikipedia Factory Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_Acts)
- 1868 to 1869 (US federal, pay cut followed hours cut): after the 1868 federal eight hour law wages were cut by 20 percent until Grant's 1869 proclamation forbade it, so the first federal hours cut initially went to lower pay, not to leisure at constant income. Plausible. [Wikipedia Eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day)
- 1891 (UK, the share-the-work claim in theory): Webb and Cox argued a legal eight hours would transfer "from irregular to full employment many thousands of those who can find no work to do". Marshall's reply in principle: there is "no foundation" for a "permanent fixed work-fund". Verified for both. [Webb and Cox 1891](https://archive.org/stream/eighthoursday00webb/eighthoursday00webb_djvu.txt) and [Marshall, Book VI ch. XIII](https://www.econlib.org/library/Marshall/marP55.html)
- 1926 (US, Ford): "we can get at least as great production in five days as we can in six", and departments that were "especially rushed" went back to six days. Plausible (Wikisource). [Wikisource, Ford 1926](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Henry_Ford:_Why_I_Favor_Five_Days%27_Work_With_Six_Days%27_Pay)
- 1927 (Germany, unions on jobs): unions demanded restoration of the legal eight hour day "weil nur dadurch die Möglichkeit besteht, das Arbeitslosenproblem zu lösen". Plausible. [DHM LeMO](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/weimarer-republik/industrie-und-wirtschaft/achtstundentag.html)
- 1930 to 1932 (US, voluntary work sharing): about half of American employers had shortened hours by 1932 and Hoover's Commission for Work Sharing estimated three to five million jobs saved. Verified. Walter Teagle of Standard Oil of New Jersey toured the country for Hoover promoting worktime cuts to preserve jobs. Plausible (snippet). [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/) and [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/black-thirty-hour-bill)
- 1933 (US, Black bill): a thirty hour cap passed the Senate 53 to 30 on 6 April 1933 as a job spreading and purchasing power measure. Supporters' claim that it would create six million jobs appears only in a news summary and was not seen in a primary source. Verified for the vote, Plausible for the six million. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/) and [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/black-thirty-hour-bill)
- 1933 to 1935 (US, NIRA): the PRA 35 to 40 hour weeks and the 40 hour codes were sold as re-employment measures (the agreement was literally the President's Reemployment Agreement). About half of covered employees were set at 40 hours. Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938) and [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)
- 1938 (US, FLSA aims): proponents said shorter hours would "create new jobs...for millions of our unskilled unemployed". Verified. [US DOL, FLSA history](https://www.dol.gov/general/aboutdol/history/flsa1938)
- 1938 to 1950 (US, FLSA result): hours fell in covered wholesale trade relative to retail, more in the South, but "the hours reductions did not produce higher employment." Verified (abstract). [Costa, NBER WP 6855](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6855)
- 1936 to 1938 (France, result): "Deflation ended but output stagnated", with the 40 hour law named as a contributor to "French stagflation." Verified (abstract). 350,000 assisted unemployed remained in 1937. Plausible (snippet). [NBER WP 22140](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22140) and [Vikidia](https://fr.vikidia.org/wiki/Semaine_de_40_heures_(1936))
- 1930 to 1985 (US, Kellogg's six hour day): in 1946, 87 percent of women and 71 percent of men at Kellogg's voted to return to the six hour day, most departments had moved to eight hour shifts by 1957, and the last department adopted eight hours in 1985. Verified for these dates. The 1930 start of the six hour day at Kellogg's is the standard account but was not seen in the opened text. [Whaples, EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/hours-of-work-in-u-s-history/)

### Inferences
- Where hours fell with productivity rising (UK textiles after 1847 with faster spindles, Ford in 1926 with assembly line gains), the saved hour showed up as leisure at roughly constant output and pay. Where hours were cut by law in a slump to create jobs (US 1933 to 1938, France 1936), the opened evidence finds little or no employment gain and, in France, lower output. This is consistent with Marshall's work-fund critique and with Costa's result.
- The share-the-work logic of 1932 to 1938 treated the saved hour as a transferable unit of employment. The measured outcomes suggest it mostly became either leisure (US, where hours fell to 37.3 by 1938 with no employment boost in trade) or lost output (France).

### Gaps
- No opened source gives a before and after output per hour series for the UK 1847 act, for Ford 1926, or for the NIRA codes.
- The Cohen-Setton, Hausman and Wieland paper's body (size of the output effect, employment response by industry) was not opened, only the abstract.
- The Nord-Pas-de-Calais coal study that answers Sauvy was found only as a listing and not opened.
- Hoover's "three to five million jobs" figure is the committee's own estimate and no independent check was found.
