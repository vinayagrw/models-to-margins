# 11b. Persona panel v2: will AI give people more free time?

Date: 27 October 2026. This is the second run of the panel for the study "Time spent vs time saved, 1826 to 2076". It does not replace file 11. It adds the evidence from files 10, 12 and 13.

Rules for this panel:

- Every number comes from files 01 to 10, 12 and 13 and is tagged Verified or Plausible there. Where a verification log corrected a value, the corrected value is used. No new numbers were added.
- Unverified figures are left out. That drops the Amsterdam power caps, the Dublin connection pause, the 23% Irish share, the LinkedIn talent pool multiplier, the Microsoft reluctance figure and the US compute share claim.
- India now uses the 2024 survey from file 10 (women 289 minutes, men 88 minutes of unpaid domestic work). File 11 used about 305 minutes from file 09. Both are tagged, but the 2024 figure is newer and comes with a 2019 baseline.
- Data centre power by 2030 is about 945 TWh (file 13, IEA). File 10 rounds it to about 950. Both are Plausible.
- The personas are composite expert viewpoints written for this study. They are not real people and do not quote anyone.
- Confidence is given in words: High, Medium, Low, Speculative.
- The break-even worked cases in file 13 are illustrative. They are used to show a method, never as measurements.

## The ten personas

### 1. Labour economist

- **Verdict:** Not by default, but more of the saving is being kept by workers than v1 assumed. Measured saving is still small, about 1.4% to 1.6% of US hours and about 3% in Denmark. Korea shows 3.8% less work time with output barely moving, which reads as slack kept on the job.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. Vendor surveys such as Zoom (76% save 30 minutes or more) have no control group.
  2. Exposure figures, including the ILO 25%, measure what could change, not what will.
  3. The Korea link between time and output is from one working paper.
- **Would watch:** Hours per worker in register data, and whether the Korea pattern repeats in Denmark style studies.
- **Advice:** Assume staff already use AI quietly. Agree openly how saved time is shared, or it stays hidden.
- **On ILO exposure:** 34% in rich countries against 11% in poor ones means the time effect, good or bad, lands in rich offices first.

### 2. Economic historian

- **Verdict:** Same as v1. No in the short run, yes over lifetimes. The forecast audit strengthens it. WEF expected machines to do 42% of task hours by 2022 and its own survey found about 34%. Frey and Osborne missed on scale.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. 1826 figures are estimates.
  2. No long run hours series exists for China, India or Japan before recent surveys.
  3. Big forecasts were never built to be scored, so the audit is partial.
- **Would watch:** Whether hours per worker fall faster than the roughly 7% US fall from 1971 to 2023.
- **Advice:** Discount any forecast without a date and a checkable unit. Plan for a decade.
- **On China:** Women in China still do about 3.5 hours of unpaid work a day against about 1.9 for men, after decades of fast growth. Growth alone did not close the home time gap.

### 3. AI technologist

- **Verdict:** Yes for some tasks, but v2 is less confident on net savings. Inside the frontier tasks ran 25.1% faster. Outside it, users were 19 points less likely to be correct, and DORA links more adoption to lower stability.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. METR used early 2025 tools on 16 developers and ages quickly.
  2. Studies still measure chat tools, not agents.
  3. Break-even inputs (s, R, p, F) are not measured by any one study.
- **Would watch:** Review time and rework per task, not just speed. Agent task length. Whether power limits slow model access.
- **Advice:** Use the break-even rule per task type. Deploy where review is cheap and errors are rare.

### 4. Union leader

- **Verdict:** Same as v1. Only if workers bargain for it. Hidden use changes tactics, not the conclusion. 48% would not tell a manager, so workers keep some time now, but that slack can be taken back once AI is formalised.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. Four-day trials were self-selected firms.
  2. Hidden use figures come from surveys, not logs.
  3. The fall in junior jobs may have other causes.
- **Would watch:** Agreements that trade AI gains for hours. Indian IT and BPO headcount.
- **Advice:** Write the hours share into contracts before tools roll out, and do not punish disclosed use.
- **On ILO exposure:** Women are more likely than men to sit in the top risk band in rich countries (9.6% vs 3.5% of jobs). Bargaining must cover clerical and admin roles first.
- **On disability CV bias:** Screening tools that save recruiter time can exclude disabled applicants. Unions should demand human review of AI hiring decisions.

### 5. Enterprise CFO

- **Verdict:** Changed. The firm does not get the time first by default. Much of the early saving is hidden and kept by staff, and review cost can cancel it. 77% of AI-using employees report more workload, with review the top cause (39%).
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. Vendor numbers dominate many sectors.
  2. Break-even cases are illustrative.
  3. The 57% hiding figure is now Verified in the full KPMG report. The 77% Upwork figure is still Plausible and rests on a small base (AI users among 625 employees). Only 39% name review as the cause, so "mostly from review" means the top cause, not a majority.
- **Would watch:** Cost per finished task, review hours, rework and change failure rate, revenue per employee.
- **Advice:** Budget review and rework, and make AI use visible without penalty so you can measure it. Power supply now belongs in AI capacity plans.
- **On skills hiring:** Skills-first claims rarely become real hires (fewer than 1 in 700). Do not count it as a saving until hiring data shows it.

### 6. Sociologist of time

- **Verdict:** Same as v1. Felt time will not improve on its own. Hidden use adds strain: people feel it is cheating (47%) or makes them look lazy (46%).
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. No study measures felt time and AI together.
  2. Harried leisure is an old theory with modest effects.
  3. Zoom's 80% who would take a real break is intent, not behaviour.
- **Would watch:** Message and meeting volume, time pressure surveys, and Japan style overwork cases.
- **Advice:** Cap messages and meetings, and make AI use normal to talk about.
- **On China:** Women's paid day of about 5 h 55 min plus about 3.5 hours unpaid leaves little room. Office AI does not touch the second shift.

### 7. Global South policymaker (India and Kenya)

- **Verdict:** Same as v1, now with firmer data. Not from office AI. ILO puts exposure at 11% of jobs in low income countries. India's unpaid gap barely moved from 2019 to 2024.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. India and China survey figures are Plausible, the primary pages failed to open.
  2. Almost every AI time study is from rich countries.
  3. M-Pesa poverty finding is contested.
- **Would watch:** Water, power and fuel access, local language voice AI, Indian IT and BPO headcount.
- **Advice:** Your offshore partners carry the first hit. Indian IT shed about 7,400 staff net in FY26. Share gains with them.
- **On India:** Women 289 minutes of unpaid domestic work a day against 88 for men in 2024, against 299 and 97 in 2019. Five years of digital growth left the gap about where it was.
- **On ILO exposure:** Low exposure protects jobs in the short run but also means low gains. Rented compute sends the margin abroad.

### 8. Feminist care economist

- **Verdict:** Same as v1. Not for most women.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. The 39% household automation figure rests on a small expert survey.
  2. The PwC household estimate was sponsored by Meta.
  3. No direct Folbre text on AI was found.
- **Would watch:** Unpaid hours by gender in national surveys, including China and India rounds.
- **Advice:** Count care loads when planning AI gains. Use saved time for flexible hours.
- **On China and India:** China women about 3.5 hours vs men about 1.9. India 289 vs 88 minutes. Care resists automation, so the gap is unlikely to close through AI.
- **On ILO exposure:** Women are more exposed in paid work and less helped in unpaid work. Both effects run against them.

### 9. Disability advocate

- **Verdict:** Changed. Large possible gains per person, but the only hard number is on harm. v1 led with gain. v2 leads with risk, because hiring is where AI time saving first meets disabled people.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. No number on AI time saved for disabled people.
  2. The CV study is one model, one team, 60 trials.
  3. Cognitive offloading studies are small.
- **Evidence:** In the UW study (Glazko et al., FAccT 2024), GPT-4 ranked the disability CV first in only about a quarter of 60 trials. Custom instructions against ableism raised it to 37 of 60, but depression and autism CVs still did badly. Verified (UW News opened).
- **Would watch:** Bias audits of AI screening, accessibility features, task time for disabled users.
- **Advice:** Test every AI hiring tool for disability bias before use, and keep a human review step. Then test productivity tools with disabled staff first.
- **On skills hiring:** Skills-first hiring could help disabled applicants, but real change reached fewer than 1 in 700 hires.

### 10. Ecological economist

- **Verdict:** Stronger than v1. Energy is now a limit on how fast time saving can spread, not just a cost. Data centres go from about 415 TWh to about 945 TWh by 2030. Large sites in Virginia can wait up to seven years for power. The US queue is nearly 2,600 GW.
- **Weakest evidence first:**
  1. No study links energy to a saved hour.
  2. IEA pages returned 403, so its figures are Plausible.
  3. The 20% project delay share is secondary only.
- **Would watch:** Grid queue times, energy per finished task, heat losses in outdoor work.
- **Advice:** Report energy next to every hour AI claims to save, and plan for capacity rationing in power tight regions.
- **On ILO exposure:** Exposure is highest where grids are already tight. Power, not jobs, may set the pace in rich countries.

## Synthesis

### Where they agree

1. Measured AI time saving is small, about 1.4% to 3.8% of hours across the US, Denmark and Korea studies, far below exposure figures.
2. Saved time does not become free time by default. In v2 it splits: some becomes output, some is kept as hidden slack, and some is eaten by review.
3. Fixed rules on hours are still the only lever with real evidence.
4. Self-report overstates. METR, Zoom and the 66% who do not check output all point the same way.
5. Averages hide who gets the time. China and India data now show the unpaid gap holding.
6. Big forecasts have run ahead of reality (WEF 2018 missed, McKinsey 2017 behind midpoint).

### Where they split

| Question | Side A | Side B |
|---|---|---|
| How fast will savings grow | AI technologist: fast inside the frontier | Historian, labour economist, ecological economist: slow, and now power limited |
| Who captures the gain first | CFO in v2: workers keep hidden slack at first | Union leader: firms will reclaim it once AI is formalised |
| Where the biggest gain is | Technologist and disability advocate: per task and per person | Global South and care economist: water, fuel and care, outside AI |
| Is the net positive | Most: yes over a lifetime | Ecological economist and sociologist: not once energy, heat and felt time count |
| Does AI help disabled people | Disability advocate: possibly most per person | Same advocate and union leader: first contact is biased screening |

### Energy-limited scenario note

If compute grows only as fast as new grid connections, with large sites waiting four to seven years, AI capacity is rationed. Use spreads first to high value tasks. Prices fall more slowly. Adoption curves stretch rather than stop. In this scenario every horizon from 5 years on shifts later by several years in power tight regions such as Virginia and Ireland, and the time-saved story arrives unevenly by country. Efficiency gains in chips and smaller models become the main source of new capacity. This is an Estimate from file 13, not a forecast with a date.

### Bottom line

AI saves time before it frees time. In v2 the saving splits three ways: output for the firm, hidden slack for the worker, and review work that eats both. Home time and felt time come last, and power may set the pace. Leaders who want staff to gain time must make AI use visible, budget review, fix hours in writing, and audit hiring tools for bias.
