# Culture force on time use: what people say they want from a saved hour

Research notes compiled 1 November 2026. Scope: stated preference for shorter weeks, what people say they would do with an extra hour or day, busyness as status, screens inside free time, the education leisure gap, and felt time pressure, today and since 2005. Countries: US, UK, EU (France, Germany, Nordics), Japan, Korea, China.

Status labels used on every number:
- Verified: I opened the source and read the number there.
- Plausible: the number comes from a secondary source only (news report, aggregator, search summary). I could not open the primary.
- Not found: I looked and could not find a usable number.
- Projection or Scenario: a future number. Never a fact.

Reading guide for the page builder. The headline pattern is simple. People say they want fewer paid hours and more free time, and the share saying so is rising slowly. When they get the time in a trial, they spend it on children, hobbies, sleep and exercise, and most refuse to give it back. But in daily life the free hour that already exists goes mostly to screens, free time has barely grown since 2005, and only about one in five would take a pay cut for the shorter week. So culture pushes paid hours down and free hours up, but weakly in the short run, with strength building over decades as cohorts replace each other.

## Key question 1. Stated preference for shorter hours or a four day week, by country and year

### Takeaway
Large majorities in the US, UK, Europe and Korea say they want a four day or 4.5 day week at the same pay, and the EU share who want fewer hours rose from 27% to 33% between 2015 and 2024. Support collapses when pay is at stake: only about one in five Americans would take a pay cut for it.

### Cited Findings

Sixth EWCS, Eurofound press release 18 September 2017. Verified. ### Inferences
- The EU shift from 27% to 33% wanting fewer hours is about two thirds of a point a year. If that pace held, roughly 36 to 37% of EU workers would want fewer hours by 2030 and about 40% by 2035. Projection, my arithmetic on the two Eurofound points.
- The US age gradient (82% of under 30s positive versus 69% of over 60s) means the stated preference should keep rising by cohort replacement even if no individual changes their mind.
- The gap between "want it at same pay" (67 to 78%) and "would take a pay cut" (about 20%) is the single best measure of how much of this is culture and how much is an income constraint. Culture is the 20%.
- Korea is the surprising case. Support is highest among those working normal hours and lowest among those working the longest hours, which fits the busyness as status story below: the people most embedded in long hours defend them.

## Key question 2. What people say they would do with an extra hour or day

### Takeaway
In the UK and Portugal trials the extra day went to children, hobbies, sleep, exercise and family, time satisfaction jumped more than two points on a ten point scale, and 90 to 93% wanted to keep the shorter week. In the UK 15% said no amount of money would make them go back to five days.

### Cited Findings
- UK, June to December 2022 pilot (61 companies, about 2,900 employees, report February 2023). Weekly work time fell from 38 to 34 hours on average. 71% of employees reported a decline in hours and 15% no change. Autonomy report. Verified. Source: [Autonomy, The results are in (PDF)](https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/The-results-are-in-The-UKs-four-day-week-pilot.pdf)
- UK, 2022 pilot. What employees said they wanted more time for, before versus after: own hobbies 87% to 54%, caring for or educating children 59% to 32%, volunteering 61% to 52%, caring for elderly or disabled 45% to 23%, cooking 30% to 15%, housework 26% to 12%. The unmet wish fell most for hobbies and children. Verified. Source: same Autonomy PDF, figure on page 40
- UK, 2022 pilot. Men's time looking after children rose 27% versus 13% for women. 68% of both men and women reported no change in the housework split. 52% reported more leisure travel. 21% reported lower childcare costs. Verified. Source: same Autonomy PDF, pages 41 to 43
- UK, 2022 pilot. 40% reported fewer sleep difficulties, 45% no change, 15% more. 46% were less fatigued. 39% were less stressed and 48% unchanged. 71% reported lower burnout and 22% higher. 54% found it easier to balance work with household jobs, 60% easier to combine paid work and care, 62% easier to combine with social life. Verified. Source: same Autonomy PDF, pages 36 to 40
- UK, 2022 pilot. Life satisfaction rose from 6.69 to 7.56 out of 10. Satisfaction with the amount of time to do the things they like rose from 5.28 to 7.51, with 73% reporting an increase. Verified. Source: same Autonomy PDF, page 41
- UK, 2022 pilot. 90% definitely wanted to continue and 0.43% (five people) somewhat did not. 96% preferred four days. Of those, 46% would return to five days for a pay rise of 0 to 25% (6% for under 10%, 40% for 10 to 25%), 29% would need 26 to 50%, 8% would need more than 50%, and 15% said no amount of money would induce them to accept five days. Verified. Source: same Autonomy PDF, pages 46 to 47
- UK, 2022 pilot. Sick and personal days fell from 2 to 0.7 per employee per month (65% reduction). 56 of 61 companies (92%) continued, 18 permanently. Revenue rose 1.4% over the trial, and 35% against comparable periods a year earlier. Verified at the campaign page and in the PDF. Sources: [4 Day Week Campaign UK](https://www.4dayweek.co.uk/pilot-results), Autonomy PDF
- UK, February 2024 follow up. 54 of 61 companies still on four days a year later, 31 permanently. Plausible (NYSSCPA news summary, Autonomy follow up not opened). Source: [NYSSCPA](https://nysscpa.org/news/1046103-most-companies-participating-in-four-day-work-week-pilot-have-stuck-with-that-schedule-2024-02-22)
- Germany, February to July 2024 (45 organisations, about 900 employees, Intraprenör with University of Münster). 73% of firms continued (34% extended pilots, 39% kept a reduced week), 20% returned to five days and 40 hours. 83% of employees wanted to keep the shorter week. Over 90% reported higher wellbeing. Wearables showed about 13 fewer "stress minutes" a day, more steps, more exercise and more sleep. Employees reported "more time for self-care, hobbies and social contact." Revenue and profit stayed stable. Plausible (The Local report of the Münster findings, primary report not opened). Sources: [The Local Germany](https://www.thelocal.de/20241106/what-are-the-results-of-germanys-four-day-work-week-trial), [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-10-21/four-day-workweek-experiment-in-germany-has-surprising-results)
- Portugal, June 2023 start (41 companies, more than 1,000 workers, government backed, coordinated by Pedro Gomes of Birkbeck and Rita Fontinha of Henley). Hours fell from 41.6 to 36.5 a week on the Henley page (about 12%), and 13.7% on the 4 Day Week Global page. Workers struggling to balance work and personal life fell from 50% to 16% (Henley, final) or 46% to 8% (campaign, interim). 65% spent more time with family. Work exhaustion fell 19%. 93% of workers want to continue. Workers valued the four day week at 28% of salary. Only 4 of 41 companies returned to five days. 95% of companies rated the trial positively. Verified at Henley and at 4dayweek.com. Sources: [Henley Business School, 18 July 2024](https://www.henley.ac.uk/news/2024/results-from-portugals-government-backed-4-day-week-trial-released), [4 Day Week Global Portugal](https://4dayweek.com/portugal-2024-pilot-results)
- US, working parents, 2026. Among full time working parents, 60% say they spend too little time with their children. 74% of full time working mothers say they do not have enough time for hobbies or interests. Majorities say they lack time for hobbies, socialising, exercising or relaxing. Pew survey of 2,242 working parents. Plausible (New York Times report reprinted by GV Wire, Pew report not opened). Source: [GV Wire, 16 June 2026](https://gvwire.com/2026/06/16/working-parents-say-theyre-in-a-losing-battle-over-their-time-pew-survey-shows/)
- US, 2008. "Having enough time to do the things you want" was the top life priority in a Pew list, chosen by about two thirds (68%). Plausible (snippet of a Pew short read). Source: [Pew 2008](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2008/07/09/free-time-middle-americas-top-priority/markdown)

### Inferences
- The saved day in trials is spent on unpaid care, rest and hobbies, not on paid side work. The UK data show the wish for hobby time fell 33 points and the wish for child time fell 27 points, while housework barely moved. The revealed use of the hour matches the stated wish.
- The money question is the most useful cultural number. 15% refusing any price and 37% demanding more than 25% is a strong revealed valuation of time after people have tasted it, and the Portuguese 28% of salary valuation lines up with it. Before the experience, only 20% would take any cut. Experience moves the valuation.
- Trial participants are self selected firms in knowledge and service work. The Gallup finding that real four day workers show slightly higher burnout is the counter signal. It may reflect compressed ten hour days rather than reduced hours.

- No survey asking the general public "what would you do with one extra hour a day" with a percentage breakdown. The nearest is the Autonomy wish list and the Portugal family figure. Not found.
- Pew 2023 or 2024 on how Americans spend free time: Not found. The Pew leisure topic page exists but no such report surfaced.
- Japan four day week trial results through 4 Day Week Global: Not found.

## Key question 3. Busyness as status after 2005

### Takeaway
UK time diaries show the Veblen pattern reversed between 1961 and 2001, so that high human capital now goes with longer paid hours, and US experiments in 2017 show that saying you are busy raises your perceived status, but only where people believe in social mobility, and the effect reverses in Italy. No newer multi country survey of whether busyness is admired exists.

### Cited Findings
- UK, 1961, 1983/4 and 2001 time diaries. "Work, not leisure, is now the signifier of dominant social status." The negative Veblen relationship between status (human capital) and work time reversed over the period, so high human capital is now associated with longer hours of work. Professional, scientific and engineering work grew from 17% of all paid work time in 1961 to 39% in 2001, and manual work fell from 53% to 30%. Gershuny, ISER Working Paper 2005-9, May 2005. Verified. Source: [ISER, Gershuny 2005 (PDF)](https://www.iser.essex.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/working-papers/iser/2005-09.pdf)
- UK, 2005. Gershuny's non technical summary: UK data "do not however show substantial evidence of increasing leisure density", so the growth in feeling busy is explained partly by a new busy group and partly because "the assertion of busyness now reflects an aspiration to high social status." Verified. Source: same ISER PDF
- US, 2017. Bellezza, Paharia and Keinan, Journal of Consumer Research 44(1), pages 118 to 138. Pilot: of 1,100 coded celebrity humblebrag tweets, about 12% were complaints about hard work and lack of time, the largest category after celebrity status. Study 1A: 307 US adults on Mechanical Turk read Facebook posts of a busy or a leisurely friend and rated the busy one higher in status. Verified. Source: [Columbia Business School copy of the JCR paper (PDF)](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/19293/Conspicuous%20Consumption%20of%20Time%20JCR.pdf)
- US versus Italy, 2017. The busyness status effect is moderated by perceived social mobility. In the paper's pilot, 30 Italians scored 3.98 on perceived social mobility against 4.91 for 30 Americans. Italians rated the leisurely lifestyle as higher status, the reverse of Americans. Verified in the paper text and in the Columbia press release. Sources: same JCR PDF, [ScienceDaily release 22 March 2017](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170322122631.htm)
- US, 2017. Time saving brands signal status: Peapod (grocery delivery) signalled status as much as Whole Foods because of its link to a busy life. Verified in the ScienceDaily release. Source: [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170322122631.htm)
- US, June 2019. 62% of Americans describe their weekly schedule as busy and 14% as extremely busy. Ipsos US Omnibus. Verified. Source: [Ipsos](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/node/510191)
- Korea, November 2025. Support for a 4.5 day week falls with hours worked (83% at normal hours, 71% above 70 hours), consistent with long hour workers defending the norm. Verified. Source: [Asia Economy](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2025120314352136457)

### Inferences
- The Gershuny reversal took about forty years (1961 to 2001) to show up in UK diaries. If a flip back toward leisure as status is under way it would be expected to show up on a similar timescale, which is why I rate the culture force stronger at 25 and 50 years than at 1 to 5.
- Bellezza's mechanism gives a testable lever. Where belief in social mobility falls, busyness loses its signal value. Falling mobility beliefs in the US and UK since 2008 would, on this logic, weaken busyness as a badge. No survey has tested that yet.
- Trial quotes in the Autonomy report ("We want to be more busy, less stressed") show the norm surviving inside the four day week: people compress rather than relax.

### Gaps
- No 2020 to 2026 survey in any country asking whether saying you are busy is admired, with a percentage. Not found. The Howden 2026 whitepaper link turned out to be a 2017 article about the Bellezza paper.
- A 2015 "28 country" survey on conflicted attitudes to busyness appeared in a search summary but I could not locate or open it. Not found.
- No Japan, Korea or China academic replication of the busyness status experiment found. Not found.

### Takeaway
US adults had 5.2 hours of leisure a day in 2025, the same as the 5.1 hours in 2005, and screens (2.6 hours of TV plus 37 minutes of games and computer use) take about 62% of it while socialising fell from about 45 to 35 minutes. Global social media time has plateaued at about 2 hours 21 minutes, and 53% of Americans say they spend too much time on their phone.

Playing games or using a computer for leisure took 37 minutes, up from 25 minutes in 2015. Socialising and communicating took 35 minutes, down from 41 minutes in 2015, and participation fell from 38% to 30%. Men 5.6 hours, women 4.8. Employed adults with no children 4.5 hours, employed adults with a child under 6, 3.2 hours. BLS American Time Use Survey. Verified. ### Inferences
- Share of US leisure on screens in 2025: TV 2.6 hours plus 0.62 hours games and computer equals 3.2 hours of 5.2, about 62%. My arithmetic on BLS numbers. Phone browsing coded as other activities is not in this figure, so it understates screens.
- 2005 to 2025 in the US: total leisure flat (5.1 to 5.2 hours), TV flat at 2.6, games and computers roughly doubled, socialising down about ten minutes. The saved hour of the last two decades did not appear as more free time at all. It appeared as a shift inside free time from people to screens.
- The UK online figure (4.5 hours on personal devices) and the China figure (5 hours 37 minutes) both exceed the whole US leisure budget, which means much screen time happens during work, commuting, eating and care, not only in leisure.
- Regret is now measurable. About half of Americans say too much phone time and about half tried to cut back. Social media time has stopped growing since 2022 in GWI data. The culture is turning against the screen hour even as it keeps spending it.

## Key question 5. The education leisure gap, updated

### Takeaway
Aguiar and Hurst found leisure rose 8 hours a week for adults without a high school diploma and fell 6 hours for college graduates between 1965 and 2005, and in 2005 the least educated had 1.9 more leisure hours a day than graduates. Newer ATUS work to 2022 shows the less educated gained the extra leisure mostly at home, alone and on screens, so the gap in hours widened while the gap in leisure quality reversed.

### Cited Findings
- US, 1965 to 2005. Average leisure rose about 4 hours a week. Adults with less than high school gained about 8 hours a week. College educated adults lost about 6 hours a week. Aguiar and Hurst, The Increase in Leisure Inequality 1965 to 2005, AEI 2009. Verified at the author's page. Source: [Mark Aguiar publication page](https://www.markaguiar.com/citation/leisure-inequality)
- US, 1965 to 2003. Most of the overall leisure gain came before 1985. After 1985 less educated men gained leisure while more educated men lost it. Changes in employment status explain less than half of the growing gap. Aguiar and Hurst, NBER WP 13837 (2008). Plausible (abstract via RePEc). Source: [NBER WP 13837](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13837.pdf)
- US, 2005. Among adults 25 and over, those with less than a high school diploma spent 1.9 more hours a day in leisure and sports than those with a bachelor's degree or higher. Full time workers earning 450 dollars or less a week had 50 more minutes of weekday leisure than those earning more than 1,076 dollars. BLS ATUS 2005 release. Verified. Source: [BLS ATUS 2005 release (PDF)](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/atus_07272006.pdf)
- US, 1965 to 2003. Less educated adults gained leisure hours but lost leisure quality (more fragmented, less time with other adults). Sevilla, Gimenez-Nadal and Gershuny, Demography 2012. Plausible (abstract). Source: [PMC copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6526239)
- US, 2003 to 2022. In 2003 adults with high school or less spent a slightly smaller share of time alone than college graduates (42.9% versus 43.9%). By 2019 the high school or less group was 4.7 percentage points higher. Less educated and non White individuals increasingly spend at home leisure alone, mainly on television and to a lesser extent video games, and a smaller share of their leisure happens outside the home or with others. Respondents who spend a large share of free time alone report lower wellbeing. Atalay, Journal of Population Economics 2024. Verified. Source: [Atalay 2024 (PDF)](https://enghinatalay.github.io/time_use.pdf)
- US, 2004 to 2017. Men aged 21 to 30 shifted leisure toward gaming and recreational computing and cut market work more than older men or women. Innovations in gaming since 2004 explain about half of the increase in their leisure and imply a 1.5 to 3.0% fall in their market hours. Aguiar, Bils, Charles and Hurst, Journal of Political Economy 2021. Plausible (abstract via RePEc). Source: [JPE listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:doi:10.1086/711916)
- US, 2025. Employed adults with a child under 6 had 3.2 hours of leisure against 4.5 for employed adults with no children. Ages 35 to 44 had 3.8 hours in 2024 against 7.6 for the 75 plus. Verified. Source: [BLS ATUS 2025 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm)

### Inferences
- The culture of the saved hour is split by class. The educated say they want more time and have less of it. The less educated have more time and spend a rising share of it alone on screens. A page tile should not treat "free hours" as one good.
- Since the ATUS shows no growth in average leisure 2005 to 2025, any widening of the education gap in this period is a redistribution, not new time. The Atalay alone time data imply the gap in hours persisted and the gap in sociable leisure widened.

### Gaps
- No published analysis of ATUS leisure hours by education for 2003 to 2024 as a time series. BLS publishes an annual table by educational attainment (Table 11 in the 2005 release) but I did not open the 2024 or 2025 version, so the current size of the 1.9 hour gap is Not found here.
- No UK, EU, Japan, Korea or China equivalent of the education leisure gap found with numbers. Not found.
- Boppart and Ngai's work on long run hours and leisure was not opened. Not found.

## Key question 6. Felt time pressure since 2005

### Takeaway
About half of Americans have said they lack enough time in every Gallup reading from 1990 to 2015 (48% in 2015, average 47% for 2001 to 2015), while 61% of workers and 61% of parents say so. Gallup's global daily stress reading rose from 31% in 2009 to a 44% peak in 2021 and settled at 40% in 2024 and 2025, with the US and Canada highest at 50%.

### Cited Findings
- US, December 2003. 52% have enough time to do what they want and 48% do not. Gallup. Verified. Source: [Gallup 2004](https://news.gallup.com/poll/11656/time-rr.aspx)
- US, 1990 to 2015. "Slightly more than half" lacked time in September 1990 and March 1995. From 2001 to 2015 the share lacking time averaged 47% and ranged 41% to 49%. In December 2015, 48% lacked time. 61% of working Americans versus 32% of non workers, 61% of parents of under 18s versus 42% of others, 61% of women under 50 versus 48% of men under 50, and 21% of those 65 plus. Gallup, 2 to 6 December 2015. Verified. Source: [Gallup, Americans' perceived time crunch no worse than in past](https://news.gallup.com/poll/187982/americans-perceived-time-crunch-no-worse-past.aspx)
- Global, 2009 to 2025. Share of employees who experienced stress a lot of the previous day: 2009 31%, 2010 32%, 2011 34%, 2012 36%, 2013 37%, 2014 33%, 2015 39%, 2016 38%, 2017 39%, 2018 37%, 2019 38%, 2020 43%, 2021 44%, 2022 44%, 2023 41%, 2024 40%, 2025 40%. Regions in the 2026 report: US and Canada 50%, East Asia 46%, Europe 39%. Gallup State of the Global Workplace 2026. Verified. Source: [Gallup State of the Global Workplace global data](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/697904/state-of-the-global-workplace-global-data.aspx)
- EU, 2015 fieldwork. 81% say hours fit well or very well with private life. Verified. Source: [Eurofound 2017 release](https://eurofound.mynewsdesk.com/news/four-out-of-five-workers-in-europe-happy-with-working-time-fit-260053)
- EU, 2024. Eurofound reports the work intensity index "has deteriorated for women but improved for men", no numbers in the release. Verified (release text via mirror). Source: [Eurofound release via pubt.io](https://www.pubt.io/view/6EF11E004726B817717EED5DEA69A7989BEACB5D)
- UK, 2022 pilot. Satisfaction with the amount of time to do the things they like rose from 5.28 to 7.51 out of 10 after six months on four days. Verified. Source: [Autonomy PDF](https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/The-results-are-in-The-UKs-four-day-week-pilot.pdf)
- US, 2026. 74% of full time working mothers lack time for hobbies or interests. 60% of full time working parents spend too little time with children. Pew, 2,242 working parents. Plausible (NYT report). Source: [GV Wire, 16 June 2026](https://gvwire.com/2026/06/16/working-parents-say-theyre-in-a-losing-battle-over-their-time-pew-survey-shows/)
- China, 2023. In a China Newsweek poll of 58,000, nearly 89% said they were too sleepy to work on make up weekends. Verified at Sixth Tone. Source: [Sixth Tone](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1012786)

### Inferences
- Felt time pressure in the US is remarkably flat at about half the population for 25 years while actual leisure is also flat at about 5.1 hours. The Gershuny paradox holds: feeling busy is not tracking hours.
- Global daily stress is about 9 points higher in 2025 than in 2009 and has not returned to pre pandemic levels. This is the cultural backdrop that makes the four day week attractive and it is strongest in the US and East Asia, the two regions with the highest stated support.

- Eurofound EQLS or EWCS time pressure trend by country 2005 to 2024 with numbers: Not found in anything I could open.
- Japan and Korea national time pressure trend items: Not found.

## Key question 7. Net direction and strength of the culture force

### Takeaway
Culture pushes paid hours down and free hours up at every horizon, but weakly in the next five years because the preference is conditional on pay and the busyness norm is intact, and medium to strong from ten years out as cohorts replace each other and the taste for time hardens after people experience it.

### Reasoning by horizon
- 1 year. Paid hours down, strength 1. Free hours up, strength 1. Only 33% of EU workers want fewer hours and 56% want the same (2024, Verified). About 20% of Americans would take a pay cut (Plausible). Nothing in one year changes that. US leisure moved 5.07 to 5.2 hours between 2024 and 2025, within noise.
- 3 years. Paid down, strength 1. Free up, strength 1. Trials keep 73 to 92% of firms on shorter weeks (Germany 2024, UK 2023, Verified and Plausible) but Eurofound calls working time reduction "experimental" at company level (2024, Verified). Korea's 78% support (2025, Verified) and the Chinese paid leave push are talk ahead of change. Screens still take about 62% of US leisure.
- 5 years. Paid down, strength 2. Free up, strength 1. Projection: EU share wanting fewer hours reaches about 36 to 37% by 2030 on the 2015 to 2024 pace. The gap between wanting fewer hours and accepting less pay still caps the push at medium. Free hours stay screen bound: Pew shows 45% trying to cut phone time with only 25% very successful (2026, Verified).
- 10 years. Paid down, strength 2. Free up, strength 2. Cohort arithmetic: 82% of US 18 to 29 year olds versus 69% of 60 plus see the four day week as positive (2023, Verified), so the median voter and median worker both move. Projection: about 40% of EU workers wanting fewer hours by 2035.
- 15 years. Paid down, strength 2. Free up, strength 2. Trial participants value the shorter week at 28% of pay (Portugal 2024, Verified) and 15% at any price (UK 2023, Verified). As the share of the workforce with that experience grows, the stated preference turns into bargaining pressure. Still medium because the busyness norm and income needs persist.
- 25 years. Paid down, strength 2. Free up, strength 2. Gershuny's reversal of the leisure status gradient took 40 years (1961 to 2001, Verified). A reversal back toward leisure as status is plausible on the Bellezza mobility mechanism but unproven. Scenario, not fact.
- 50 years. Paid down, strength 3. Free up, strength 3. Scenario: if busyness loses its signal value as mobility beliefs fall, culture becomes the dominant push for shorter weeks, as it already is in Italy where leisure still signals status (2017, Verified). Confidence is low and the number behind it is the forty year Gershuny lag, not a forecast.

### Gaps
- There is no quantitative model linking stated preference shares to realised hours. The strengths above are judgement calls anchored on the cited numbers.
