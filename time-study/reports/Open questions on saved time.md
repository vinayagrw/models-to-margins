# Open questions on saved time

Report for the Time Spent vs Time Saved study, 2 November 2026. The owner asked which five questions researchers are still trying to answer about time saved. Every number sits in research/33-open-questions.md with its tag and link, so sources appear by name and date only.

The five share one gap: each has a good number for today, and none has a study that follows the saved hour any further.

## 1. How much saved time refills?

Known. Humlum and Vestergaard (revised March 2026) asked about 25,000 Danish workers what they do with time a chatbot saves: 85 percent said other job tasks, under 10 percent said breaks. Those are shares of users, with no minute count. A Korean survey (Suh and Oh, February 2026) found a 3.8 percent cut in working time taken mostly as slack on the job. Only road building has a settled number, an elasticity of about 1.0 (UC Davis, January 2025).

Not known. The share of saved minutes that refills, counted by diary or telemetry, in any field but roads. Whether Denmark or Korea is right.

Who is on it. The Danish and Korean teams. Berkeley Haas (one tech firm, no numbers yet). Caltrans, November 2026.

What the study should say. Most of a saving comes back as work unless someone decides otherwise first, and the Danish shares are self report.

## 2. Where does a saved hour go?

Known. The only diary evidence is Jiang, Park, Xiao and Zhang (draft December 2025): on US diaries to 2023, the most AI exposed workers gained about 3 work hours a week after ChatGPT and lost about 3 leisure hours, a correlation of Plausible size. Only the commute has been followed home: Pabilonia and Vernon (December 2025) find a home working day frees 52 minutes, which men spend on leisure and sleep and women on chores and childcare.

Not known. Any study following the same workers' saved AI hours into free time or back into work. Any forecast of free time use in 2030 or later.

Who is on it. The two diary teams. Humlum and Vestergaard remain the only ones who ask the question directly.

What the study should say. Workers say the hour goes back to work, the one diary study agrees, and nobody has followed it home.

## 3. Do self reported savings show up?

Known. Self reported savings sit near 2 percent of all US work hours, flat for four quarters (St. Louis Fed, October 2026). METR's 2025 trial had developers feeling 20 percent faster while the clock showed them slower, and its February 2026 follow up found them faster only within intervals that include zero. A Fed survey of nearly 750 finance chiefs (March 2026) found executives reporting about 1.8 percent AI productivity growth for 2025 when their own answers imply about 0.6.

Not known. Any study logging the same workers' time with and without AI and then asking what they saved. Any official attribution of 2025 or 2026 productivity growth to AI.

Who is on it. The St. Louis Fed tracker. METR, whose redesigned trial has not reported. The Fed CFO survey.

What the study should say. Treat a self reported saving as two to three times the measured one until logged data exists.

## 4. Who keeps the saving?

Known. So far the firm, on its own earnings calls (C.H. Robinson, January 2026). Danish AI users saw no change in pay or hours above 2 percent two years after ChatGPT (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2026). The only dated projection, Korinek and co-authors (September 2026), runs the labour share from 60 percent (national accounts base) to between 59.4 and 45.2 percent in 2030. Of 47 US union contracts with AI clauses, about 23 percent share savings and none shortens hours (AI Exposure, March 2026).

Not known. The customer's share as prices. Any signed contract that turns an AI saving into shorter hours. Any labour share projection past 2030.

Who is on it. Korinek and co-authors to 2030. The BLS, whose third quarter labour share is due 5 November 2026. The union tracker.

What the study should say. Workers have not received the saving as pay or time, and nobody has shown customers receiving it as price. Name both series before putting the page's 52.8 percent (BLS nonfarm business) beside Korinek's 60.

## 5. Why did US hours stop falling?

Known. US leisure rose 4.5 hours a week from 1965 to 2013, over 8 hours for the least schooled and under 1 for graduates. The split opened in the 1980s when pay spread out (Boppart and Ngai, 2021). The St. Louis Fed (revised September 2026) traces the post 2000 fall in US hours per person mostly to health benefits for people not in work. The World Inequality Lab (May 2025) finds global hours per worker fell 34 percent from 1800 to 2025, mostly before 1980, and says its 2100 scenarios are not predictions.

Not known. How much of the 1980 to 2019 stall each cause carries. Any official US hours figure for 2050 or 2075.

Who is on it. The St. Louis Fed team and the World Inequality Lab. A Metaculus crowd (April 2026) expects US weekly hours to fall from 38 to 34 by 2035.

What the study should say. Americans took productivity as pay. For 2050 and 2076 show a band, never a point.
